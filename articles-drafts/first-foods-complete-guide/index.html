First foods for babies: a complete starting-solids guide

3-6 months · 6-9 months · Feeding · Reviewed 9 June 2026 · All articles

Introducing solid foods is one of the most exciting and, let's be honest, slightly nerve-wracking milestones in your baby's first year. There are decisions to make about timing, texture, what to offer first, how much to give, and which foods to keep out of reach entirely. The good news: the underlying guidance from major health authorities is clear and consistent, and you don't need to get everything perfect on day one. This guide draws directly on recommendations from the CDC, WHO, and NHS to give you a thorough, practical foundation.

One thing to keep in mind throughout: all of the timing and safety guidance here applies to healthy, term-born babies. If your baby was born prematurely or has a medical condition that affects feeding, always get personalised advice from your GP, paediatrician, or health visitor before starting solids.

Signs your baby is ready

Age alone doesn't tell the whole story. Both the NHS and CDC describe a cluster of developmental milestones that indicate a baby is ready to handle solid food. These signs typically appear together, generally around 6 months, and all three matter:

  • Head and trunk control. Your baby can sit upright, either independently or with light support, and hold their head steady. This is essential for safe swallowing. A baby who can't maintain this position is at greater risk of choking.
  • Eye-hand-mouth coordination. They can look at an object, pick it up or reach for it, and bring it to their mouth. This shows the motor coordination needed for self-feeding.
  • Willingness to swallow. When something is placed on the tongue, they attempt to move it to the back of the mouth and swallow rather than automatically pushing it back out. Younger babies have a tongue-thrust reflex that does the opposite; its fading is a genuine readiness marker.

The NHS is also clear about what does not reliably signal readiness: chewing fists, waking more at night, and demanding extra milk feeds are normal infant behaviours that don't tell you the gut and motor system are ready for solids. Acting on them too early can lead to starting before the baby is developmentally prepared.

When to start: what the evidence says

The short answer from all three authorities is: around 6 months, and not before 4 months.

The WHO and UNICEF recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life. "Exclusive" means breast milk only, with no other food or drink, including water. At around 6 months, a baby's energy and nutrient needs start to exceed what breast milk alone can provide, and this is the right point to introduce complementary foods while continuing to breastfeed. The WHO recommends continuing breastfeeding alongside solid foods up to 2 years of age or beyond.

The CDC states that solids should not begin before 4 months. Starting too early is linked to difficulties for the baby's developing digestive system and may increase the risk of obesity and other health issues later. The NHS echoes this, recommending that solids begin at around 6 months and that parents consult a health visitor if they are considering starting before this point.

Formula-fed babies follow the same general timeline. Infant formula continues to be the primary source of nutrition in the first year for babies who are not breastfed, with solids gradually supplementing it from around 6 months.

If your baby was born prematurely, timing may be calculated differently, often from corrected age rather than birth age. Speak to your healthcare team for guidance specific to your baby.

First foods and textures

One of the most reassuring pieces of guidance from the CDC is that there is no required order for introducing foods. You don't have to start with a single vegetable and work through a strict sequence. The goal in the early weeks is to introduce a variety of single-ingredient foods, one at a time, so that if a reaction occurs you can identify the culprit.

Textures at the start

Begin with smooth, thoroughly pureed or mashed textures that require no chewing whatsoever. The CDC describes this as mashed, pureed, or strained foods. As your baby gains experience, you can progress fairly quickly toward lumpier textures and soft finger foods. The NHS advises moving toward mashed, lumpy, or finger foods as soon as they can manage them, rather than staying on smooth purees for longer than necessary. Extended time on smooth purees alone can make it harder for babies to accept varied textures later.

Good first foods

Both the NHS and CDC support starting with vegetables and fruits, either cooked and mashed or offered as soft finger food pieces. The NHS specifically suggests including non-sweet vegetables early, such as broccoli, cauliflower, and spinach, alongside naturally sweeter options like sweet potato, carrot, and pear, as broad early exposure may reduce fussiness later. Soft cooked options like parsnip, potato, yam, apple, and pear are all appropriate.

Protein-rich foods, including well-cooked and mashed meat, poultry, fish, and legumes, are also suitable from around 6 months according to the CDC. The CDC recommends oat, barley, or multigrain infant cereals over rice cereal alone, which has historically been the default first food in many cultures but provides less nutritional variety.

Indian-context meal ideas

Many traditional South Asian first foods fit naturally within this guidance. Well-cooked moong dal thinned to a smooth consistency, soft khichdi made from rice and lentils mashed together, ragi (finger millet) porridge thinned with breast milk or formula, and mashed seasonal fruits like banana, chikoo (sapodilla), or ripe papaya are all gentle, nutrient-rich starting points. These are cultural meal ideas rather than specific medical recommendations: anchor timing and preparation to the official guidance and your health visitor's advice.

Introducing common allergens

Current guidance from both the CDC and NHS supports introducing potentially allergenic foods, including eggs, peanut products, tree nut butters, fish, shellfish, and foods containing wheat, from around 6 months, alongside other foods. You should introduce these one at a time in small amounts and watch for any reaction before introducing the next one. The previous practice of delaying allergenic foods has not been shown to reduce allergy risk, and early introduction may in fact be beneficial. If your baby has severe eczema or an existing food allergy, speak to your doctor before introducing high-risk allergens.

How much to give: responsive feeding

In the first weeks of solid food introduction, quantity is far less important than the experience of eating itself. The NHS recommends starting with just a few pieces or teaspoons of food. Breast milk or formula remains the main source of nutrition throughout most of the first year, with solids gradually increasing in quantity and variety.

The WHO provides frequency guidance that is a useful framework for progression:

  • 6 to 8 months: 2 to 3 meals per day.
  • 9 to 23 months: 3 to 4 meals per day, with 1 to 2 additional snacks as needed.

Responsive feeding, letting your baby signal hunger and fullness, is central to the NHS approach. The signs that a baby wants more include leaning forward, opening their mouth, and reaching for food. The signs they are done include closing their mouth, turning their head away, or showing disinterest. Never try to push a baby to finish a portion. Forcing or pressuring a baby to eat overrides their natural hunger and fullness cues and can undermine healthy eating habits over time.

The NHS also notes that a new food may need to be offered 10 times or more before a baby accepts it. An initial rejection is not a signal to remove that food permanently. Keep offering it calmly and without pressure alongside foods you know they enjoy.

Milk feeds alongside solids

The NHS advises offering small amounts of food before a milk feed, so the baby isn't too full to explore the food but isn't hungry enough to be frustrated by a small portion. Continue offering breast milk or formula as usual. As solid intake grows naturally over the coming months, milk feeds will gradually reduce in frequency on the baby's own timeline.

Foods to avoid and choking safety

This section covers the full list of foods the NHS and CDC advise avoiding, along with the reasons, grouped by risk type.

Salt

Babies should not eat foods with added salt from birth. The NHS states that salt is not good for a baby's kidneys. Avoid adding any salt to food prepared for your baby, and keep salty processed foods such as bacon, sausages, crisps, crackers, ready meals, takeaways, and foods made with stock cubes or gravy off the menu.

Sugar

Avoid adding sugar or sweet syrups to baby food or drinks. The NHS notes that sugar contributes to tooth decay and that babies have no nutritional need for added sugar. Avoid sugary snacks, fruit juices, and flavoured fruit drinks.

Honey

Do not give honey to any child under 12 months. The NHS explains that honey can occasionally contain bacteria that produce toxins in a baby's intestines, causing infant botulism, a very serious illness. This applies to all honey, including "raw" or "natural" varieties.

Whole cow's milk as a main drink

The CDC is clear that whole cow's milk should not be given as a main drink before 12 months of age. Small amounts of cow's milk used in cooking, or unsweetened full-fat yogurt and hard pasteurised cheese offered as foods, are acceptable from around 6 months.

Certain cheeses

The NHS advises avoiding mould-ripened soft cheeses such as brie and camembert, ripened goat's cheese, soft blue-veined cheeses, and any unpasteurised cheese under 12 months due to the risk of listeria. Pasteurised hard cheeses are safe from 6 months.

Raw and lightly cooked eggs

From 6 months, the NHS states that babies can have eggs provided they carry the British Lion stamp (or equivalent quality mark guaranteeing vaccination against salmonella), and these can be served runny. Eggs without such a stamp, and duck, goose, or quail eggs regardless of stamp, should be cooked until both the white and yolk are completely solid.

Certain fish

The NHS advises that shark, swordfish, and marlin should not be given to children under 16 years because their high mercury content can affect the development of the nervous system. Other oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, and sardines are suitable and nutritious.

Raw shellfish

Raw or lightly cooked shellfish, including mussels, clams, and oysters, should not be given to babies or young children due to the increased risk of food poisoning. Thoroughly cooked shellfish is a separate matter; consult your health visitor.

Whole nuts and large nut pieces

Whole nuts and large pieces of nut should not be given to children under 5 years because of the choking risk. Finely ground nuts, smooth nut butters (without added salt or sugar), and nut butters spread thinly on soft toast are safe ways to introduce nuts from 6 months.

Rice drinks

The NHS advises against giving rice drinks as an alternative to formula or milk for children under 5 years, as they may contain levels of arsenic that are inappropriate for young children.

Choking hazards: preparation rules

The CDC and NHS both emphasise careful food preparation to minimise choking risk. Always supervise your baby while they eat. Key preparation rules include:

  • Cut round foods such as grapes, cherry tomatoes, and berries into quarters or smaller pieces rather than halves, as a half can still form a seal in the airway.
  • Cut cylindrical foods such as sausages lengthways into strips rather than round slices.
  • Cook vegetables until they can be easily squashed between two fingers before offering them.
  • Remove all bones from fish and meat before serving.
  • Remove pits and stones from fruit.
  • Avoid raw jelly cubes, which are a choking hazard, and prepare jellies only according to manufacturer instructions.
  • Cool hot foods to a safe temperature before offering them.

Gagging and choking are different things. Gagging, which involves coughing, retching, and a reddened face, is a normal protective reflex as babies learn to manage food in their mouths. Choking, which is silent, involves an inability to cough or cry and requires immediate first aid. It is worthwhile for all parents to complete an infant first aid course before starting solids.

Practical feeding tips

Beyond the clinical guidance, a few practical habits make the starting-solids experience more positive for both of you:

  • Eat together when you can. The NHS notes that babies learn from watching others eat. Family mealtimes, where the baby sits at the table and sees caregivers eating a variety of foods, can positively influence their willingness to try new things.
  • Allow mess. Babies learn about food through touch, smell, and play before they ever swallow much of it. Embrace the mess; it's developmentally appropriate.
  • Keep distractions low. The NHS advises minimising screens and distractions at mealtimes so the baby can focus on the eating experience and their own hunger and fullness signals.
  • Don't season for adults. Prepare the baby's portion before adding salt, stock, or spices intended for the rest of the family's meal. Mild spices commonly used in South Asian cooking, such as a small amount of turmeric or cumin, are not listed as hazardous by the NHS, but this is general guidance rather than a specific endorsement. If in doubt, speak to your health visitor.
  • Water from a cup. Small sips of cooled boiled water can be offered from around 6 months in an open cup or free-flow sippy cup. The NHS advises avoiding juice or sweetened drinks.

Frequently asked questions

My baby is 4 months old and seems interested in food. Should I start?

Interest in food at 4 months is common and normal, but it doesn't mean the digestive system and motor skills are ready. Both the CDC and NHS state that solids should not begin before 4 months, and the clear recommendation is to wait until around 6 months. If you feel strongly that your baby is ready before 6 months, discuss this with your health visitor first. Do not start before 4 months under any circumstances.

Does it matter which food I introduce first?

No. The CDC is explicit that there is no required order. You can start with vegetables, fruit, baby cereal, or protein-rich foods in any order that suits you. What does matter is introducing one new single-ingredient food at a time and waiting 3 to 5 days before introducing another, so that any allergic reaction can be traced back to the right food.

When do I need to introduce allergens, and how?

The NHS and CDC both recommend introducing common allergens, including peanut products, tree nut butters, eggs, fish, and wheat-containing foods, from around 6 months, one at a time in small amounts. Introduce a potential allergen in the morning or early afternoon so you can observe your baby for a reaction during the day. Signs of an allergic reaction include hives, swelling around the mouth or throat, vomiting, or difficulty breathing. Seek emergency help immediately if you suspect anaphylaxis. If your baby has severe eczema or an existing food allergy, consult your doctor before introducing high-risk allergens.

My baby keeps gagging. Is this normal?

Gagging is a normal and protective reflex as babies learn to manage food. It involves coughing, retching, and often a reddened face. It looks alarming but usually resolves on its own in a few seconds. Choking is different: a choking baby will be silent, unable to cough or cry, and may go pale or blue. All caregivers should learn infant first aid, including how to respond to choking, before starting solids. If you are unsure whether your baby's gagging is within normal range, speak to your health visitor.

Can I do baby-led weaning instead of purees?

Baby-led weaning (offering soft finger foods from the start rather than spoon-fed purees) is a recognised approach that is consistent with NHS guidance encouraging early introduction of soft finger foods and self-feeding. The NHS says babies can start with soft finger foods if they have the necessary head control and coordination. A combined approach, offering both soft mashed foods and suitable finger foods, is also widely used. Whichever approach you choose, the safety rules around textures, supervision, and food preparation remain the same. The term "baby-led weaning" and its specific protocol are not directly addressed in the WHO or CDC sources used for this article, so discuss the approach with your health visitor.

I'm still breastfeeding. Do I need to give my baby iron-rich foods?

The WHO notes that as complementary foods are introduced from around 6 months, meeting the baby's growing iron and zinc needs becomes important. The CDC recommends including iron-rich foods, such as fortified infant cereals and pureed meat, among first foods. Breast milk contains some iron, but in smaller amounts than a growing 6-month-old needs from diet alone. Talk to your health visitor or GP if you have concerns about iron intake; they can advise on whether supplementation is appropriate in your situation.
