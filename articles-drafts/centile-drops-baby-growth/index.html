What a centile drop means for your baby's growth

0-12 months · Growth · Reviewed 12 June 2026 · All articles

Seeing your baby's dot move down the growth chart at a check-up can bring on a wave of worry. In most cases, a small shift is a normal part of how babies grow, and it does not mean anything is wrong. Understanding what the chart actually shows, and knowing the one key threshold the NHS uses to decide when to act, can help you tell the difference between routine variation and something worth looking into.

What centile lines actually show

A centile chart is a reference tool built from measurements taken across a large population of babies. The lines represent the spread of weights and lengths at each age. If your baby sits on the 25th centile for weight, it means that 25 out of every 100 babies of the same age weigh less than your baby and 75 weigh more. Being on a lower centile does not mean your baby is underweight or unhealthy. It simply shows where they sit in the population at that moment.

The NHS uses separate growth charts for boys and girls, because boys and girls have different typical growth patterns. Your baby's personal child health record, often called the red book, contains the correct chart for your child. Measurements are plotted over time to reveal a pattern, and that pattern matters far more than any single reading.

How much movement is normal

Babies do not grow in a perfectly smooth curve. They have periods of faster growth and quieter patches, and their weight can vary slightly from one weigh-in to the next depending on time of day, clothing, and recent feeds. Because of this natural variation, the NHS states that a baby's measurements may go up or down by one centile line and this is considered a normal fluctuation.

The threshold that prompts a closer look is crossing two or more centile lines. If your baby's weight or length drops across two centile lines, the NHS recommends speaking to a health visitor. This is not the same as a cause for alarm. It is a prompt for a conversation and, if needed, a more detailed assessment.

Weight and length do not have to match

It is common for a baby to sit on slightly different centiles for weight and length. The NHS notes that these are usually fairly similar to each other, but it is not unusual for them to differ. A large baby who is also long may sit on the 75th centile for weight and the 70th for length, which is entirely consistent. What health visitors look for is not a precise match between the two, but a general pattern that makes sense for that individual child.

Normal weight loss after birth

One early centile movement that surprises many parents is the weight dip in the days immediately following birth. The NHS is clear that it is normal for babies to lose some weight in the first few days after birth. This happens as babies shed extra fluid and as feeding becomes established. Most babies regain their birth weight by around 3 weeks of age.

If your baby has not regained their birth weight by 3 weeks, the NHS advises contacting a health visitor. This is the kind of early signal worth acting on promptly, not because something is certain to be wrong, but because it is easier to investigate and support early feeding if needed.

Illness and temporary dips

A common reason for a short-term centile drop is illness. When babies are unwell, they often feed less, sleep more, and temporarily slow their growth. The NHS notes that weight gain may slow during illness but that babies usually return to their normal growth pattern within 2 to 3 weeks of recovering. If your baby has recently been unwell and you notice a dip at the next weigh-in, mention it to your health visitor for context. They will take the illness into account when interpreting the chart.

How often to weigh your baby

There is a practical reason why the NHS sets recommended limits on how often babies should be weighed. Weighing too frequently can make normal short-term fluctuations look alarming. The guidance says:

  • From birth to 6 months: weigh no more than once a month after the first 2 weeks
  • From 6 to 12 months: weigh no more than once every 2 months
  • After 1 year: weigh no more than once every 3 months

These intervals give a clearer picture of true growth trends. A weight taken every few days will show noise. A weight taken monthly will show signal. If you find yourself wanting to weigh your baby more frequently out of anxiety, that is worth discussing with your health visitor directly, because the worry itself is something they can help with.

When to contact a health visitor

Based on NHS guidance, the situations that warrant a call to your health visitor are:

  • Your baby loses a large amount of weight, or has not regained birth weight by 3 weeks
  • Your baby's measurements cross two or more centile lines, either downward or upward
  • You have a persistent feeling that something is not right with your baby's feeding or development

Your health visitor is there precisely for these conversations. A single appointment is often enough to provide reassurance, adjust feeding support, or decide whether a GP referral is appropriate. Acting on a concern early is always better than waiting.

Frequently asked questions

Is a small centile drop always a problem?

Not necessarily. NHS guidance states that a baby's measurements may move up or down by one centile line, and this is within normal variation. It is less common to cross two centile lines, and if that happens you should speak to your health visitor.

How often should I weigh my baby?

The NHS recommends no more than once a month up to 6 months, no more than once every 2 months between 6 and 12 months, and no more than once every 3 months after 1 year. Weighing too frequently can turn normal short-term variation into unnecessary worry.

My baby lost weight right after birth. Is this normal?

Yes. The NHS states it is normal for babies to lose some weight in the first few days after birth. Most babies regain their birth weight by around 3 weeks. If yours has not reached birth weight by 3 weeks, contact your health visitor.

My baby was ill and their weight dipped. What should I expect?

The NHS notes that weight gain can slow during illness, and babies usually return to their normal growth pattern within 2 to 3 weeks of recovering. Mention the illness at your next health visitor appointment so they can interpret the chart with full context.

Why do boys and girls use different charts?

Boys and girls have different typical growth patterns. Using the wrong chart would make a healthy measurement look out of range. Your baby's red book contains the correct chart for your child's sex.
