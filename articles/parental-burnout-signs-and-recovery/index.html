Parental burnout: signs you are running on empty and how to recover

Mental health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that goes beyond tired. It is the feeling of going through the motions with your child, of having nothing left to give and feeling like a failure for it, of wishing you could just stop for a while. If that sounds familiar, you might be experiencing parental burnout, and understanding what it is can be the first step toward feeling better.

What parental burnout is

Parental burnout is a specific state of chronic exhaustion that researchers have been studying seriously since the 2010s. It has three defining features. The first is overwhelming exhaustion in the parenting role, a bone-deep tiredness that is not just about sleep. The second is emotional distancing from your children, going through the motions of care without feeling emotionally present or connected. The third is a loss of a sense of effectiveness as a parent, feeling like you are doing everything wrong even when you are doing your best.

It is not the same as a hard week or a rough patch. It is a sustained state that tends to develop gradually, as demands outpace resources over a long period of time without relief.

Signs that might indicate parental burnout

Parental burnout can look different for different people. Some signs that are commonly reported include:

  • Feeling completely depleted by parenting, even when you have had some rest
  • Dreading interactions with your child or feeling relieved when they are asleep or away
  • Going through caregiving routines on autopilot, without emotional warmth or engagement
  • Feeling like a completely different parent to the one you thought you would or wanted to be
  • Fantasising about escaping, being alone, or not being a parent
  • Feeling ashamed of how you feel, which increases the isolation
  • Irritability, short fuse, or disproportionate responses to ordinary challenges

These feelings can be distressing to admit to, especially when parenting is supposed to be the most rewarding thing you have ever done. But acknowledging them honestly is not a failure. It is the beginning of getting things back into balance.

What causes parental burnout

Research by Isabelle Roskam and Moira Mikolajczak, who have studied parental burnout extensively, finds that it comes down to an imbalance between the demands of parenting and the resources available to meet those demands. Resources include practical support, partner involvement, financial stability, and your own sense of competence. Demands include the number and complexity of children's needs, the standards you hold yourself to, and the isolation of modern parenting.

Perfectionism is a significant risk factor. Parents who set very high standards for themselves, who struggle to accept help, or who feel that anything less than fully present, enrichment-packed parenting is inadequate, are particularly vulnerable. The gap between the ideal and the reality can be exhausting to live in.

Isolation makes it worse. Without enough adults to share the load with, the relentlessness of parenting becomes genuinely unsustainable for most people.

How parental burnout differs from postnatal depression

The two can overlap, and if you are not sure which you are experiencing, a GP is the right person to help you think it through. But there are some differences worth understanding.

Postnatal depression tends to involve a more pervasive low mood and hopelessness that affects most areas of life, including things that have nothing to do with parenting. Parental burnout is more specifically tied to the parenting role. You might feel perfectly fine when you have a few hours away from parenting and quite depleted again when back in it. Depression tends not to respond to those temporary breaks in quite the same way.

That said, sustained burnout can develop into depression, which is another reason not to wait and see for too long.

What helps: reducing the gap between demands and resources

Recovery from parental burnout is not primarily about rest, though rest matters. It is about meaningfully changing the balance between what is being asked of you and what you have to meet those demands. That might look like:

  • Lowering the standards you hold yourself to in the parenting role, including letting go of some things that do not really matter
  • Asking for and accepting help from a partner, family, friends, or other support
  • Reclaiming any regular time that is yours alone, not productivity time but genuine restoration time
  • Having an honest conversation with a partner about the distribution of care responsibilities
  • Finding other parents who can relate, because isolation is both a cause and a symptom of burnout

These changes can feel impossible to implement when you are deep in burnout. That is often the point at which professional support becomes most valuable.

When to seek professional support

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, running away, or hurting your child, please reach out to your GP today. Those thoughts are a signal that you need more support than you are currently receiving, and they do not make you a bad parent. They make you someone in crisis who deserves help.

More broadly, if burnout is affecting your ability to care for your child or yourself, a GP, health visitor, or therapist can help. CBT can be effective for the perfectionism and self-critical thinking patterns that often underlie burnout. Your GP can refer you, or you can self-refer to NHS talking therapies in many areas.

A word about the standards we set ourselves

Parental burnout does not happen to indifferent parents. It happens to people who care deeply and who have been trying harder than is sustainable for longer than is fair. If you are burned out, it is not because you are lazy or lacking. It is because you have been asking too much of yourself for too long, often in conditions that were not designed to support you adequately.

Recovery is possible and it is worth pursuing, not just for you but for the relationship you want with your children. A parent who is present, even imperfectly, is far more valuable to a child than one who is perfect on paper but running on empty.

Frequently asked questions

What is parental burnout?

Parental burnout is a state of chronic exhaustion specifically linked to the parenting role. It involves three key features: deep physical and emotional exhaustion from parenting, emotional distance from your children (feeling like you are going through the motions), and a sense of ineffectiveness as a parent. It is distinct from general fatigue and from postnatal depression.

Is parental burnout the same as postnatal depression?

They are different, though they can overlap. Postnatal depression involves persistent low mood and often affects all areas of life. Parental burnout is more specifically tied to the parenting role and is characterised by exhaustion, detachment, and a loss of satisfaction in parenting, rather than the pervasive hopelessness that can characterise depression. Both deserve support.

Who is most at risk of parental burnout?

Research points to perfectionism, high parenting standards, lack of support, social isolation, and significant imbalance between the demands of parenting and the resources available to cope. It is more common in high-achievement cultures that celebrate intensive parenting.

Can parental burnout affect my relationship with my child?

Yes, and it is one of the reasons getting support matters. Burnout can make it harder to be emotionally present and responsive. Recovering from burnout, which is absolutely possible, typically restores that connection. The very fact that you are worried about it shows how much the relationship matters to you.

How long does recovery from parental burnout take?

There is no standard timeline. Recovery usually requires meaningful change in the demands and resources balance, not just rest. That might mean asking for more help, reducing pressure on yourself, or making structural changes to how care is shared. Therapy can accelerate the process significantly.

When should I seek professional help for parental burnout?

If you are having thoughts of escaping, harming yourself, or of running away from your family, please speak to your GP promptly. More broadly, if burnout is affecting your ability to function or your relationship with your children, professional support through your GP, a therapist, or a health visitor is a sensible and courageous step.
