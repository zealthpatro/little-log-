Colic: what it is, what helps, and when it ends

Newborn · Care · Reviewed 20 June 2026 · All articles

Colic is one of the most distressing experiences in early parenthood. Your baby is fed, changed, held, and seemingly comfortable, yet the crying starts anyway and nothing you do seems to stop it. Understanding what colic actually is, what the evidence says about the remedies that genuinely help, and knowing that it has an end point makes it more manageable. This article covers the definition, causes, evidence-based soothing strategies, and crucially, how to take care of yourself while you go through it.

What colic is and how it is defined

The clinical definition most commonly used is the Wessel criteria, established by paediatrician Morris Wessel in 1954 and still used today. Colic is defined as crying or fussing for more than three hours per day, on more than three days per week, for more than three weeks, in an otherwise healthy and well-fed infant. This is sometimes called the "rule of threes."

The key phrase in that definition is "otherwise healthy and well-fed." Colic is a diagnosis of exclusion: it is what you are left with after medical causes such as a cow's milk protein allergy, reflux, a urinary tract infection, hernia, or another condition have been ruled out. This is why it is always worth having your GP or health visitor assess a baby who is crying excessively, even if colic seems the most likely explanation.

In practice, many parents and clinicians use colic as a shorthand for any intense, inconsolable, apparently inexplicable crying in a young baby. The crying typically follows a recognisable pattern: it tends to start in the late afternoon or evening, it peaks around 6 to 8 weeks of age, and it resolves in the majority of babies by 3 to 4 months. The baby is not in danger. They are not being harmed. But the sound and the helplessness of not being able to stop it is genuinely difficult to live through.

Colic affects roughly 10 to 40 percent of infants depending on the study and the criteria used. It occurs equally in breastfed and formula-fed babies, in boys and girls, and in first and subsequent children. There is no association with feeding method, socioeconomic status, or birth order.

What causes colic: what we know and don't know

Despite decades of research, the cause of colic is not well understood. Several theories have been investigated, none of which fully explains all cases.

The gut microbiome theory has attracted significant attention. Studies have found that colicky babies have a different gut bacterial composition compared to non-colicky babies, with less Lactobacillus and more gas-producing bacteria. This has led to interest in probiotic interventions, which are discussed in the next section. Whether this microbiome difference causes colic or is a consequence of the same conditions that cause colic is still unclear.

The gut motility theory suggests that colic may involve irregular or immature peristalsis: the muscular contractions that move food through the digestive tract. Incomplete digestion and gas accumulation could cause cramping-like pain. This theory is consistent with the "trapped wind" experience many parents observe in their baby during a colic episode: a hard distended tummy, pulling up of the legs, and passing gas when the episode ends.

Other proposed contributors include an immature nervous system that makes the baby unable to self-regulate arousal, overstimulation during the day that leads to a period of release in the evening, a sensitivity to certain proteins in cow's milk (in formula or passed through breast milk), and maternal smoking during pregnancy (which is associated with a modest increased risk).

None of these theories is proven as the universal cause, which is part of why treating colic is so difficult. What works for one baby may be targeting one of these mechanisms without addressing another.

What actually helps: evidence and practicalities

The honest answer is that no single intervention reliably eliminates colic in all babies. However, a number of approaches have reasonable evidence behind them and are safe to try.

Skin-to-skin contact and carrying are among the most consistently supported approaches. Close physical contact regulates the baby's nervous system, reduces cortisol, and provides the kind of sensory input that calms arousal. Many parents find that a baby who screams in a cot settles within minutes of being carried in a sling or held against the skin. This does not "spoil" babies and is not creating a bad habit: it is meeting a genuine neurological need.

Rhythmic movement is similarly well-supported. Rocking, bouncing, walking, car rides, pram walks: the vestibular stimulation from repetitive motion has a calming effect on the immature nervous system. White noise and shushing sounds work through a similar mechanism, mimicking the constant sound environment of the womb. Running a vacuum cleaner, a white noise machine, or a fan near (not directly at) the baby can reduce crying in some infants during a colic episode.

Probiotics, specifically Lactobacillus reuteri, have been tested in several randomised controlled trials. A 2018 Cochrane review found that L. reuteri reduced crying time in exclusively breastfed babies with colic, but the evidence in formula-fed babies was weaker and less consistent. If you are breastfeeding and want to try a probiotic, Lactobacillus reuteri drops (available over the counter) are safe and have the strongest evidence base of any probiotic intervention for colic.

Simethicone (sold as infant gas drops or colic drops in many countries) is widely used. It works by breaking up gas bubbles in the gut so they can be passed more easily. Despite its popularity, clinical trials have generally not found simethicone to be more effective than placebo for reducing colic crying. The NHS does not recommend simethicone as a treatment for colic. That said, it is considered safe and some families report it helps their baby, which may reflect individual variation in the cause of their baby's symptoms. It is reasonable to try it, but it should not be the first or only approach.

Dietary elimination is worth considering if other approaches are not helping. In babies with a cow's milk protein sensitivity, removing cow's milk protein from the breastfeeding parent's diet can reduce colic symptoms significantly. This takes one to two weeks to show effect. If you are formula-feeding, your GP can advise on whether a hypoallergenic formula trial is appropriate. Do not eliminate major food groups from your own diet for extended periods without dietitian guidance.

Approaches that are not supported by evidence include gripe water, herbal teas (which can contain unsafe ingredients for infants), sugar solutions, and changes in feeding technique unless there is a specific identified problem such as a poor latch or incorrect bottle use.

What does not help and what to avoid

It is worth being clear about what has not been shown to work, partly because parents under severe sleep deprivation and stress are vulnerable to claims made by products and practitioners who overstate their evidence.

Gripe water has been used for generations but contains no ingredient with clinical evidence for colic. Herbal preparations marketed for colic have variable ingredients and dosing and have not been tested in large trials. Some contain star anise, which can cause neurological problems in infants: any herbal preparation for a baby should be checked with a pharmacist or doctor before use.

Chiropractic manipulation is sometimes marketed for colic. There is no good evidence that it works, and there are rare reports of serious adverse events in infants. The NHS does not recommend it for colic.

Switching between multiple formula brands rapidly, eliminating multiple foods from your diet at once, and adding rice cereal to bottles are all interventions that carry risks or downsides without clear evidence of benefit for colic specifically.

When colic ends: the timeline

This is the most important thing to know. Colic ends. In the majority of babies, it improves significantly between 3 and 4 months of age, and most cases have fully resolved by 5 months. The peak is typically around 6 to 8 weeks, which means that if you are in the thick of it in the first two months, you are likely at or near the hardest point.

The NHS states clearly that colic usually gets better by the time a baby is 4 months old. This timeline is consistent across studies conducted in different countries and across different definitions of colic. It is not dependent on which intervention you try or do not try: the spontaneous resolution rate is very high.

Knowing this does not make the current moment easier to live through, but it provides a genuine endpoint. The weeks ahead will be hard, and then they will be over. Your baby will not remember this, and it will not affect their development, attachment, or relationship with you.

The impact on parents and how to cope

Colic has a measurable impact on parental mental health. Studies have found higher rates of postnatal depression and anxiety in parents of colicky babies. The relentless nature of the crying, the sense of helplessness, the sleep deprivation, and the social isolation of the early newborn period combine to make colic genuinely dangerous for parental wellbeing, not just uncomfortable.

Several things matter here. The first is to take the impact seriously. It is not melodramatic to find this hard: it is objectively hard, and research confirms that. If you are experiencing persistent low mood, inability to function, or thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, please contact your GP, midwife, or health visitor urgently. Postnatal depression is treatable, and getting help early is far better than waiting.

The second is the safe put-down principle. If you have been trying to soothe a crying baby for a long time and you are reaching your limit, it is always safe to place the baby in their cot or another safe position and step away for a few minutes. Shaking a baby is never safe and can cause serious brain injury. Placing them safely and walking away until you feel calmer is the right thing to do, not a failure.

Tag-teaming helps enormously if you have a partner or support person available. Agree in advance on time limits: 20 minutes on, 20 minutes off. Neither of you will be able to sustain hours of soothing an inconsolable baby without a break. If you are parenting alone, identify one person (a parent, a friend, a neighbour) who can come and hold the baby for an hour while you rest, shower, or simply sit in silence.

Support lines are available in most regions. In the UK, the Cry-sis helpline (0800 448 0737) is staffed by parents who have been through colic and is available for the evenings when it tends to be worst. The NCT helpline (0300 330 0700) covers feeding and early parenting support. Similar services exist in other countries.

When to see a doctor

Colic is a diagnosis of exclusion: medical causes must be ruled out first. See your GP or health visitor promptly if: the crying is accompanied by fever, if your baby is not gaining weight well, if there is blood in the nappy or vomit, if the baby seems unusually lethargic or unresponsive between crying episodes, if the baby vomits forcefully after feeds, if there is a visible swelling in the groin or abdomen, or if you have any other concern that something is wrong beyond typical colic.

Even if there is no specific medical concern, it is reasonable to visit your GP simply to confirm that colic is the most likely explanation and to get support for your own wellbeing. Health visitors in particular are trained to support families through colic and can provide practical strategies as well as monitoring for postnatal depression in the primary caregiver.

Frequently asked questions

How is colic defined exactly?

The most widely used clinical definition is the Wessel criteria: crying or fussing for more than three hours per day, on more than three days per week, for more than three weeks, in an otherwise healthy and well-fed infant. This is sometimes called the "rule of threes." In practice, most clinicians use colic as a descriptive label for intense, inconsolable crying in an infant where no medical cause can be found.

Does anything actually help with colic?

No single remedy reliably works for all babies, but several approaches have the most evidence. Skin-to-skin contact, rhythmic movement (rocking, bouncing, car rides), white noise, and gentle tummy pressure can calm some babies during a colic episode. Probiotics, specifically Lactobacillus reuteri, have shown modest benefit in breastfed babies in some trials. Simethicone (infant gas drops) has not shown consistent benefit in controlled trials but is considered safe to try. Eliminating cow's milk protein from the diet may help in a subset of babies where an underlying sensitivity is present.

When does colic usually end?

For the majority of babies, colic improves significantly between 3 and 4 months of age. Most cases have resolved entirely by 5 months. The peak of colic crying is typically around 6 to 8 weeks of age. This timeline is consistent across many studies and is one of the few things about colic that clinicians agree on.

How do I cope with colic as a parent?

Coping strategies matter enormously. Tag-team with a partner so neither person is the sole soother for extended periods. Accept that you cannot always stop the crying: your calm presence is valuable even when nothing is working. Use safe put-down breaks: placing the baby in a safe place and stepping away for a few minutes is far safer than caring for a baby while severely stressed. Reach out to your health visitor, GP, or a support line such as the National Childbirth Trust (NCT) helpline. Remind yourself and your partner that this is temporary and will end.
