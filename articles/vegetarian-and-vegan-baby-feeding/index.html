Vegetarian and vegan baby feeding: getting the nutrients right from 6 months

Feeding · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Millions of babies around the world are raised on vegetarian diets, and many on fully plant-based ones. Both approaches can support healthy growth and development when planned thoughtfully. The difference between vegetarian and vegan weaning is meaningful: a vegetarian diet that includes dairy and eggs has a broader nutritional safety net. A vegan diet requires more specific attention to a handful of nutrients that are either absent from or poorly absorbed from plant foods alone.

This article explains what you need to focus on, which foods provide it, and when it is worth involving a registered dietitian.

Vegetarian weaning: well-supported with planning

The NHS, British Dietetic Association, and American Academy of Pediatrics all state that a well-planned vegetarian diet can fully meet a baby's nutritional needs from 6 months. "Well-planned" is the operative phrase. A vegetarian baby who eats primarily beige carbohydrates and fruit, with no legumes, eggs, or dairy, will have nutritional gaps. A vegetarian baby who regularly eats eggs, full-fat dairy products, lentils, beans, fortified cereals, nuts, and a wide range of vegetables is in a strong position nutritionally.

The nutrients that require most attention in vegetarian weaning are iron, zinc, calcium, vitamin D, iodine, and omega-3 fatty acids. None of these are impossible to obtain from a well-designed vegetarian diet, but each requires some deliberate food choices.

Vegan weaning: possible but needs closer attention

A fully plant-based diet for a baby is possible but carries a higher risk of nutritional deficiency if not carefully planned. The NHS recommends that parents planning vegan weaning for their baby speak to a registered dietitian. Specific nutrient gaps in an unplanned vegan baby diet include vitamin B12 (not found in plant foods), vitamin D (limited food sources regardless of diet), iodine (largely absent from plant foods unless fortified), and long-chain omega-3 fatty acids DHA and EPA.

Supplementation is not optional for vegan babies: it is essential. Any breastfed baby of a vegan mother needs vitamin B12 from birth, because breast milk B12 content reflects the mother's status, and vegan diets without supplementation are often B12-deficient. Formula-fed vegan babies receive B12 from fortified formula, but require it from food and supplements once weaning begins in earnest.

Iron: the most critical nutrient at 6 months

Iron needs particular attention in all baby diets at 6 months, and even more so in vegetarian and vegan ones. A baby's iron stores from birth run low around 6 months, and the iron in breast milk is not sufficient to replenish them. Plant-based iron, known as non-haem iron, is less bioavailable than haem iron from meat and fish. This means that a vegetarian or vegan baby needs to eat more iron-rich plant food to absorb the same amount of iron.

The best plant-based iron sources for babies include:

  • Well-cooked lentils and split peas (red lentil dal with no added salt is excellent)
  • Cooked chickpeas and kidney beans (blended or mashed)
  • Tofu (soft or silken, well-mashed)
  • Iron-fortified baby rice cereal or oat porridge
  • Tahini (sesame paste, spread thinly on food)
  • Cooked dark leafy greens such as spinach and kale
  • Ground pumpkin seeds (mixed into food)
  • Mashed egg yolk (for vegetarian babies)

The key to maximising non-haem iron absorption is vitamin C. Serving an iron-rich food alongside a vitamin C source at the same meal roughly doubles the amount of iron absorbed. Practical pairings: lentil dal with a squeeze of orange juice, spinach with soft-cooked tomato, fortified cereal with a small amount of kiwi puree.

Conversely, certain foods inhibit iron absorption when eaten at the same time. Calcium-rich foods, tannins from tea, and phytates in wholegrains can all reduce non-haem iron uptake. This does not mean avoiding these foods; it means not serving large amounts of calcium-rich dairy immediately alongside the iron-rich component of the same meal. Practical spacing, rather than strict separation, is the goal.

Vitamin B12: essential for vegan babies

Vitamin B12 is found naturally only in animal products. Plant foods, including seaweed, do not provide reliable or bioavailable B12. B12 deficiency in infancy causes serious neurological damage and developmental delay, and the signs can be subtle until significant harm has occurred.

For vegan babies, B12 supplementation is non-negotiable. A breastfeeding vegan mother should supplement her own B12, as this improves her milk B12 content, but should not rely on this alone: the baby should also receive B12 directly. Fortified foods such as some plant milks, nutritional yeast, and fortified cereals provide some B12, but drops or a supplement are the most reliable source for infants.

Vegetarian babies who eat dairy and eggs receive B12 from these sources and generally do not need supplementation beyond standard vitamin D drops, but regular eggs and dairy in the diet are needed to maintain this.

Zinc: important but manageable

Zinc is involved in immune function, growth, and wound healing. It is found in meat and seafood in a highly bioavailable form, and in plant foods in a less bioavailable form. Good plant-based zinc sources include legumes, tofu, wholegrains, nuts, and seeds. As with iron, zinc absorption from plant foods is reduced by phytates. Soaking legumes before cooking and choosing leavened bread over unleavened products reduces phytate content and improves zinc absorption.

Calcium: dairy vs plant sources

Calcium is critical for bone development and nerve function throughout infancy and childhood. Vegetarian babies who eat dairy products have well-covered calcium needs. Vegan babies need calcium from plant sources, which requires more planning:

  • Fortified plant-based drinks such as unsweetened oat, soy, or pea milk are good calcium sources from 12 months onward (not as a main drink before 12 months)
  • Calcium-set tofu (look for calcium sulphate in the ingredients)
  • Cooked dark leafy greens: kale, pak choi, bok choy (not spinach, whose calcium is poorly absorbed due to oxalic acid)
  • Fortified cereals and bread
  • Ground almonds and almond butter (in small amounts from 6 months)
  • White beans and chickpeas

Omega-3 fatty acids: choose algae, not flaxseed

Long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, particularly DHA and EPA, are important for brain and eye development throughout the first two years. They are found primarily in oily fish. Plant foods such as flaxseed, chia, and walnuts contain a short-chain omega-3 called ALA, which the body can theoretically convert to DHA, but conversion is inefficient, especially in infants.

For babies on plant-based diets, an algae-based DHA supplement is the most reliable and appropriate source of long-chain omega-3. Algae is where fish get their DHA from in the first place, so an algae-based supplement delivers the same DHA without fish. Flaxseed and other ALA sources are not adequate substitutes for infants under 12 months.

Iodine: often overlooked

Iodine is needed for thyroid function and brain development. It is found primarily in dairy products, fish, and eggs. Plant foods contain very little iodine unless grown in iodine-rich soil or fortified. Vegan babies are at meaningful risk of iodine deficiency. Iodised salt is one source, but adding salt to baby food is not appropriate. Fortified plant milks and a specific iodine supplement for vegan infants are the most reliable options. A dietitian can advise on appropriate dosing.

Protein: the combination myth

One of the most persistent myths in vegetarian and vegan nutrition is that plant proteins must be combined at each meal to provide all essential amino acids. This is not correct. The body maintains an amino acid pool that draws on protein from all foods consumed across the day. As long as a variety of plant proteins, including legumes, wholegrains, nuts, and seeds, feature regularly in the diet, complete protein coverage is achieved without calculating combinations at each meal.

Practical protein sources for vegetarian and vegan babies include well-cooked mashed lentils and beans, tofu, full-fat yoghurt and cheese (for vegetarians), eggs (for vegetarians), ground nuts and nut butters spread thinly, sunflower seed butter, and fortified soy or pea-based foods.

When to involve a registered dietitian

A dietitian's input is strongly recommended, rather than just useful, for vegan baby weaning. A dietitian can assess your baby's overall diet, check for gaps, advise on specific supplements and doses, and review blood results if your GP requests them.

You should also seek a dietitian's guidance if your baby is not growing well, if your baby has a food allergy that further restricts their diet, or if you are planning a very restricted diet for any reason. In many regions, a referral from your GP or health visitor is all that is needed to access paediatric dietetic support on the NHS or through your health system.

Frequently asked questions

Can babies thrive on a vegetarian diet?

Yes. A well-planned vegetarian diet that includes dairy and eggs can fully meet a baby's nutritional needs from 6 months. The NHS supports vegetarian weaning with attention to iron, zinc, calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids. Regular eggs, full-fat dairy, legumes, and fortified foods provide most of what a vegetarian baby needs.

Is a vegan diet safe for babies?

A vegan diet for a baby requires more careful planning and supplementation than a vegetarian one. The NHS recommends that parents planning a vegan diet for their baby speak to a registered dietitian. Vitamin B12 supplementation is essential for breastfed babies of vegan mothers, and for any baby on a fully plant-based diet. Vitamin D and iodine supplementation are also needed. With appropriate planning, supplementation, and monitoring, vegan babies can grow and develop normally.

Do I need to combine proteins at every meal?

No. The theory that plant proteins must be combined at every meal to form a complete protein is outdated. The body draws on amino acids from food consumed across the day. As long as your baby eats a variety of plant proteins across their meals, including legumes, wholegrains, nuts, seeds, and soy products, their amino acid needs are met without calculating combinations at each sitting.

Which plant foods are best for iron?

The best plant-based iron sources for babies include well-cooked lentils and other legumes, tofu, iron-fortified infant cereals, tahini, well-cooked dark leafy greens such as spinach and kale, and ground pumpkin seeds. Non-haem iron from plant foods is absorbed less efficiently than haem iron from meat, but absorption increases significantly when vitamin C is consumed at the same meal. Try serving orange segments alongside lentil dal, or a squeeze of lemon juice on cooked greens.

What supplement does a breastfed vegan baby need?

A breastfed baby of a vegan mother needs vitamin B12 supplementation from birth, as breast milk from a vegan or vitamin B12-deficient mother contains very little. Vitamin D drops are recommended for all breastfed babies regardless of diet. The baby may also need iodine supplementation if the mother's diet is low in iodine. Any baby on a completely vegan diet from weaning should be reviewed by a dietitian to assess the full supplementation picture.

What is the best omega-3 source for a vegan baby?

For a baby on a plant-based diet, an algae-based DHA supplement is the most reliable source of long-chain omega-3 fatty acids. Flaxseed, chia seeds, and walnuts contain a short-chain omega-3 called ALA, but conversion to the DHA and EPA that the brain needs is inefficient, particularly in infants. Algae-based supplements are appropriate from 6 months and are the same original source of DHA that oily fish accumulate by eating algae.
