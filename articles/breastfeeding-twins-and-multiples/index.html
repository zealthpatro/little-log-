Breastfeeding twins and multiples: tandem feeding and getting enough milk

Breastfeeding · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Breastfeeding twins or higher-order multiples is one of the most intensive things a parent can take on, and it comes with a particular set of practical challenges. How much milk can your body make? How do you actually feed two babies at once? Who fed last, and on which side? This guide works through the key questions with realistic, practical answers.

How supply works with two babies

Milk production is governed by demand. The more milk that is removed from the breast, the stronger the signal to produce more. With two babies stimulating the breast, your body calibrates to meet double the demand. This is not a theoretical capacity: parents of twins regularly produce enough milk for both babies, often exceeding what a singleton parent produces.

The critical factor is that both babies need to be feeding frequently and effectively from the start. If one baby is in NICU or cannot feed at the breast, expressing at the same time as the other baby nurses, or pumping regularly in place of that baby's feeds, is essential to build a supply that will be big enough for both. A hospital-grade double pump is worth using in the early weeks, as it saves time and is more effective at building supply than a standard pump.

Supply tends to establish more firmly by around six to eight weeks, by which point most parents breastfeeding twins have a clearer picture of whether they can fully meet both babies' needs from the breast, need to supplement some feeds, or want to move to full combination feeding. All of these are valid outcomes.

Tandem feeding positions

Tandem feeding (feeding both babies at the same time) is not strictly necessary, but it saves a significant amount of time each day. With two babies feeding eight to twelve times each in 24 hours, feeding sequentially means a large portion of your day and night is spent nursing. Tandem feeding halves the time spent at each round.

The double football hold is the most widely used position for tandem feeding in the newborn period. Each baby is cradled like a rugby ball under each arm, facing inward, with their bodies running alongside yours behind you. Their legs extend behind your back rather than across your lap. A firm nursing pillow designed for twins (the EZ-2-Nurse Twins and My Brest Friend Twins Plus are widely recommended) makes this position far more manageable by bringing both babies up to nipple height without requiring you to hold their weight entirely with your arms.

The double cradle hold, where both babies lie across your lap facing you, one on each side, becomes easier as babies develop more head control around six to eight weeks. Some parents find a combination works well: one baby in a cradle hold and one in the football hold on the other side. As your babies grow and can hold their heads more independently, the feeding options become considerably more flexible.

Alternating sides versus dedicated sides

Alternating which baby feeds on which side at each session helps ensure that any difference in the two babies' sucking strength is evenly distributed across both breasts. If one twin is a stronger or more efficient feeder, always putting them on the same side will eventually lead to a size and supply difference between the two breasts. Rotating keeps things balanced.

Some parents of twins find it simpler to assign each baby a dedicated side, particularly if one baby has feeding difficulties and needs a consistent setup. The trade-off is that if the babies have different feeding styles or efficiencies, supply on each side may diverge over time.

Whatever system you choose, keeping a feeding log is genuinely useful with twins. It is easy to lose track of who fed last, for how long, and on which side when you are managing two babies, often on little sleep. A log takes the memory work out of it and lets you spot patterns (one twin always feeding more in the evenings, for example) that help you plan.

Keeping track of who fed when

Simple methods work well here. A hair elastic moved from wrist to wrist at each session is a classic trick for remembering which side to start on next. Sticky notes on the bedside table, a whiteboard in the feeding room, or a feeding log in Cubby can all serve the purpose. The important thing is having a system that takes zero mental effort at 3 am.

When feeding twins tandem, some parents log the session as starting with Baby A on the left and swapping the next session so Baby A is on the right. Others keep a simple count of total feeds per baby per 24 hours to check both are feeding enough. The nappy check matters just as much with twins as with a singleton: each baby should be producing at least six wet nappies in 24 hours by day four or five.

NICU situations with multiples

Twins and higher multiples are more likely to be born early, and it is common for one or both to spend time in NICU. This creates a complex feeding situation where you may be nursing one baby at home while expressing for another baby still in hospital, or where one baby has made the transition to direct feeding while the other is still on tube feeds.

Ask the NICU team for a clear plan for each baby and make sure feeding and expressing are coordinated. A hospital-grade pump used at home for the NICU baby while you nurse the baby at home, or pumping after each home feed, helps maintain supply for both. The transition out of NICU is a time to get extra lactation support, as you are essentially establishing breastfeeding from scratch for one baby while maintaining it for another.

Combination feeding and no-guilt realism

Combination feeding, where some feeds come from the breast and others from expressed milk or formula, is an extremely common approach with twins and multiples. It spreads the feeding load to a partner, family member, or other carer, it allows the breastfeeding parent to sleep longer stretches, and it removes some of the pressure to provide every feed from the breast.

There is no minimum threshold at which breastfeeding stops being valuable. If you can breastfeed for some feeds each day, your babies benefit from those feeds regardless of what else they receive. Breastfeeding does not have to be all or nothing to be worthwhile. Many parents of twins settle into a rhythm that works for their family, which might be full breastfeeding, it might be one formula feed a day, or it might be a more even split. There is no right answer beyond what sustains you and nourishes your babies.

Twin-specific support organisations

The Twins Trust in the UK runs a breastfeeding support line staffed by peer supporters who have breastfed twins themselves. TAMBA offers local groups and online support. La Leche League, the Association of Breastfeeding Mothers, and many IBCLCs have specific experience with multiples. Reaching out to a specialist rather than general breastfeeding support is worth it when you are managing two babies, because the logistics and challenges are genuinely different.

Frequently asked questions

Can I produce enough milk for twins?

Yes. Milk production is driven by demand: the more milk is removed, the more your body makes. With two babies stimulating the breast, supply naturally calibrates to meet double the demand. Most parents of twins who breastfeed from the start find their bodies adapt to produce enough for both. The key is frequent feeding or pumping from the beginning and ensuring both babies are latching well.

What is the best breastfeeding position for twins?

The double football hold is the most popular tandem position for newborn twins. Each baby is held like a rugby ball tucked under each arm, facing inward with their legs behind you. A firm nursing pillow (such as a twin-specific pillow like the My Brest Friend Twins or EZ-2-Nurse) helps support both babies at the right height. As babies get older and have more head control, the cradle hold and cross-cradle hold become options too.

Should I feed twins at the same time or separately?

Tandem feeding (both at once) saves time and is something many parents of twins rely on. However, feeding separately has advantages too, particularly in the early weeks when latching both babies simultaneously can be challenging without help. Many families start with separate feeds until they are confident in positioning, then move to tandem once both babies are latching well. There is no single right approach.

How do I keep track of which twin fed from which side?

Alternating which baby feeds on which side each session helps ensure both breasts receive equal stimulation. You can use a hair tie moved from wrist to wrist, a sticky note, or an app like Cubby to log each feed. Some parents assign each twin a dedicated side to keep it simple, especially if one baby is a stronger feeder than the other.

Is combination feeding with formula okay for twins?

Absolutely. Combination feeding is a very common and practical approach for twins, particularly if one or both babies were born early or spent time in NICU. Supplementing with formula for some feeds does not have to mean the end of breastfeeding. Many families settle into a pattern where they breastfeed for some feeds and use formula for others, which allows other caregivers to share the feeding load and gives the breastfeeding parent more rest.

What support organisations exist specifically for twins?

The Twins Trust (UK) offers a breastfeeding support line staffed by trained counsellors who have breastfed twins themselves. TAMBA (Twins and Multiple Births Association) also provides resources and local groups. La Leche League and the Association of Breastfeeding Mothers can connect you with peer supporters, and many IBCLCs have specific experience supporting parents of multiples. You do not have to figure this out without help.
