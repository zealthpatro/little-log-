Viral illness in babies: stages, what to expect and when to worry

0 to 3 years · Health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

If your baby seems to go from one illness straight into the next in their first year, you are not imagining it, and nothing has gone wrong. The average baby gets 8 to 10 viral illnesses in their first two years of life. Understanding why, what the typical illness arc looks like, and when to be genuinely concerned makes each episode far less frightening to manage.

Why babies get so many viral illnesses

Your baby arrived in the world with some immunity borrowed from you, passed across the placenta during pregnancy. Breastfeeding continues to provide antibodies through breast milk. But your baby's own immune system has never encountered the hundreds of respiratory viruses circulating in the population, and there is no shortcut to that experience. Each new virus requires the immune system to build a fresh response from scratch. That takes a few days, during which your baby feels unwell, and once the response is built, the immune system holds onto the memory for life.

Babies who attend nursery or spend time around older children are exposed to more viruses, which is why nursery-going babies often seem to be ill more frequently in the first year. This is not a failure of hygiene or care. It is simply more exposure, and it means their immune memory is developing faster. Studies consistently show these children tend to have fewer illnesses in later childhood as a result.

The typical viral illness arc

Most viral illnesses follow a predictable pattern. Knowing the stages helps you gauge where you are in the illness and what to expect next.

Incubation (1 to 4 days before symptoms): the virus has entered the body and is multiplying, but your baby has no symptoms yet. They are often already infectious during this phase, which is why viruses spread so effectively in nurseries and family households.

Prodrome (days 1 to 2 of symptoms): early, mild signs appear. Your baby may be slightly fussier than usual, slightly off their feeds, or running a low-grade temperature. You might sense something is coming without being able to name it. This phase can be hard to distinguish from normal unsettled days.

Peak phase (days 2 to 4): this is when your baby feels the worst. Fever is at its highest, symptoms are most obvious, and your baby is at their most miserable and most infectious. This peak phase can feel alarming, but it is normal and does not mean the illness is out of control. It is the immune response working at full intensity.

Resolution (days 4 to 7): fever is usually the first symptom to resolve. Your baby will slowly become more alert, more interested in feeds, and less distressed. Congestion and cough typically persist for up to 10 to 14 days after the fever has gone, which is normal. Appetite is usually the last thing to fully return.

Why antibiotics do not help

Antibiotics kill bacteria. They have no mechanism of action against viruses. Giving antibiotics for a viral illness does not shorten the illness, does not reduce symptoms, and does not prevent complications in otherwise healthy babies. The NHS advises against prescribing antibiotics for standard viral upper respiratory tract infections, and this is guidance the vast majority of GPs follow carefully.

Using antibiotics when they are not needed is not harmless. It contributes to antibiotic resistance, a serious and growing public health problem. It also disrupts your baby's gut microbiome, which can have short-term effects on their digestion. If a GP has not prescribed antibiotics, this is the right clinical decision, not a gap in care.

Common viruses in the first two years

The most frequent culprit is rhinovirus, which is responsible for the majority of common colds and causes well over 100 distinct strains, meaning your baby can catch rhinovirus many times without becoming immune to all of them. Other frequent viruses include parainfluenza (which causes croup), adenovirus, enterovirus, influenza, and various coronavirus strains distinct from SARS-CoV-2.

RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) deserves particular mention. It is the most common cause of bronchiolitis in babies under 1 year. Most healthy term babies manage RSV at home with supportive care. However, premature babies, very young babies under 3 months, and babies with underlying heart or lung conditions are at significantly higher risk of a severe episode that requires hospital admission. If your baby is in any of these groups and develops RSV symptoms, seek medical advice early rather than waiting to see how things develop.

When a viral illness becomes complicated

A bacterial infection can follow a viral one. The pattern to watch for is a fever that seemed to be improving and then returned, particularly on days 4 or 5. If your baby appeared to be getting better, then became clearly more unwell again with a rising temperature, this can signal a secondary bacterial infection such as an ear infection or, less commonly, a chest infection or urinary tract infection.

New or worsening localised symptoms are also a warning sign: ear pulling accompanied by obvious distress and high fever suggests a middle ear infection; rapid worsening of breathing difficulty beyond what congestion alone would explain suggests lower respiratory involvement. See a GP if these patterns develop rather than waiting another day or two.

Tracking the illness arc in Cubby

Logging your baby's temperature, symptoms, feeds, and wet nappies each day while they are ill gives you something genuinely useful: a clear picture of whether they are following the expected arc or deviating from it. Instead of relying on a general feeling of whether things are better or worse, you can look at the data. Did the temperature come down today compared to yesterday? Are there more wet nappies? Is feeding improving? When you call 111 or see a GP, having this log to hand makes the conversation faster and more accurate.

Frequently asked questions

How many colds is it normal for a baby to get in the first year?

The average baby gets 8 to 10 viral illnesses in their first two years. Babies in nursery or around older children may have more. This is normal and is how the immune system builds its memory. The frequency tends to decrease significantly in the second and third years as immunity accumulates.

Why does my baby keep getting ill?

Your baby's immune system has never encountered the hundreds of respiratory viruses circulating in the population. Each one requires a fresh response. It can feel relentless, especially in the first nursery year, but each illness is training the immune system. This is the process working as it should.

Do antibiotics help with viral infections in babies?

No. Antibiotics kill bacteria and have no effect on viruses. The NHS advises against prescribing antibiotics for standard viral illnesses. If a GP has not prescribed them, that is the correct clinical decision. Taking unnecessary antibiotics contributes to antibiotic resistance and disrupts your baby's gut microbiome without any benefit.

How do I know when my baby is getting better from a viral illness?

The fever usually resolves first, within 3 to 5 days. You will notice slightly more alertness, more interest in feeds, and more wet nappies. Congestion and a cough often persist for up to 10 to 14 days after the fever has gone. This is a normal tail end of the illness, not a sign of something new or continuing.

When does a viral illness become something more serious?

Watch for fever that returned after a period of improvement, new localised pain such as ear pulling with obvious distress, or a baby who looks very unwell despite several days having passed. These patterns can indicate a secondary bacterial infection and warrant a GP visit.

Why does my baby's temperature come and go during illness?

Fever naturally rises and falls in cycles as the immune response progresses. This is normal. A temperature that dips and rises across the day is expected. However, if a fever appeared to have fully resolved and then returns significantly on day 4 or 5, that is a different pattern and is worth checking with a GP, as it can occasionally signal a secondary infection.
