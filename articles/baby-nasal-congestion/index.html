Baby blocked nose: clearing congestion safely without sprays

0–12 months · Health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

A blocked nose that you could clear yourself in seconds becomes a different problem entirely when it is your baby's. They cannot blow their nose, they cannot tell you how uncomfortable they are, and a stuffed-up nose affects everything from feeding to sleep. The good news is that there are safe, effective ways to help, and most of what you need is already available at any pharmacy.

Why a blocked nose is harder for babies

Babies are obligate nose breathers. This means that, especially in the first few months of life, they have not yet developed the ability to easily switch to breathing through their mouth when their nose is blocked. Their anatomy and reflexes are simply not set up for it in the way that older children and adults manage automatically.

A blocked nose is therefore not just uncomfortable for a baby in the way it might be for you. It can make feeding very difficult: a baby trying to feed while struggling to breathe through a congested nose has to repeatedly break off to catch their breath, which means feeds take much longer, are more distressing, and your baby may take in less milk than they need. Sleep is similarly affected: a baby who cannot breathe comfortably through their nose is unlikely to settle and stay settled.

Understanding this helps explain why what seems like a small symptom can have a fairly outsized effect on your baby's day and night, and why it is worth knowing what to do about it.

Common causes of baby congestion

The most common cause of a blocked nose in babies is a viral cold. There are over 200 viruses that cause the common cold, and in their first year babies are exposed to many of them for the first time. This is entirely normal; it is how the immune system learns. A baby in nursery or with older siblings at home may seem to have a constant cold in their first winter, which is exhausting but usually not cause for alarm.

Other causes worth knowing about:

  • Dry air in centrally heated homes, particularly in winter. Central heating significantly reduces the moisture in indoor air, which can dry and thicken nasal secretions.
  • Irritants in the environment, including cigarette smoke, strong perfumes, air fresheners, or heavily scented cleaning products.
  • Milk reflux in some babies. When milk backs up from the stomach into the oesophagus and occasionally further, it can irritate the nasal passages and cause what looks like congestion, even without an obvious cold.
  • Occasionally, nasal congestion in a newborn can be a sign of a structural issue. If your baby has had severe nasal blockage from birth and has persistent difficulty breathing through the nose, mention it to your GP or health visitor.

Safe ways to help clear the nose

Saline (salt water) nasal drops or spray are the first, safest, and most recommended step. A few drops of plain saline in each nostril help to loosen and thin the mucus, making it easier to drain or clear. You can use saline drops two or three times a day, and they are safe from birth. Plain sodium chloride 0.9% saline, available cheaply from any pharmacy, works just as well as more expensive branded versions.

How to use saline drops: lay your baby on their back, tilt their head back gently, and place two or three drops in each nostril. Give it 30 seconds or so to work, then use a bulb syringe or nasal aspirator to gently remove loosened mucus if needed. Doing this before feeds is particularly helpful, as it gives your baby the best chance of feeding comfortably.

A bulb syringe or nasal aspirator (including electric ones such as the Frida NoseFrida) can be used to physically remove mucus. They are safe and effective when used gently. Use them after saline drops, no more than three or four times a day, and be gentle: overuse can irritate and inflame the delicate nasal lining, which makes things worse.

A cool mist humidifier in the bedroom adds moisture back to dry winter air and can reduce the stickiness of nasal secretions overnight. NHS guidance on room temperature suggests keeping a baby's bedroom at around 16 to 20°C. Avoid making the room very warm, as overheating is an independent risk factor for SIDS and does not help with congestion.

The steam bath approach

Sitting in a bathroom with a hot shower running to create a steam-filled room is a widely shared home remedy for baby congestion. The idea is that the warm, moist air loosens mucus and makes breathing temporarily easier. Many parents find it helpful anecdotally.

However, NHS guidance no longer recommends it strongly, and the scientific evidence for its effectiveness is limited. There is also a safety consideration: a bathroom with a hot shower running contains hot water and steam near ground level, and a wriggling baby in that environment requires constant, very close supervision. If you do try the steam room approach, stay with your baby at all times and keep them well away from the hot water source. A cool mist humidifier in the bedroom has a better safety profile and achieves a similar goal more gently.

Positioning for feeds and sleep

Feeding your baby in a slightly more upright position than usual can reduce how much congestion affects their ability to feed. Holding your baby more upright against your chest, or using a more reclined feeding position for bottle feeding, lets gravity do some of the work.

For sleep, the safe sleep guidance from the NHS and Lullaby Trust is clear: babies should always sleep flat on their back, on a firm, flat surface. Do not prop up one end of the mattress, do not use wedges, and do not put anything in the cot to raise their head. These interventions have not been shown to help with congestion and are associated with increased risk. The safe sleep position does not change because your baby has a cold.

What not to use

NHS guidance is explicit on several products that should never be used in babies:

  • Decongestant nasal sprays and drops containing xylometazoline or oxymetazoline should never be used in babies and children under six. These are the active ingredient in many adult nasal decongestant sprays. In babies they can cause rebound congestion, raised blood pressure, and other serious side effects. The adult versions sold in pharmacies are not safe for babies, even in small amounts.
  • Menthol rubs such as Vicks VapoRub should not be used on or near babies under two. Both the NHS and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) advise against this. The menthol vapour can irritate a baby's airways and has been reported to cause breathing difficulties. The same applies to other menthol-containing vapour rubs or baby-marketed products with similar ingredients.
  • Camphor-containing products are potentially toxic to babies and should not be used.
  • Essential oil diffusers, particularly those using eucalyptus, peppermint, or tea tree oil, should be avoided around babies. These oils can irritate the airways even in diffused form.

Stick to saline. It is the only proven-safe, effective option for babies.

When to seek help

Most baby congestion is caused by a cold and will resolve on its own within seven to ten days. However, see your GP or contact 111 if:

  • Your baby has a high fever alongside the congestion, particularly any fever in a baby under three months, or a fever over 39°C in an older baby.
  • They are having trouble breathing even when their nose seems relatively clear: you notice fast breathing, nostrils flaring, or the skin between or below their ribs pulling in with each breath.
  • They are feeding very poorly because of the blockage, and you are concerned about dehydration.
  • The congestion has lasted more than ten days without the other signs of a cold improving. A cold should steadily get better; if it is not, or if it seems to be getting worse after the first week, it is worth getting it checked.
  • Your baby seems increasingly unwell rather than gradually better.

If your baby is breathing fast, has their ribs visibly pulling in with each breath, or their lips are turning blue or grey, these are emergency signs. Call 999 or go to A&E immediately.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a nasal aspirator on my baby?

Yes. Both bulb syringes and electric nasal aspirators are safe and effective. Use them gently, no more than three or four times a day, and always after saline drops to loosen the mucus first. Over-use can cause irritation to the nasal lining and make the congestion worse.

Are saline drops safe for newborns?

Yes. Plain saline drops (sodium chloride 0.9%) are safe from birth. Use two or three drops in each nostril before feeds. Pharmacy own-brand saline drops or sprays work just as well as named brands and are considerably cheaper.

Why does my baby sound congested all the time?

Some babies sound snuffly even when they do not have a cold. This is often simply because their nasal passages are very small and any normal secretions can sound loud when breathing. If your baby is feeding well, gaining weight, and seems comfortable and settled, this is usually not a cause for concern. Mention it at your next health visitor appointment if it worries you.

Can I use Vicks VapoRub on my baby?

No. The NHS and AAP both advise against using Vicks VapoRub or similar menthol products in babies under two years. The menthol vapour can cause airway irritation and has been linked to breathing difficulties. If you want to add moisture to the air, a plain cool mist humidifier running with water only is a safer option.

When does congestion mean something more serious?

If your baby's breathing rate increases significantly, if you see their nostrils flaring or their chest retracting (the skin between or below the ribs pulling in with each breath), or if they seem distressed even when not actively trying to feed, these are signs to seek urgent help. Congestion alone is not usually an emergency, but breathing difficulty is.

Do decongestant medicines work for babies?

No. Decongestant medicines containing pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine, or xylometazoline are not safe and are not recommended for babies and children under six. They can have serious side effects including raised blood pressure, and they are not licensed for this age group. The only safe option for babies with nasal congestion is saline.
