Toddler aggression at playgroup: handling biting and hitting in social settings

Behaviour · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Your toddler is sweet and calm at home, but something happens the moment you walk through the door of the playgroup. By the end of the session, another child has been bitten or hit, and you are leaving red-faced and exhausted. If this sounds familiar, you are far from alone. Social settings are the hardest possible environment for a toddler's underdeveloped regulation system, and aggression in group settings is one of the most common and distressing challenges parents face in this age range. Here is what is actually happening, and what genuinely helps.

Why aggression is more common in group settings

Playgroup sessions are genuinely more demanding for a toddler than almost any other part of their week. At home, the environment is familiar, resources are plentiful, and the number of unpredictable people is small. At a playgroup, everything changes at once: a loud room full of children, toys that must be shared, adults who are not the primary carer, and a constantly shifting social landscape. The toddler brain responds to this heightened arousal by becoming less regulated, not more. When arousal levels rise, impulse control falls.

Resource competition plays a particularly significant role. Toddlers are developmentally egocentric: they understand the world from their own perspective and have not yet developed the cognitive ability to fully model other children's wants. When a toy they want is taken, or when another child moves into their space, the body reacts before the brain can reason. Add noise, heat, and novelty, and the conditions for a physical response become very clear. It is not defiance and it is not cruelty. It is a nervous system running out of room.

The developmental explanation

Between the ages of 1 and 3, toddlers are in a period of intense neurological development, but the part of the brain responsible for impulse control, the prefrontal cortex, is barely online. It will not fully mature until well into adulthood. A toddler who bites or hits in a group setting is not demonstrating a character flaw or predicting a future of aggression. They are demonstrating that their developing brain has hit its limit for the day.

Language development plays an equally important role. Many toddlers who bite or hit do so because they do not yet have the words to say "I am frustrated," "that is mine," or "this is too much." The body says it instead. As language grows and the prefrontal cortex matures, physical aggression almost universally decreases. The child who bites at 18 months does not become the child who bites at 5. You are in a phase, even when it does not feel like one.

The immediate response: attend the hurt child first

The instinct when your child bites or hits another child is to rush to your own child immediately. This instinct is understandable, but it works against you. Rushing to the hitter with a big emotional response, even an angry one, provides exactly what the toddler's aroused nervous system can begin to associate with aggression: intense adult attention. This can inadvertently reinforce the behaviour over time.

The more effective approach is to attend to the hurt child first, calmly and warmly. Get down to their level, check they are okay, and acknowledge what happened: "That was a bite, that hurts, let me see." This takes about 20 to 30 seconds. Then, briefly and calmly, address the hitter: "Biting hurts. We do not bite." Keep the message short. Long explanations, drawn-out corrections, or emotional escalation from the parent all add stimulation to an already over-stimulated situation. Say it once, clearly, and move on. The calm brevity is the point.

What to do when leaving is not an option

Sometimes you cannot leave the playgroup immediately. Transport is not available, the session is mid-way, or leaving would create a bigger disruption. In these cases, the priority is to reduce stimulation without exiting entirely.

Move your child to a quieter corner or edge of the space. Sit with them without demand or extended correction. Give them five to ten minutes of close, quiet proximity before considering reintegration. This allows the arousal level to come down and gives you information about whether they are ready to re-enter the group. Watch for signs that arousal is building again: flushed cheeks, increased voice volume, clinginess, pushing into other children's space. Intervening at this stage, before the incident, is always easier than responding after it.

Scripting the response to other parents

The moment after a biting or hitting incident is socially uncomfortable. The urge to over-explain, minimise, or become defensive is strong. A calm, direct acknowledgment works better than any of these options.

Try: "I am so sorry, he is really working on this and we are taking it seriously." Or: "I know how upsetting that must be, we are actively addressing it." You do not need to provide a developmental lecture or justify your child's behaviour. What matters is that you are visibly present, you are not dismissing the other child's distress, and you are following through with a clear response to your own child. If the other parent is upset, give them space to feel that without matching their distress. Your own calm tone sets the register for the whole interaction.

How to support your child's regulation before and during playgroup

What happens before the session is one of the most powerful variables you can influence. A tired or hungry toddler arriving at a loud playgroup is already starting with a depleted regulatory reserve. Prioritise a nap or quiet time before the session, make sure they have eaten well, and leave enough time that arrival is not rushed.

Brief one-on-one time before the session, even 10 to 15 minutes of calm connection with you, acts as a regulatory top-up. Narrate what is about to happen in simple terms: "We are going to playgroup now. There will be lots of children. I will be there with you." During the session, stay aware of your child's arousal state. You are the monitoring system. Check in without hovering, and be ready to move closer when you see the early signs of overwhelm. Prevention is not overprotection. It is scaffolding.

Working with the playgroup leader or nursery

Transparency with the playgroup leader is worth more than embarrassment. Let them know you are aware of the pattern and that you are working on it. Ask them to keep an eye on antecedents: what happened in the minute before the incident? Was a toy taken? Was there a sudden noise? Was your child at the edge of the group for a long time without connection?

A consistent response across all the adults in the room matters more than the sophistication of any single response. Inconsistency, where different adults respond in different ways, prolongs the pattern because the child cannot build a reliable expectation of what follows. If the playgroup is willing to work with you on a consistent whole-room approach, this is a significant asset. Most experienced leaders have navigated this many times and welcome a collaborative plan.

When to reduce social situations temporarily

If every session ends in an incident despite preparation and consistent response, the current level of stimulation may simply be more than your child can manage right now. This is not failure. It is information, and acting on it is good parenting.

Reducing the frequency of sessions, shortening the duration, or trying a smaller group are all legitimate and effective responses. Some children at 18 to 24 months genuinely cannot regulate in large group settings yet, and that is entirely okay. Social development is not a race. A child who spends fewer sessions in an overwhelming large group and more time in small play dates or one-to-one settings may develop social skills more effectively than one who is overwhelmed and aggressive in a large group each week. Return to larger settings gradually as their capacity grows.

Frequently asked questions

My child only bites at playgroup, never at home. Why?

Group settings are significantly more demanding on a toddler's regulatory system than the home environment. At home, the space is familiar, resources are plentiful, and the pace is shaped by the child's own rhythm. At playgroup, all of that changes at once. The biting is a regulation problem specific to a set of conditions, not a character problem. The fact that it does not happen at home is actually good evidence of that.

How do I apologise to the parent of a child my toddler bit?

Directly and simply: "I am so sorry, I know that must have been upsetting and painful for your child." Do not minimise what happened, but do not spiral into excessive shame. Most parents understand that toddler biting is a developmental reality, even if they are shaken in the moment. Following up by briefly sharing how you are addressing it, if asked, is appropriate and usually well received.

Should my toddler be banned from playgroup after biting?

Exclusion is rarely an effective or appropriate response to developmental aggression at this age. A consistent, calm, brief response combined with antecedent management is far more useful than removal. If the playgroup is considering exclusion, it may be worth discussing a supported, shorter-session plan instead, with clear goals for return to full participation. Full exclusion is a last resort, not a first response.

My toddler bites the same child every time. What does that mean?

There may be a specific dynamic between the two children that consistently tips your child's regulation. The other child may play loudly, compete for the same toys, or move into personal space in a way that repeatedly overwhelms yours. Managing their proximity while working on regulation skills is a practical short-term approach. It is not about blame on either side. It is about reducing the conditions that reliably lead to an incident.

Is my toddler going to be aggressive forever?

Almost certainly not. Physical aggression in group settings peaks in the 18- to 30-month window and declines sharply as language develops and the prefrontal cortex matures. With consistent responses and appropriate support, the vast majority of children have resolved group aggression well before age 4. You are in a difficult phase, but it is a phase with a clear developmental endpoint.

The playgroup leader said my child needs to be "disciplined more firmly." How do I respond?

Acknowledge their concern and thank them for raising it. You can gently share that the current evidence on toddler aggression points to brief, consistent responses and antecedent management as more effective than prolonged corrections. Ask if they can work with you on a consistent, whole-room response. If the environment continues to feel unsupportive of your child's developmental needs, it is entirely reasonable to try a different group.
