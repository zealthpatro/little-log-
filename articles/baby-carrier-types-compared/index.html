Baby carrier types compared: wrap, ring sling and structured carrier

Newborn · Comparisons · Reviewed 12 June 2026 · All articles

Carrying your baby close is something families around the world have done for generations. The range of slings and carriers available today can feel overwhelming, but every option falls into one of a few broad categories: stretchy wrap, woven wrap, ring sling, or structured buckle carrier. Understanding what distinguishes each type, and which situations each suits, makes choosing far more manageable.

The TICKS safety checklist: applies to every carrier, every time

Before comparing styles, it helps to understand the one safety framework that covers all slings and carriers. The Consortium of UK Sling Manufacturers and Retailers developed the TICKS checklist. Each letter describes something that must be true every single time you put your baby in any carrier:

  • Tight. Slings and carriers should be kept tight enough to hug your baby close to your body. Any slack allows your baby to slump into a position that can restrict breathing.
  • In view at all times. You should be able to see your baby's face at all times, without needing to open or adjust the carrier to check.
  • Close enough to kiss. Your baby should be positioned high enough that you can bend your head forward and kiss the top of their head.
  • Keep chin off the chest. You must ensure your baby's chin is not resting on their chest. A chin dropped to the chest can restrict the airway. This is the primary safety risk associated with slings and carriers.
  • Supported back. Your baby's back should be supported so it is comfortably upright. A baby should not be allowed to slump or bow outward.

Running through the TICKS checklist takes only a few seconds and significantly reduces the risk of your baby's airway being compromised. The danger with slings and carriers occurs when a baby's mouth or nose is covered, or when their chin falls onto their chest and compresses the airway.

Stretchy wraps

A stretchy wrap is a long length of knitted, slightly elasticated fabric, typically around four to five metres, that you tie on your body before placing your baby inside the fabric.

Stretchy wraps are popular choices for newborns. The soft, stretchy fabric molds closely to both caregiver and baby, and the hold tends to feel snug and secure. Because the wrap is tied before the baby is placed in, there is no need to adjust buckles or rings with a baby already in your arms.

The tying process requires practice. It is worth working through the tie several times without your baby before using it for the first time with them. Stretchy wraps can also become harder to keep supportive as a baby grows heavier, because the knit fabric stretches more under increasing weight. Most parents find stretchy wraps most useful during the early newborn months.

Ring slings

A ring sling is a length of fabric, usually around two metres, threaded through two metal rings. You wear it over one shoulder and adjust the fit by pulling the fabric through the rings to tighten or loosen different sections of the sling.

Ring slings are quick to use once the ring-tightening technique has been learned, and they adjust easily for different caregivers. They suit shorter carries, newborns, and situations where you need to put your baby in and out frequently. The over-one-shoulder carry style feels natural to many parents.

Because the weight rests on one shoulder, ring slings can become tiring during longer outings. Correct rail tightening is important: there should be no slack that would allow your baby to slump. The TICKS checklist applies on every use, as with all carrier types.

Structured buckle carriers

Structured carriers, sometimes called soft structured carriers, use padded shoulder straps, a waistband, and buckles or clips to create a shaped carrier that fastens in a consistent, repeatable way.

Many parents find structured carriers the most straightforward to put on and adjust, because the buckles work in a predictable way each time. They typically distribute weight across both shoulders and the hips, which many caregivers find comfortable for extended carries. Most structured carriers support a front-facing-in position, and many also allow a back carry once a baby has good head and neck control.

Structured carriers designed for newborns often require either a specific newborn setting built into the carrier or a separate newborn insert, which positions a small baby correctly for their age and size. Always read the manufacturer's guidance for minimum weight and the correct newborn carry position before using one with a young baby.

Woven wraps

Woven wraps use non-stretch, woven fabric in the same long-cloth format as stretchy wraps. Because the cloth does not stretch, it stays supportive across a much wider age and weight range than a stretchy wrap.

Woven wraps can be tied in many different carry positions, including back carries, and they adjust widely, so a single wrap can often be shared between caregivers of different sizes. This flexibility comes with a steeper learning curve than any of the other carrier types. There are many possible ties, and getting each one right takes real practice. For parents who want something usable from day one with minimal preparation, this investment can feel significant.

Choosing a carrier for your family

No single carrier type is objectively best. A few practical questions help narrow the choice. If you want to get out of the door quickly with minimal preparation, a structured buckle carrier is typically the fastest to put on. If you will be sharing the carrier between two caregivers of different sizes, woven wraps and ring slings adjust widely, but both require the second caregiver to learn the technique. For long outings, a structured carrier or woven wrap often distributes weight more evenly than a ring sling worn on one shoulder.

If you are unsure which style suits you, a sling library is a useful starting point. These are community-run services, sometimes based at children's centres or NHS trusts, where a trained sling consultant or peer supporter can help you try different carrier types and achieve a safe, comfortable fit for your baby and your body before you commit to buying.

Feeding in a carrier

It is not safe to feed your baby while they are in a sling. If you need to feed your baby, take them out of the sling, feed them in a safe position, and then return them to the carrier when the feed is finished. This applies to both breastfeeding and bottle feeding.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a sling or carrier with a newborn?

Yes. Many carriers are suitable from birth. Whichever type you choose, apply the TICKS checklist every time: Tight, In view at all times, Close enough to kiss, Keep chin off the chest, and Supported back. Keeping the chin off the chest is especially important for young babies because a compressed airway is the primary documented risk associated with slings and carriers.

What is the TICKS safety rule for slings and carriers?

TICKS covers five checks for every carry. T is Tight: the sling or carrier should hug your baby close with no loose sections. I is In view at all times: you should be able to see your baby's face without moving or opening the carrier. C is Close enough to kiss: bending your head forward, you should be able to touch your lips to the top of your baby's head. K is Keep chin off the chest: there must be sufficient clearance to protect the airway. S is Supported back: your baby's back should be comfortably upright and supported.

Is it safe to breastfeed in a sling?

No. It is not safe to feed your baby while they are in a sling. If you need to feed your baby, take them out of the sling first, feed them, and then return them to the carrier when the feed is finished.

Which carrier type is easiest to use day to day?

Structured buckle carriers are widely considered the most straightforward because the buckles and clips adjust in a consistent way each time. Ring slings become quick to use once the ring-tightening technique feels familiar. Stretchy wraps take the most practice to tie correctly but are particularly well suited to newborns. Woven wraps are the most versatile but have the steepest learning curve. A sling library lets you try each style with guidance before buying.

What is a sling library?

A sling library is a community service where you can try different carrier types, get a fitting from a trained peer supporter, and sometimes borrow a carrier before buying one. Some sling libraries are run from NHS children's centres or children's services. A sling consultant or peer supporter can show you how to achieve a safe, comfortable fit for your specific baby and body.
