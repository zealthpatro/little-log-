Types of infant formula in the UK: what parents need to know

Newborn · Comparisons · Reviewed 12 June 2026 · All articles

Walk the baby aisle of any UK supermarket and you will find a dozen different formula tins, each with confident-sounding names. Hungry baby formula, comfort formula, follow-on formula, anti-reflux formula: the range can feel overwhelming, especially in those first sleep-deprived days. The good news is that the NHS guidance is clear once you know what each product actually is and when, if ever, it is appropriate to use it.

Infant formula sold in the UK is regulated and must meet strict nutritional standards. Most formula is made from cow's milk that has been treated and modified to be safe and nutritious for babies. It comes in two forms: a dry powder that you make up with boiled water, or a ready-to-feed liquid that is more convenient but costs more and must be used quickly once opened.

First infant formula: the one to start with

First infant formula, sometimes sold as first milk, is suitable from birth and is the formula the NHS recommends as the starting point for all formula-fed babies. It is based on whey protein, which is considered easier for babies to digest than casein-based alternatives.

Unless a healthcare professional advises otherwise, first infant formula is the only type your baby needs. You can continue using it throughout the entire first year, including after solid foods are introduced at around 6 months. There is no need to switch formulas as your baby grows unless a health professional recommends it for a specific reason.

Goat's milk formula

Goat's milk formula has become more widely available in UK pharmacies and supermarkets. These products are produced to the same nutritional standards as cow's milk-based formula and are suitable from birth.

One important point from the NHS: goat's milk formula is not less likely to cause allergies in babies than cow's milk formula. The proteins in goat's milk are structurally similar to those in cow's milk. This means goat's milk formula is not a safe alternative for babies with a diagnosed cow's milk protein allergy.

Specialist formulas and when a health professional might suggest them

Several formulas are available over the counter in the UK, but the NHS recommends only using them on professional advice. Using the wrong formula unnecessarily can lead to preparation errors and, in some cases, additional health risks.

Hungry baby formula (sometimes called hungry milk) is suitable from birth but the NHS says to ask a midwife or health visitor before using it. It contains more casein than whey. Casein is a protein that takes longer to break down and digest. Although manufacturers often describe these products as suitable for hungrier babies, the NHS states clearly that there is no evidence babies settle better or sleep longer on this type of formula.

Anti-reflux formula (sometimes labelled staydown) is available from birth but only under medical supervision. It is thickened to help reduce reflux, which is when babies bring up milk during or after a feed. Some manufacturers of anti-reflux formula recommend using lower water temperatures than the standard 70°C guideline, because the thickening agent can become lumpy at higher temperatures. The NHS highlights a safety concern here: powdered formula is not sterile, and preparing it at a lower temperature will not kill potentially harmful bacteria. Extra care during preparation is essential if you are using this type.

Comfort formula contains partially hydrolysed cow's milk proteins, meaning the proteins have been broken down into smaller pieces. It is marketed for babies with digestive discomfort such as colic or constipation, and is suitable from birth. However, the NHS notes there is no evidence to support these claims. Comfort formula is also not suitable for babies with a cow's milk protein allergy, because it still contains cow's milk proteins.

Lactose-free formula is designed for the rare infant who cannot tolerate lactose, the natural sugar found in milk. Symptoms of lactose intolerance in babies include diarrhoea, abdominal pain, wind, and bloating. This formula is available from birth but should only be used under medical supervision, as true lactose intolerance in young infants is uncommon.

Hypoallergenic formula uses fully hydrolysed proteins, where the cow's milk proteins have been broken down much more thoroughly than in comfort formula. This type is generally prescribed by a doctor for babies with a diagnosed cow's milk protein allergy. It differs significantly from comfort formula, which only partially breaks down the proteins and is therefore not appropriate for allergy management.

Soya formula: use only when recommended

Soya formula is made from soya beans rather than cow's milk and is sometimes considered for babies who cannot tolerate cow's milk. The NHS advises using soya formula only from 6 months old, and only if a doctor or health professional has recommended it.

There are two reasons for caution. First, soya formula contains phytoestrogens, which are naturally occurring plant compounds with a chemical structure similar to oestrogen. There are concerns about the effect of phytoestrogens on development in very young infants consuming soya formula as their only food. Second, the NHS notes that soya formula is more likely than cow's milk formula to harm a baby's teeth.

Follow-on formula and toddler milks

Follow-on formula is marketed for babies from 6 months. The NHS is explicit: follow-on formula must never be given to babies under 6 months old. Research also shows that switching to follow-on formula at 6 months provides no benefits over continuing to use first infant formula. The packaging of follow-on formula is often designed to look similar to first infant formula, so it is worth reading the label carefully before buying.

Growing-up milks and toddler milks are aimed at children from 12 months. The NHS states that children aged 1 to 5 years do not need these products. From the age of 1, whole or semi-skimmed pasteurised cow's, goat's, or sheep's milk is appropriate as a main drink and no specialist formula is required.

Milks to avoid in the first year

The NHS is clear that several products should never be given as a drink to babies under 12 months:

  • Condensed milk, evaporated milk, and dried milk
  • Unpasteurised goat's or sheep's milk
  • Plant-based drinks such as soya, rice, oat, or almond
  • Cow's milk as a main drink (though it can be used in cooking and with foods from 6 months)

If you are unsure which formula is right for your baby, speak with your midwife, health visitor, or GP before making any change.

Frequently asked questions

Which formula should I use from birth?

First infant formula (sometimes called first milk) is the formula the NHS recommends from birth. It is based on whey protein and can be used throughout the entire first year, including after solid foods are introduced at around 6 months.

Is goat's milk formula better for babies who might have allergies?

No. The NHS states that goat's milk formula is not less likely to cause allergies than cow's milk formula. The proteins in goat's milk are similar to those in cow's milk, so it is not suitable for babies with a diagnosed cow's milk allergy.

Can I switch to hungry baby formula if my newborn seems unsettled?

The NHS says there is no evidence that babies settle better or sleep longer on hungrier baby formula. It contains more casein, which is harder to digest. Speak to your midwife or health visitor before making any switch.

When can my baby start follow-on formula?

Follow-on formula must never be given to babies under 6 months old. The NHS also notes that research shows switching to follow-on formula at 6 months provides no benefits over continuing with first infant formula.

Is soya formula safe for newborns?

The NHS advises using soya formula only from 6 months and only if recommended by a doctor or health professional. Soya formula contains phytoestrogens, which have a chemical structure similar to oestrogen, and there are concerns about their effect on development in young infants. It is also more likely than cow's milk formula to harm a baby's teeth.
