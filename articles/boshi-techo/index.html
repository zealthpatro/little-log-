Boshi techo: Japan's mother and child health handbook

Pregnancy · 1st trimester · Reviewed 15 June 2026 · All articles

The boshi techo is one of the most distinctive features of Japan's approach to maternal and child health. Officially called the Boshi Kenko Techo (mother and child health handbook), it is a single booklet that follows a family from the moment a pregnancy is registered through the child's first years of school. Issued free of charge by the local government, it functions as a portable, unified record that every healthcare provider involved in the journey can read and contribute to. For anyone navigating pregnancy or early parenthood in Japan, understanding what the boshi techo is and how it works is an early and important step.

What is the boshi techo?

The boshi techo is an official health record booklet provided to all pregnant women in Japan at no cost. The name translates literally as "mother and child health handbook," and that description captures exactly what it does: it holds health information about the mother during pregnancy and then, after the birth, transitions into a continuing record for the child. A single document covers the antenatal period, the birth itself, and the child's health from the newborn stage through primary school entry.

The system was introduced in 1948 and has been internationally recognised as an effective model for ensuring continuity of care across different healthcare providers and life stages. Because the record travels with the family rather than staying in a clinic file, there is no gap in information when a woman moves between hospitals, changes midwife or relocates to a different city. Every professional who sees the mother or child writes in the same booklet, creating a running account of health that is always to hand.

When and where to get it

The boshi techo is obtained from your local municipal office, which is the city hall (shiyakusho) or ward office (kuyakusho) depending on where you live. You go there to register your pregnancy, and the booklet is issued as part of that registration process. There is no charge for either the registration or the booklet itself.

Most families register in the first trimester, as soon as pregnancy is confirmed by a doctor. Registering early is practical for several reasons. It entitles you to access various maternal and child health services administered at the municipal level, and it means the boshi techo is in your possession from the start of your antenatal care. You will be asked to bring it to every antenatal appointment, so having it early avoids gaps in your records from the beginning of the pregnancy.

Some municipal offices have specific windows or counters for pregnancy registration, and staff will explain the local support services available to you at the same time. These can include subsidised health checks, parenting classes, and postnatal visiting programmes, all organised at the local government level.

What the boshi techo records

The booklet is divided into sections that reflect the different stages it covers. During pregnancy, it functions as the mother's antenatal record. Healthcare providers use it to note the findings at each visit: blood pressure readings, weight, the results of blood tests, information from ultrasound scans, and observations about the progress of the pregnancy. The record is cumulative, so any provider picking it up can quickly see the pattern across multiple appointments.

The birth itself is recorded in the booklet, capturing details such as the date and time of delivery, the mode of birth, and the baby's condition at birth including weight and length. This section bridges the maternal record and the child's record within the same document.

After birth, the boshi techo becomes the child's health record. It is used at every well-child check-up, including the one-month, three-month, six-month, nine-to-ten-month and eighteen-month reviews. At each check-up, healthcare providers record the child's growth measurements, developmental observations, and any health concerns that arise. Vaccination records are also kept in the booklet, meaning that the immunisation history is held alongside the broader health record rather than in a separate document.

Because the same booklet records both vaccinations and developmental milestones, it gives families and healthcare providers a complete picture of the child's health at any given point. Growth charts within the booklet allow weight and height to be tracked visually over time. The document is presented at school entry, where it forms part of the child's health history for the education system.

Why the system matters

Continuity of care is a challenge in any healthcare system. When records are held by individual clinics or hospitals, information can be lost if a patient moves, changes provider, or is seen in an emergency by an unfamiliar team. The boshi techo addresses this by placing the record in the hands of the family. The mother, and later the parents, carry it to every appointment rather than relying on different institutions to share information with each other.

This design has practical benefits at every stage. An antenatal care team can immediately see the full history of a pregnancy when they open the booklet at an appointment. An emergency department seeing a child can look at their vaccination record and recent developmental check-ups in the same place. A paediatrician picking up care of a child who has moved from another city has a complete record from birth rather than a blank file.

The boshi techo has been studied as a model by health systems in other countries precisely because it places comprehensive, longitudinal records with the family rather than within fragmented institutional systems. Its longevity since 1948 reflects how well the approach has worked within Japan's healthcare context.

The boshi techo for non-Japanese residents

Japan has a growing international population, and the practical question of language is a real one for non-Japanese speakers. The standard boshi techo is written in Japanese. However, some municipalities with larger international communities have produced versions in English or other languages. It is worth asking your local municipal office directly whether a multilingual version is available in your area.

In areas where official multilingual versions are not available, local international community groups in some cities produce unofficial English translation guides that explain the sections of the booklet and what each part is asking. These are not official documents but can be a helpful supplement when navigating the system. Your midwife, obstetrician or local international support group may be able to point you toward relevant resources.

The registration process itself can usually be navigated with some advance preparation. Municipal offices in larger cities often have staff who speak English, and the process of pregnancy registration follows a similar pattern across municipalities even if the specific layout of offices varies.

Frequently asked questions

What is the boshi techo?

The boshi techo (mother and child health handbook) is an official health record booklet issued free to all pregnant women in Japan. It records the mother's antenatal health, the birth, and then the child's health from birth through school entry, including vaccinations and developmental check-ups.

When and where do I get the boshi techo?

You receive it from your local municipal office (city hall or ward office) when you register your pregnancy. Most women register in the first trimester. Registration also entitles you to various maternal and child health services offered by the municipality.

Is the boshi techo used throughout childhood?

Yes. After the birth, the boshi techo becomes the child's health record, used at every well-child check-up and for recording vaccinations. It is presented at school entry and remains an important document throughout the child's early years.

Are boshi techo available in English?

Some municipalities with large international populations issue versions in English or other languages. It is worth asking your local municipal office. Unofficial English translation guides are also produced by local international community groups in some areas.
