Body image after having a baby: navigating a body that feels unfamiliar

Mental health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Very few people come out of pregnancy and birth with the same relationship to their body that they went in with. Your body has done something extraordinary, and it looks and functions differently now. Adjusting to that, in a world full of pressure to look a certain way, is harder than it is given credit for.

What actually changes after birth

The physical changes of pregnancy and birth are significant and wide-ranging. Understanding what is actually happening can help make the changes feel less alarming.

Abdomen: The muscles and skin of the abdomen have stretched substantially over nine months. The uterus takes several weeks to return to its pre-pregnancy size. Many women experience diastasis recti (a separation of the abdominal muscles) which can make the abdomen look softer or protrude slightly. All of this is normal and usually improves over time, though it rarely returns to exactly how it was.

Skin: Stretch marks, which may fade but rarely disappear entirely, are extremely common. Skin on the abdomen can feel different in texture and elasticity for many months after birth.

Breasts: Whether you breastfeed or not, your breasts change after pregnancy and birth. They may be larger, then smaller, then shaped differently than before. These changes can take months to stabilise.

Hips and pelvis: The pelvis widens during pregnancy, partly under the influence of the hormone relaxin, and hips may genuinely be broader than before.

Hair: Significant hair loss in the months after birth is extremely common. It is related to the post-birth drop in oestrogen and tends to resolve by around six months postpartum, though it can be distressing while it is happening.

Weight: Many people lose some weight in the early weeks postpartum but not all of it immediately, and the idea of a rapid return to a pre-pregnancy weight does not reflect the reality for most bodies.

Why recovery is rarely linear

The popular narrative about postpartum body recovery implies a steady progression toward something resembling your pre-pregnancy body. For most people, the reality is more complex. Progress happens, then stalls. Things improve, then a growth spurt or a return to menstruation shifts things again. Breastfeeding keeps certain hormones elevated, which affects everything from body composition to mood to how your joints feel.

This non-linearity is normal. It does not mean you are not recovering. It means recovery is a process, not a project with a deadline.

The gap between social media and reality

The postpartum bodies most visible on social media are not representative of the range of postpartum bodies in the real world. They are more likely to reflect genetic advantage, professional support, financial resources, and significant editing. Comparing your body in the early months after birth to those images is not a fair comparison, and it is not a neutral one, either. Those comparisons can quietly erode how you feel about yourself and your recovery.

Being selective about what you consume during the postpartum period is a practical, evidence-supported thing to do. You do not have to follow accounts that make you feel bad about your own body.

Diastasis recti and body confidence

Diastasis recti, the separation of the rectus abdominis muscles that very commonly occurs during pregnancy, is one of the physical changes that can significantly affect how a postnatal body looks and functions. It can cause the abdomen to bulge forward, particularly when straining, and it can affect core stability and how comfortable you feel in your body.

The good news is that diastasis recti often improves significantly with the right rehabilitation, guided by a physiotherapist with pelvic health expertise. Understanding the mechanics of what has happened, and working with your body rather than against it, tends to produce both better physical outcomes and a more positive relationship with your body than pushing through exercises that are not appropriate for the condition.

Pelvic floor recovery and body confidence

The pelvic floor is one of the most important and most overlooked aspects of postpartum recovery. Weakness or dysfunction in the pelvic floor can affect how your body feels from the inside, and working to restore its function, with support from a pelvic health physiotherapist if available, can significantly improve how you feel in your body more broadly.

A body that functions well, that you can trust to hold you up and move without pain, tends to feel like a more habitable place than a body that looks a certain way but does not function reliably. Shifting attention toward function as well as appearance can be a meaningful reframe.

When body dissatisfaction becomes a clinical concern

It is normal to feel unfamiliar with your postnatal body, and some level of dissatisfaction is understandable and common. But there is a point at which body image concerns move beyond the normal range and begin to affect your wellbeing in ways that deserve attention.

It may be worth speaking to your GP if you are:

  • Spending significant parts of each day thinking about your body in distressing ways
  • Restricting food intake significantly, particularly while breastfeeding
  • Exercising compulsively in ways that are affecting your recovery or your relationship with your baby
  • Avoiding leaving the house or situations that involve your body being seen
  • Finding that feelings about your body are significantly affecting your mood or your relationship with your baby

Rebuilding a kinder relationship with your body

The aim, for most people, is not to return to a previous body. It is to find a way to inhabit the body you have now with more ease and less judgement. Some things that genuinely help:

  • Wearing clothes that fit your current body comfortably, rather than holding out for clothes that fit a previous version of you
  • Moving in ways that feel good rather than ways that punish or push, especially in the early months
  • Getting physiotherapy support for specific physical issues, because being given guidance often reduces anxiety
  • Speaking honestly with other parents about the reality of postpartum bodies, because isolation around this makes it worse
  • Seeking therapy if body image concerns are significantly affecting your mental health

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take for your body to recover after having a baby?

There is no standard timeline, and the idea that your body should "bounce back" within weeks is not grounded in reality for most people. Significant internal healing takes at minimum six to eight weeks, and many physical changes, including abdominal muscles, pelvic floor, and hormonal balance, can take many months or longer to stabilise.

What is diastasis recti and will it change how my stomach looks?

Diastasis recti is a separation of the two rectus abdominis muscles that run down the front of your abdomen. It is very common during and after pregnancy and can make the abdomen look soft or protrude slightly, particularly when straining. A physiotherapist with pelvic health experience can assess it and guide your recovery.

Why do I still look pregnant months after having my baby?

The uterus takes time to return to its pre-pregnancy size, and the abdominal muscles that stretched over nine months do not spring back overnight. Fluid retention, diastasis recti, and skin that has been stretched all contribute. For many people, the abdomen changes significantly over the first year but rarely returns to exactly how it was before.

When does body dissatisfaction become a problem that needs help?

If thoughts about your body are taking up significant mental energy each day, affecting your ability to be present, leading to restriction of food or excessive exercise, or contributing to low mood or anxiety, that is worth discussing with your GP. Body image difficulties after birth can be part of a broader perinatal mental health picture.

How can I feel better about my body after having a baby?

Shifting focus from how your body looks to what it does and what it has done can help, though it takes practice. Engaging with your pelvic floor and core recovery through a physiotherapist often builds a more functional relationship with your body than focusing on appearance. Being selective about social media content is also genuinely useful.

Does breastfeeding affect how your body looks and feels?

Yes. Breastfeeding delays the return of menstruation for many women, affects breast size and shape, and keeps prolactin and oxytocin levels elevated. Some women find breastfeeding affects body image positively (a sense of function and purpose) and some find it difficult (changes to breasts, feeling of not owning their body). All of these responses are valid.
