Your baby at 5 months: development, play and what to expect

Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Month five is a wonderfully active time. Your baby is no longer the sleepy newborn who arrived not so long ago. They are rolling, laughing, babbling and reaching with real purpose. The pace of change right now can feel almost dizzying, and every week brings something new to notice and celebrate.

This guide covers what most babies are doing around the 5-month mark across motor, social, language and sensory development. Remember that developmental ranges are wide, and a baby who reaches a particular milestone a few weeks earlier or later than described here is very often perfectly healthy. These descriptions are a general guide, not a checklist that every baby must tick off on a specific day.

Motor development: rolling and building strength

Physical development at 5 months is all about building on the rolling and strength gains that began in the previous weeks.

  • Rolling both ways. Many babies master rolling from tummy to back first, then back to tummy. By 5 months a good number are rolling in both directions. Some babies skip one direction temporarily or discover both within days of each other. Once rolling begins, never leave your baby unattended on a raised surface.
  • Pushing up on forearms during tummy time. With consistent tummy time practice, most 5-month-olds can push their chest up off the floor onto their forearms comfortably. Some are already pushing up onto straighter arms, getting a broader view of the world.
  • Sitting with support. Your baby cannot sit independently yet, but propped against a cushion or held in a supported seat they can maintain an upright position for short stretches, which is great for their awareness of the world around them.

Tummy time remains the single best thing you can do for your baby's motor development right now. Short, frequent sessions throughout the day are more manageable than one long one, especially if your baby still protests a little.

Social and emotional development: a recognisable person

Your baby is becoming a more unmistakably social creature with every passing week.

  • Recognising your voice. By 5 months most babies will turn reliably toward the sound of a familiar voice even before they can see the face behind it. Your voice is a source of genuine comfort and excitement.
  • Social smiling is well established. The first social smiles usually appear around 6 to 8 weeks, and by 5 months they are part of everyday life. Your baby smiles to greet you, in response to being spoken to, and sometimes just because life is good.
  • Beginning to recognise emotions. Research suggests that by around 5 months babies are sensitive to the emotional tone of the voices and faces around them. A calm, warm presence genuinely supports their developing sense of security.

Language and communication: the babble is beginning

At 5 months your baby is moving from simple cooing and vowel sounds toward early consonant experimentation.

  • Babbling is starting. You may hear your baby beginning to string sounds together, experimenting with consonants like "b", "d" and "m". True babbling with repeated syllables typically emerges in the weeks ahead, but its early signs often show up around now.
  • Pausing and listening. One of the most charming things about this age is that babies start to leave gaps in their vocalisations, almost as if they are waiting for a response. These little proto-conversations are a key foundation for real language.
  • Responding to their name. Some 5-month-olds are just beginning to turn toward the sound of their own name. This milestone is more reliably established by 6 to 9 months, but early signs can appear now.

The best thing you can do for language development is to talk and sing. Narrate your day, describe what you are doing, sing the same songs repeatedly. Repetition is how babies begin to make sense of words.

Sensory and cognitive development: a curious explorer

Your baby's brain is making rapid connections, and they are increasingly curious about the world around them.

  • Tracking objects accurately. A 5-month-old can follow a moving object smoothly with their eyes and head. They can track it across their full field of vision, not just in a narrow arc.
  • Reaching accurately. Early reaching was a bit hit and miss. By now most babies can direct their reach toward an object they want with much greater precision, and they are delighted when they succeed.
  • Mouthing everything. The mouth is still a primary information-gathering tool. Your baby is learning about the texture, temperature and shape of objects through their mouth, so safe, age-appropriate objects to explore this way are ideal.
  • Showing interest in their own reflection. A baby-safe mirror is a wonderful toy at this age. Most 5-month-olds are fascinated by the face looking back at them, though they do not yet understand it is themselves.

Sleep: navigating the aftermath of the 4-month change

Sleep can be a mixed picture at 5 months. The so-called 4-month sleep regression is not really a regression but a permanent shift in how your baby's sleep cycles are structured, moving toward more adult-like cycles with lighter sleep stages.

  • Nap consolidation. Many babies are beginning to settle into 2 to 3 longer naps rather than frequent catnaps. This process takes time and is rarely linear.
  • Night waking is still normal. Most 5-month-olds still wake at least once overnight, and many wake more frequently. This is biologically normal at this age. If sleep has been difficult since the 4-month change, know that things do settle for most families over the coming weeks.
  • Consistent routines help. A calm, predictable bedtime sequence of bath, feed and a song or two gives your baby reliable cues that sleep is coming, which many families find reduces the fuss around settling.

Feeding: still all about milk

At 5 months your baby is getting everything they need from breast milk or formula, and this remains the case until around 6 months.

  • No need to rush solids. Current guidance from the WHO, NHS and AAP consistently recommends waiting until around 6 months before introducing solid foods. Starting before 4 months is not recommended.
  • Signs of interest may appear. You might notice your baby watching you eat with intense concentration, or reaching toward your food. This interest in what you are doing is normal but does not indicate that your baby is ready for solids.
  • Readiness signs to watch for later. True readiness for solids includes being able to sit with minimal support, having lost the tongue-thrust reflex, and showing interest in food. Most babies show these signs closer to 6 months.

Play ideas for a 5-month-old

Play at this age does not require much equipment. Your face, your voice and a few simple objects are all you need.

  • Tummy time games. Lie on your tummy facing your baby during tummy time and have a conversation. Place a toy just out of reach to encourage them to push up or pivot.
  • Sound and texture exploration. Offer objects with different textures, such as a soft cloth, a smooth plastic ring or a crinkle toy. Let your baby explore them with their hands and mouth under supervision.
  • Face-to-face chat. Sit your baby in a supported position facing you and just talk. Respond to their sounds as if you are having a real conversation. These interactions are incredibly valuable for brain development.
  • Mirror play. Hold a baby-safe mirror in front of your baby or prop it where they can see it during tummy time. Watch them react to their own face with delight.
  • Singing and rhymes. Simple repetitive songs with actions like "Row, Row, Row Your Boat" or "Pat-a-Cake" are genuinely engaging for 5-month-olds and support language development at the same time.

When to talk to your health visitor or GP

Developmental ranges are wide, and most differences in timing are nothing to worry about. That said, it is always worth mentioning to your health visitor or GP if your baby:

  • Is not making any sounds or cooing
  • Does not smile or show any social response to familiar people
  • Cannot hold their head steady
  • Is not reaching for or grasping objects
  • Seems unusually stiff or floppy in their limbs

Early referral and support, when needed, makes a meaningful difference. Trust your instincts. If something feels off to you, it is always worth raising it.

Frequently asked questions

Should my 5-month-old be rolling both ways?

Many babies begin rolling from back to tummy around 4 to 5 months, having already mastered the tummy-to-back direction a little earlier. Some babies roll both ways by 5 months; others are still working on one direction. Both are normal at this age.

When does babbling start?

Early vocalisations and cooing begin in the first few months, but babbling with consonant sounds typically emerges between 4 and 6 months. At 5 months you may hear your baby experimenting with sounds and pausing as if waiting for you to reply.

Is the 4-month sleep regression still affecting my baby at 5 months?

Yes, it can be. The 4-month regression is tied to a permanent change in sleep cycles rather than a temporary phase, so its effects can linger into 5 months. Most families find that consistent bedtime routines help sleep gradually settle again.

Can I start solid foods at 5 months?

Most health guidelines recommend waiting until around 6 months before introducing solid foods. At 5 months your baby is still thriving on breast milk or formula. You may notice signs of interest in food, but that does not necessarily mean your baby is ready for solids.

How much tummy time should a 5-month-old have?

Aim for around 30 minutes of tummy time spread across the day. Short sessions of a few minutes each work well. Tummy time while your baby is awake and supervised helps build the strength needed for rolling, sitting and eventually crawling.

When should I speak to a doctor about my baby's development?

If your baby is not making any sounds, not smiling socially, not reaching for objects or not holding their head steady by 5 months, mention it to your health visitor or GP. Early support makes a real difference, so it is always worth raising concerns promptly.
