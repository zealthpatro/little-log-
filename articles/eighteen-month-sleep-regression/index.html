18-month sleep regression: why your toddler has stopped sleeping and what helps

18 months · Sleep · Updated July 2026 · All articles

If your toddler was sleeping reasonably well and has suddenly started fighting bedtime, waking at night or refusing their nap, you have almost certainly hit the 18-month sleep regression. It is one of the most disruptive regressions parents experience, partly because it arrives just when you thought you had this sleep thing figured out. Understanding what is driving it, and knowing that it does pass, makes a real difference to how you get through it.

Why the 18-month regression happens

The 18-month sleep regression is not one single thing. It is the result of several genuine developmental changes happening at the same time, all of which affect sleep.

A language explosion. Between 16 and 24 months most toddlers go through a significant burst of language acquisition. They are processing and storing huge amounts of new vocabulary and syntax, often absorbing many new words a week. This level of brain activity makes it harder to settle, lighter to sleep and more prone to waking.

A surge in independence. Around 18 months toddlers become acutely aware of themselves as separate people with their own preferences and will. This is an important and healthy developmental milestone, but it means they now actively resist things they do not want, including going to bed. The push-back is real and it is normal.

Molars. The first molars tend to emerge between 12 and 16 months, and the second molars come through between 20 and 30 months. If your toddler is near either of these windows, teething pain is likely contributing to the disruption. Molars are much larger than earlier teeth and can cause significant discomfort, particularly at night when there are fewer distractions.

Separation anxiety at its peak. Separation anxiety runs high throughout the first two years of life and tends to peak again around 18 months. Your toddler understands far more than they did at six months but still cannot hold the concept that you will return reliably when you leave. Bedtime asks them to do exactly that, which is why protests can feel so intense right now.

How it is different from the 12-month regression

The 12-month regression is often marked by unsettled nights and a transition in nap patterns, but toddlers at that age have limited tools to protest with. By 18 months the picture is more complicated because your toddler has developed in several important ways.

They now have words, or at least sounds and gestures with clear communicative intent. "No", "Mama", "again" and pointing at the door give them real protest vocabulary that they will use at bedtime. The 12-month-old cried; the 18-month-old argues.

Many toddlers at this age can also climb. If your toddler has not yet figured out how to get out of their cot, the regression is a common trigger for the first escape attempt. Suddenly settling them down is not just emotionally difficult but physically complicated.

They also have a much stronger understanding of cause and effect than they did six months ago. They know that if they call out, you come. They know that if they stand up in the cot, you come back into the room. This is clever, not naughty, but it does mean the 18-month regression can involve more deliberate, persistent testing than earlier regressions.

How long it lasts

The honest answer is that it varies. Most toddlers come through the 18-month regression in two to six weeks. For some children, particularly those who sail through developmental changes without much disruption, it might feel like barely two weeks of unsettled nights before things settle again. For others, especially if several contributing factors land at once (molars, a new nursery start, a sibling, a language burst), it can stretch closer to six weeks.

If sleep is still significantly disrupted after six to eight weeks, it is worth pausing to think about whether there is something else going on, or whether a sleep association that formed during the regression is now the main driver. The regression itself is temporary, but habits that develop during it can outlast it.

It does not mean anything is wrong if your toddler takes the full six weeks or slightly longer. Every child moves through developmental changes at their own pace.

What the 18-month regression looks like

The regression does not look the same in every child, and you may recognise only some of these patterns. Common signs include:

  • Bedtime battles. A toddler who previously settled fairly easily now refuses to lie down, cries when you leave the room, climbs out of the cot or calls for you repeatedly after you have left.
  • Night waking. Waking once or several times in the night, where previously they slept through or woke minimally.
  • Early rising. Some toddlers start waking significantly earlier than usual, often between four and six in the morning, and cannot settle back to sleep.
  • Nap resistance. Fighting the daytime nap, taking much longer to fall asleep for it, or waking from it early.
  • Wanting a parent in the room. Your toddler may be happy to go to bed but cannot settle unless you are visible. This is separation anxiety showing up in a very specific way.
  • Increased clinginess in the day. The separation anxiety that drives bedtime protests tends to show up during the day too, particularly at drop-off or when you move out of sight.

You do not need to see all of these to be in the regression. If your toddler is showing two or three of them and is between 16 and 22 months old, the regression is the most likely explanation.

The one-nap question

By 18 months, most toddlers have already transitioned to one nap a day, usually somewhere between 12 and 18 months. If your toddler is still on two naps as the regression hits, this is often a signal that the transition to one nap is ready to happen. The regression can make a two-nap schedule feel suddenly impossible to maintain, which is your toddler's way of showing they no longer need it.

If your toddler is already on one nap and that nap suddenly shortens, disappears for a few days or becomes a battle, this is completely common during the regression. It does not mean they are ready to drop the nap altogether. Most toddlers need one nap until somewhere between two and three years. The nap disruption during the regression is temporary, and it usually returns to its previous pattern once the developmental burst settles.

The key thing to watch is total sleep across the day and night. At 18 months most toddlers need around 13 to 14 hours in total. If the nap shortens significantly during the regression, a slightly earlier bedtime can help make up some of that lost sleep and prevent overtiredness from making nights worse.

What helps during the regression

The most important thing to know is that consistency helps far more than novelty during a regression. This is not the time to try a completely new approach or to start buying lots of new sleep aids in the hope that something will fix it. Familiarity and predictability are what toddlers need when they are in the middle of a developmental disruption.

Keep the bedtime routine the same. Whatever your routine is, keep doing it in the same order every night. Bath, pyjamas, a couple of books, a feed if you are still breastfeeding, lights out. Predictable sequences act as a strong signal that sleep is coming, and toddlers find the routine itself calming even if they protest the end of it.

Expect a slightly later bedtime for now. During regressions, the biological pressure to sleep can shift. Trying to put a fully alert 18-month-old to bed at 6:30pm when they are not remotely ready can turn bedtime into a prolonged battle. A temporary shift of 15 to 30 minutes later, bringing them to bed when they are genuinely tired rather than before they are ready, often makes settling much easier.

Check wake windows. At 18 months most toddlers do best with a wake window of around five to six hours before bedtime. If the nap ended at 2pm, a 7pm to 7:30pm bedtime gives roughly the right amount of awake time. Getting this window right, and not letting it stretch too long, reduces overtiredness and makes settling easier.

Do not introduce new sleep associations. It is tempting, when your toddler is distressed, to start feeding to sleep, bringing them into your bed or lying with them until they are fully asleep. There is nothing wrong with any of these things in principle, but if you start them now and your toddler comes to rely on them, you will still be doing them in two months when the regression has long passed. If you are happy to continue a habit indefinitely, use it. If you would rather not, try to avoid starting it.

Be consistent in your response to night waking. A brief, low-key response that reassures your toddler without fully waking them or creating high stimulation is usually most effective. The same response every time, offered calmly, helps your toddler understand what to expect from the night.

Night waking at 18 months

If your toddler is waking at night during the regression, the most important thing you can do is respond calmly and consistently without introducing habits you do not want to keep long term.

A brief check-in, a quiet "I am here, you are okay, time to sleep," offered with minimal light and low stimulation, is usually enough. You do not need to pick them up if they are not distressed, and you do not need to stay until they are fully asleep if that is not something you want to continue.

Night waking during the regression is not a sign that something has gone wrong with your parenting or your toddler's sleep development. It is a temporary disruption driven by genuine developmental change. Most toddlers who were previously settling well return to that pattern within a few weeks without any formal intervention, as long as the routine and response stay consistent.

It is also worth remembering that separation anxiety is at its height right now. Your toddler is not waking to manipulate you. They genuinely find being alone at night harder than they did a few months ago, and they are seeking reassurance. Offering that reassurance briefly and calmly is the right thing to do.

When to reach out

The 18-month regression is common, normal and temporary. But there are some situations where it is worth talking to your health visitor or GP rather than waiting it out.

  • Sleep is significantly disrupted for more than six to eight weeks. If nights are still very broken after this point, or if the disruption is having a serious impact on your family, a conversation with your health visitor is a good next step. They can help you work out whether the regression is the whole story or whether something else is contributing.
  • Your toddler seems unwell or in pain. If they are pulling at their ears, have a temperature, are unusually unsettled in the day as well as the night, or you are concerned about molar pain, it is worth a check-up.
  • Your toddler is snoring loudly every night. Persistent loud snoring in a toddler can be a sign of obstructive sleep apnoea, which is significantly under-diagnosed in young children and does need assessment. Raise it with your GP.
  • You are struggling. Sleep deprivation has real consequences for parents as well as children. If you are not coping, please ask for help. Your health visitor is there for exactly this, and reaching out is not a sign of failure.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 18-month sleep regression real?

Yes. The 18-month regression is a recognised and well-documented period of disrupted sleep driven by genuine developmental changes: a language explosion, a surge in independence and self-assertion, molar teething, and peak separation anxiety. These are real neurological and physical events, not a parenting problem, and they temporarily disrupt sleep that had previously been settled.

Why is my 18-month-old suddenly fighting bedtime?

Bedtime battles at this age usually come from a combination of factors. Your toddler is asserting their independence and now has the language and understanding to actively refuse. They also know from experience that you come back when they call, which makes them more likely to protest. Separation anxiety peaks around 18 months, so they genuinely find it hard when you leave. On top of that, molars can cause real discomfort. Keeping a consistent, predictable routine helps enormously, even when it feels like a losing battle.

How long does the 18-month regression last?

For most toddlers, two to six weeks. Some children sail through in a couple of weeks with only mild disruption. For others, especially if several things are happening at once, it can stretch to six weeks or slightly beyond. If sleep is still significantly disrupted after six to eight weeks, it is worth speaking to your health visitor.

Should I sleep train during the regression?

Most sleep experts suggest waiting until the regression has passed. Trying to change sleep habits during a period of peak developmental disruption is harder for everyone. That said, the regression is a good time to lay foundations: keep the bedtime routine consistent and avoid introducing any new sleep associations you would not want to continue. Once the regression lifts, those foundations make any further changes much easier.

My toddler was sleeping through and now wakes every night. Is this normal?

Yes, this is one of the most common patterns parents describe during the 18-month regression. A toddler who had been sleeping through reliably can suddenly start waking once or several times a night. This is a typical feature of the regression rather than a sign that something is wrong. Responding calmly and consistently, without introducing new habits you do not want to keep, is usually enough. Most toddlers return to their previous pattern within a few weeks.

When does the 18-month sleep regression end?

There is no fixed date, but most toddlers come through within two to six weeks. The clearest sign it is lifting is that your toddler starts settling more easily at bedtime again and night waking reduces without you having changed anything. If you have kept a consistent routine throughout, sleep usually returns to its previous pattern fairly quickly once the developmental burst settles down.
