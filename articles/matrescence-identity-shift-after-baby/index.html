Matrescence: the identity shift that comes with becoming a mother

Mental health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

If you became a mother and felt, somewhere beneath the love and the busyness, as though you were not quite sure who you were anymore, you were not losing your mind. You were going through matrescence, and it is one of the most significant, least talked-about transitions in a person's life.

The word that names it

Matrescence was coined by American anthropologist Dana Raphael in the 1970s. She used it to describe the process of becoming a mother, drawing a deliberate parallel with adolescence. Both are periods of profound physical, emotional, psychological, and social transformation. Both are marked by ambivalence and disorientation. And in both cases, how you come out the other side depends significantly on the support and understanding around you.

The term fell largely out of use for decades and has been revived more recently, partly through the work of reproductive psychiatrist Alexandra Sacks, whose 2018 TED talk brought it to a much wider audience. Having a name for this experience matters, because it tells people that what they are going through is real, recognised, and part of a larger human story.

What actually changes

When a baby arrives, the change is not just practical. Your sense of who you are, what matters, what your relationships mean, and where you stand in the world all shift. You may find that your relationships change, sometimes in ways you did not expect. Your relationship with your own mother might become more complex. Friendships that were easy before can start to feel strained, while connections with other parents deepen in ways that surprise you.

Your identity, which was probably built over decades around work, relationships, interests, and how you show up in the world, is suddenly being reorganised around a tiny person who needs everything from you. That reorganisation is not always smooth, and it does not always feel like what you were told becoming a mother would feel like.

The ambivalence nobody talks about

Here is the thing that tends not to make it into the baby shower cards: becoming a mother involves a kind of loss, even when it also involves enormous love. Loss of the self you were. Loss of certain freedoms. Loss of relationships that do not survive the shift. Loss of the future you had imagined for yourself before this one.

That ambivalence, holding love for your child and grief for other parts of your life at the same time, does not make you a bad mother. It makes you human. Research consistently finds that a majority of new mothers experience significant ambivalence that they are too afraid to name aloud, partly because of the enormous social pressure to perform uncomplicated joy.

The silence around that ambivalence is one of the things that makes early motherhood lonelier than it needs to be. You can be devoted to your baby and also be grieving other things. Those two things are not in competition.

How matrescence can overlap with postnatal depression

Matrescence is not a mental health condition. It is a developmental transition. But because it is disorienting and often painful, it can be hard to know whether what you are experiencing is a normal part of the transition or whether it has tipped into postnatal depression or anxiety.

A useful rough distinction: if the difficulty is mostly about identity, meaning, and the complexity of transformation, that sounds more like matrescence. If it involves persistent low mood, loss of interest in things beyond parenting, significant anxiety, or feelings of worthlessness, that warrants a conversation with a GP or health visitor.

The two can absolutely coexist. Going through matrescence and developing postnatal depression are not mutually exclusive, and support is available for both.

Changed relationships and social loss

One aspect of matrescence that catches many people off guard is how much their social world shifts. Some friendships quietly fade because the shared context that sustained them no longer exists. Family relationships can become more loaded. The couple relationship changes as two people navigate a profound new responsibility together, not always in sync.

New parents are often encouraged to build community with other parents, and that is genuinely good advice. But it can also feel strange to suddenly be seeking connection primarily on the basis of a shared life stage rather than shared interests or history. All of this is part of the territory.

What helps during matrescence

The single most helpful thing for many people is simply having a name for what they are going through and having it recognised as real. If you are reading this and feeling seen, that is a start.

Beyond that, several things can genuinely help:

  • Community with other mothers who are willing to speak honestly about the difficult parts, not just the highlights
  • Space to name the ambivalence without being reassured out of it
  • A therapist who understands perinatal mental health and is familiar with matrescence, someone who will not pathologise a developmental process but who can support you through it
  • Permission from yourself to grieve what has changed, alongside the love you feel for your child
  • Time. This is a process and it unfolds at its own pace

Matrescence as a process, not a problem to solve

There is no endpoint to matrescence in the way there is an endpoint to, say, recovering from an illness. It is more like adolescence: you move through it, you come out changed, and some of the work continues quietly for years. The identity you build as a mother is added to who you were before, and integrating those two things takes time and often some dedicated reflection.

If you are in the thick of it right now, please know that the disorientation does not last forever. Many mothers describe eventually arriving at a sense of themselves that feels richer, more grounded, and more hard-won than the self they were before. That destination is not guaranteed, but it is genuinely within reach, and you do not have to get there alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is matrescence?

Matrescence is the developmental process of becoming a mother. The term was coined by anthropologist Dana Raphael in the 1970s. It describes the physical, psychological, emotional, and social transition that happens when a woman becomes a mother, and it can be as profound and disorienting as adolescence.

Is it normal to grieve your pre-baby self?

Yes, and it is far more common than people let on. Feeling grief for the freedom, the identity, the relationships, or the version of yourself that existed before your baby does not mean you love your child less. It means you were a whole person before, and becoming a mother changes that person significantly.

Does matrescence happen with each baby?

There is a significant shift with a first baby, but subsequent babies can also trigger a version of it, particularly if they significantly change the family structure or arrive in a very different life context. The experience is not identical each time.

Is matrescence a mental health condition?

No. Matrescence is a developmental transition, not a disorder. However, it can overlap with postnatal depression or anxiety if the difficulty of the transition is significant. If you are experiencing persistent low mood, significant anxiety, or difficulty functioning, please speak to your GP or health visitor.

How long does matrescence last?

There is no defined endpoint. Some of the most intense disorientation tends to ease in the first year or two, but the identity work of motherhood is ongoing. Many women describe still moving through aspects of it years later. It is a process rather than a phase with a fixed finish line.

What helps during matrescence?

Simply having a name for the experience can be enormously helpful. Beyond that, community with other mothers who can name the complexity honestly, a therapist who understands matrescence, and permission to speak about the harder parts of becoming a mother all make a real difference. You are not supposed to find this easy.
