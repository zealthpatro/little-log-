Birth choices in Australia: public, private and birth centres

Pregnancy · 3rd trimester · Reviewed 15 June 2026 · All articles

One of the most significant decisions you will make in the third trimester is where and how you want to give birth. Australia offers several distinct settings, each with its own model of care, associated costs, and eligibility criteria. Understanding what each option involves gives you the best foundation for a conversation with your midwife or obstetrician so that the choice you make reflects both your preferences and your clinical situation.

Public hospital birth

The vast majority of births in Australia take place in public hospitals, and for good reason. Public hospital maternity care is free under Medicare, with no out-of-pocket costs for the birth itself. These hospitals are staffed by midwives and obstetricians around the clock and are equipped to manage all levels of risk, from straightforward vaginal births to complex surgical deliveries. Access to an operating theatre, anaesthetists, and a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) within the same building means that if circumstances change during labour, the team and the equipment needed are immediately at hand.

In larger public hospitals, the model of care you receive is not necessarily one-size-fits-all. Depending on the hospital and your circumstances, you may be offered standard shared midwifery care, access to a midwifery group practice (MGP), or referral to an obstetric team for more closely supervised antenatal appointments. The trade-off with standard public care is that you are less likely to know the midwife who attends your birth, since staff rotate across shifts. Many people find this perfectly fine, while others place significant value on continuity and pursue a model that offers it.

Private hospital birth

Giving birth in a private hospital requires two things: private health insurance that includes obstetric hospital cover, and a relationship with a private obstetrician whom you have chosen and engaged directly. Even with insurance in place, a gap fee is generally payable, covering the difference between what your obstetrician charges and what Medicare and your insurer will cover combined. These fees vary considerably between practitioners, so it is worth asking for a detailed estimate early in your pregnancy.

The main advantages of private hospital birth are continuity and choice. Your obstetrician will attend your birth personally and will have been involved throughout your antenatal care, meaning they know your history and your preferences in detail. You will typically have a private room for your stay. The physical environment in many private hospitals is also noticeably more hotel-like than in public facilities, though this varies significantly between hospitals. The clinical care available, including theatre access, is broadly equivalent to a well-resourced public hospital.

It is worth noting that the high quality of care in public maternity services means that for low-risk pregnancies, the clinical outcomes in public and private settings are very similar. The differences are largely about continuity, accommodation, and the relationship with your care provider rather than clinical superiority of one over the other.

Birth centres

Birth centres are designed to provide a homelike environment while keeping maternity care within or close to a hospital system. They are run by midwives and are specifically suited to low-risk pregnancies. The rooms are typically larger and less clinical than a standard labour ward, often furnished with a double bed and decorated to feel less institutional. Birthing pools are usually available and water birth is commonly offered. Partners are generally welcomed to stay overnight.

There are two types of birth centre to be aware of. An alongside birth centre sits within or directly connected to a hospital building, which means transfer to the labour ward, operating theatre, or NICU can happen quickly if needed. A freestanding birth centre is physically separate from a hospital, which means transfer in an emergency takes longer. Both types typically require that you meet low-risk criteria to be admitted. These criteria may include factors such as a single pregnancy, a cephalic (head-down) presentation, no significant medical complications, and a pregnancy that has progressed normally.

One important point for people considering a birth centre: epidurals are not available in most birth centres. If you want an epidural or decide during labour that you would like one, you will need to transfer to the labour ward. This is not a failure or an emergency, and transfer for pain relief is a routine part of how birth centre care works. Many people find that the lower-intervention environment of a birth centre, combined with the birthing pool and one-to-one midwifery support, means they manage very well without an epidural. However, if having definite access to epidural analgesia is a priority for you, a birth centre may not be the right fit.

Planned home birth

Planned home birth in Australia is attended by a registered midwife and is an option for women with low-risk pregnancies. Publicly funded home birth programs exist in some states and territories, meaning that in certain parts of the country it is possible to access this model of care at no cost. In other areas, privately engaged independent midwives may be the primary route to a planned home birth, and fees apply.

The evidence on planned home birth is nuanced and should be discussed with a midwife or obstetrician who can relate it to your individual situation. RANZCOG recommends that all women considering home birth have a thorough conversation about the specific risks and benefits given their own clinical history. Transfer to hospital is sometimes required during planned home births; available data suggest this occurs in roughly 10 to 15 per cent of cases, and most transfers are non-urgent rather than emergencies. Having a clear transfer plan agreed in advance with your midwife, and knowing the route to your nearest hospital, is an important part of preparing for a home birth.

Home birth is not suitable if you have risk factors that require close monitoring or immediate access to obstetric intervention. Eligibility criteria are taken seriously by registered midwives, whose professional registration and insurance obligations require them to practise within defined parameters. If your pregnancy develops complications, your midwife will discuss with you whether home birth remains appropriate.

Caesarean section

Caesarean birth, whether planned (elective) or unplanned (emergency), is available in all hospital settings throughout Australia. According to Australian Institute of Health and Welfare data, caesarean sections account for around 36 to 38 per cent of births nationally. Both elective and emergency caesareans are performed routinely and safely across public and private hospitals. If you are planning a vaginal birth, understanding the circumstances in which a caesarean might become necessary helps you feel more informed if the recommendation arises during labour. If you are planning an elective caesarean, your obstetrician will walk you through the procedure, recovery, and what to expect in the days that follow.

Midwifery group practice and caseload midwifery

Midwifery group practice (sometimes called caseload midwifery) is a model of care that deserves particular attention because it bridges the gap between standard public care and the continuity you get in the private system. In a midwifery group practice, you are assigned a primary midwife and a small team of backup midwives. This team provides your antenatal appointments, attends your birth, and follows up with you postnatally. Because you are seeing the same small group of people throughout, they know your history, your birth preferences, and your support network in detail.

Research consistently shows that this continuity of carer model is associated with better birth outcomes and higher satisfaction for both mothers and babies. MGP is available in most large public hospitals in Australia and carries no additional cost compared to standard public care. The main practical limitation is that demand exceeds supply in many hospitals, and waiting lists to access MGP can be long. Applying early in your pregnancy, ideally at your booking appointment, gives you the best chance of securing a place.

How to choose

The right birth setting depends on a combination of clinical factors, personal priorities, and practical logistics. Your eligibility for certain options, particularly birth centres and home birth, will depend on your risk classification, which is assessed by your midwife or obstetrician based on your medical history and how your pregnancy progresses. Some options that seem attractive early in pregnancy may become unavailable if your circumstances change.

Questions worth thinking through as you make this decision include: How important is continuity of carer to me? What is my preference around pain relief, and do I want to keep all options open? What does the process of transferring look like if I choose a birth centre or home birth? Is private health insurance already in place, and have I been waiting the required number of months for obstetric cover? What are the distances involved if I need urgent transfer?

None of these questions has a universally right answer. The best approach is to use your antenatal appointments to ask specifically about what is available at your hospital or in your area, and to make the decision with your actual clinical picture in mind rather than on the basis of what worked for a friend or family member whose circumstances may have been quite different.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a birth centre and a hospital birth?

A birth centre offers a homelike environment with midwife-led care, usually including a birthing pool. It is suitable for low-risk pregnancies. An alongside birth centre is attached to a hospital and can transfer quickly if needed. A hospital birth offers the full range of interventions including epidural, theatre and NICU.

Do I need private health insurance to give birth in a private hospital?

Yes. Private hospital births require obstetric-level private health insurance, and even then a gap fee is usually payable. Without insurance, a public hospital birth is free. The care provided in public hospitals for low-risk pregnancies is of very high quality.

Is home birth safe in Australia?

For low-risk women who have given birth before, planned home birth attended by a registered midwife has outcomes that are broadly comparable to birth centre birth. For first-time mothers, the evidence suggests slightly higher risk. RANZCOG recommends discussing your specific situation with a midwife or obstetrician.

What is caseload midwifery or midwifery group practice?

In a midwifery group practice (MGP), you are assigned a primary midwife and a small backup team who provide all your antenatal, birth and postnatal care. Seeing the same familiar face throughout is associated with better birth outcomes and higher satisfaction. MGP is available in most large public hospitals in Australia but waiting lists can be long.
