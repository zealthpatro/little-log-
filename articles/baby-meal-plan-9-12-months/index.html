Baby meal plan at nine to 12 months: textures, portions and what to include

9 to 12 months · Feeding · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Somewhere around the nine-month mark, feeding shifts gear. Your baby is no longer a beginner tasting thin purees for the first time. They have opinions. They want to grab things. They are watching you eat and reaching for your plate. This is exactly the right instinct, and it is telling you they are ready for the next stage: thicker textures, more variety, and a proper three-meal-a-day rhythm. This guide covers what to feed, how much to offer, and the nutrients to keep a close eye on through to 12 months.

Where babies are at nine to 12 months with food

By nine months, most babies are settled into three meals a day, with one or two small snacks fitting in between if needed. Milk, whether breastmilk or formula, remains a genuinely important source of nutrition at this stage and should continue alongside solid meals. You are not replacing milk yet — you are building on top of it.

Most babies at nine months have some teeth, but teeth are not actually required for the soft, well-cooked foods appropriate at this stage. What is changing rapidly is the fine motor control in their hands. The pincer grip, picking up small pieces between the index finger and thumb, typically develops between nine and twelve months, and it transforms what babies can manage independently. You may find your baby suddenly able to pick up small pieces of food that they would have batted away a few weeks earlier.

Appetite will vary considerably from day to day and week to week. Growth happens in bursts, and so does hunger. Try not to read too much into a single quiet day at the table.

Texture at this stage

The goal between nine and 12 months is a gradual move toward the textures the rest of the family eats, adapted as needed. NHS Start4Life guidance describes this stage as moving from smooth purees toward mashed, lumpy, and minced food, with soft finger foods alongside.

In practice this means:

  • Well-mashed or soft-cooked vegetables rather than blended smooth
  • Soft pasta, rice, and grains in their normal form rather than pureed
  • Soft finger foods your baby can pick up and hold: ripe banana, soft-cooked broccoli florets, toast strips, soft-cooked carrot sticks
  • Minced or finely chopped meat rather than pureed

You do not need to be precise about this. Follow your baby's lead and what they can manage. Some babies move through textures quickly; others prefer a slower progression. Both are fine. The important thing is not to stay on smooth purees until 12 months, as babies who miss the lumpy texture window can sometimes find it harder to accept textures later.

Always avoid hard, round foods that present a choking risk: whole grapes, whole cherry tomatoes, whole berries, raw carrot sticks, whole cherry tomatoes, large chunks of apple. Cut everything into pieces no larger than your baby's thumbnail.

A sample daily structure

This is not a prescription. Every family's rhythm is different, and every baby's appetite varies. Think of this as a starting framework to adapt.

Breakfast: Soft porridge with mashed banana or stewed fruit, or scrambled eggs with toast soldiers, or soft weetabix with full-fat milk. A breastfeed or formula feed can come before or after, depending on your routine.

Mid-morning: An optional milk feed at this stage is normal. A small snack of soft fruit pieces or rice cakes works well if your baby is hungry before lunch.

Lunch: Soft pasta with a vegetable sauce, or lentil dhal with soft bread, or a mini cheese and vegetable omelette. Add a small portion of full-fat yoghurt or soft fruit to finish. This is often a good meal for introducing new flavours.

Afternoon: A milk feed. Many babies still have one here at nine months, and that is completely normal.

Dinner: Something from the family meal, adapted: soft fish with mashed potato and peas, mini meatballs in a tomato sauce with pasta, or a vegetable and lentil soup with bread for dipping. Finish with fruit or yoghurt.

Evening: A breastfeed or formula feed before bed is common and appropriate throughout this stage.

The key is variety across the week, not perfection at every meal. If dinner is rejected on Tuesday, Wednesday is a new day.

Iron at this age

Iron deserves special attention at nine to 12 months. The iron stores babies are born with, built up during pregnancy, begin to deplete around six months. After that point, dietary iron becomes the main source, and it is one of the nutrients most commonly low in babies of this age.

For babies who eat meat, including red meat or poultry several times a week is the most straightforward way to meet iron needs. The iron in meat (haem iron) is absorbed much more efficiently than iron from plant sources.

For vegetarian or vegan babies, you need to be deliberate about iron-rich plant foods: lentils and dal, beans and chickpeas, tofu, fortified breakfast cereals, and dark leafy greens like spinach. The key addition is vitamin C alongside these foods, because vitamin C dramatically improves how much iron the body absorbs from plant sources. Serving lentils with tomato sauce, or spinach with orange segments, makes a real difference to how much iron your baby actually gets from the meal.

Continuing breastmilk or formula provides some iron but not enough to cover all needs at this age. Food is the primary iron source from six months onward.

Allergens at this stage

If you introduced the major allergens during the weaning window between six and nine months, the important next step is to continue including them regularly. Research shows that continuing regular exposure after introduction is what maintains tolerance. If you introduced peanut at six months and then stopped offering it, the protection you built can fade.

The major allergens in the UK are: cow's milk (dairy), eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, wheat, fish, shellfish, sesame, and soy. If you have not yet introduced all of them, nine to 12 months is still a good window to do so, one at a time, watching for any reaction for a couple of days before introducing the next.

If your baby has eczema, a known food allergy, or a family history of severe allergies, speak to your GP or health visitor before introducing allergens, as they may want to guide the process.

How much to offer

Portion size guidance at this stage is a starting point, not a target. A rough guide from NHS Start4Life is two to three tablespoons of food per meal at nine months, increasing gradually. But many babies eat considerably more than this, especially when they are in a growth phase, and some eat less on quieter days.

The most important principle is responsive feeding: offer food, let your baby lead on how much they eat, and try not to encourage them to finish a specific amount. Babies at this age have strong hunger and fullness signals, and respecting those helps build a healthy relationship with food that lasts well beyond infancy.

Gagging is normal at this stage as babies learn to manage new textures. It looks alarming but is the body's safety mechanism working correctly. Choking is different: your baby will be silent and unable to breathe. It is worth doing a basic first aid course that covers infant choking before you get well into weaning, if you have not already.

Foods to still avoid at this age

Some foods remain off the list until 12 months or beyond, regardless of how advanced your baby seems with solids:

  • Honey: Risk of infant botulism. Do not offer honey in any form under 12 months, including in cooking.
  • Whole nuts: Choking hazard. Nut butters, finely ground nuts mixed into food, and smooth nut-based sauces are all fine.
  • Added salt: Babies' kidneys are not mature enough to process salt the way adult kidneys can. Cook family meals without salt and season adult portions separately.
  • Added sugar: Avoid adding sugar to food or drinks. This includes seemingly innocuous additions like a little jam on toast.
  • Low-fat dairy: Babies need the calories and fat-soluble vitamins in full-fat dairy. Full-fat yoghurt, full-fat cheese, and whole milk in cooking are all appropriate.
  • Whole round foods: Always cut grapes, cherry tomatoes, blueberries, and strawberries. Cut round or cylindrical foods like sausages lengthways. Large chunks of apple or raw carrot should be avoided entirely until well into the toddler years.
  • Undercooked eggs: Until 12 months, eggs should be fully cooked. After 12 months, British Lion-stamped eggs can be soft-cooked for babies.

Frequently asked questions

How many meals should a nine-month-old baby have per day?

By nine months, most babies are ready for three meals a day plus one or two small snacks. Milk feeds, whether breastmilk or formula, still sit alongside meals at this stage and remain an important source of nutrition. You are not replacing milk at this point — solid meals and milk feeds work together through the first year.

What textures should a nine-to-12-month-old be eating?

At nine to 12 months, babies are moving away from smooth purees toward well-mashed, lumpy, and soft chopped foods. Soft finger foods are increasingly important as the pincer grip develops. Most things the family eats can be adapted for a baby at this age by soft-cooking or mashing, without adding salt or sugar.

How much should a baby eat at nine months?

Two to three tablespoons per meal is a reasonable starting guide, but many babies eat more. Appetite varies a great deal from day to day at this age, so follow your baby's hunger and fullness cues rather than aiming for a fixed amount at each sitting. If they turn away, close their mouth, or push food away, they are telling you they have had enough.

Do I still need to give formula or breastmilk at this age?

Yes. Milk continues to be an important source of nutrition alongside solids through the first year. Most babies at nine to 12 months still have three to four milk feeds per day. The balance gradually shifts as solid food intake increases, but you do not need to rush reducing milk feeds. Follow your baby's lead.

What iron-rich foods should I include for my baby?

The iron stores babies are born with deplete around six months, making dietary iron critical at this stage. For meat-eating babies, red meat and poultry several times a week is the most efficient source. For vegetarian babies, include lentils, beans, chickpeas, fortified cereals, tofu, and dark leafy greens, and pair them with vitamin C-rich foods to improve absorption.

What foods should I avoid for a baby under 12 months?

Avoid honey (botulism risk), whole nuts (choking hazard), added salt and sugar, low-fat dairy products, and whole round foods like grapes, cherry tomatoes, and whole berries. Always cut round foods into quarters or smaller. Undercooked eggs should also be avoided until 12 months.
