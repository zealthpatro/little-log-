The dream feed: what it is, how to do it and whether it actually works

Sleep · Updated July 2026 · All articles

When you have a newborn and sleep is broken into two-hour fragments, any strategy that might buy you a longer stretch feels worth trying. The dream feed is one of the most widely recommended of these strategies, and for good reason: for many families, it genuinely works. But it also confuses a lot of parents. How can you feed a sleeping baby? Will it disrupt their sleep more than it helps? And when does it stop being useful?

This guide covers the theory behind the dream feed, how to do it without waking your baby fully, what the evidence says about whether it extends sleep, and how to know when to stop. Whether you are deciding whether to try it, troubleshooting a dream feed that has stopped working, or working out how to drop it, this is the information you need.

What is a dream feed

A dream feed is a feeding you offer your baby while they are still drowsy or lightly asleep, typically somewhere between 10pm and 11pm, before you go to bed yourself. The goal is to top up your baby's milk tank at the start of the night so they can sleep for a longer first stretch, giving you a block of uninterrupted sleep before the next waking.

What makes it a "dream" feed is that the baby is never fully woken. You lift them carefully from their sleep surface, support their head, and offer the breast or bottle. Most young babies will root and feed in a semi-conscious state, taking a reasonable feed without ever becoming fully alert. They stay in that drowsy, between-worlds state throughout, and with a careful transfer back to the cot, they often continue sleeping without a full waking in between.

The dream feed is most commonly used in the first four to five months, when babies need frequent night feeds and cannot yet consolidate a long stretch independently. It is a proactive strategy: rather than waiting for your baby to wake and cry at midnight, you go to them at 10pm while you are still awake and functional, get the feed in, and aim to shift the next waking to 2 or 3am rather than 1am.

The theory: why topping up at 10pm might extend the first stretch

The logic behind the dream feed is rooted in how babies regulate hunger and sleep. Young babies have small stomachs and high caloric needs relative to their body weight, which means they need to feed frequently. In the early months, most babies cannot physically go more than three to four hours between feeds without waking from hunger, regardless of how tired they are.

The dream feed works with this biology rather than against it. If a baby last fed at 7pm and falls asleep at 7:30pm, their stomach will be signalling hunger again somewhere around 10 to 11pm. At that point, if no feed happens, they will wake, cry, fully rouse, feed, and then need resettling. If instead a parent offers a feed at 10pm before the hunger fully surfaces, the baby takes that milk without fully waking, and the next hunger signal does not arrive until 1 or 2am. The net result is a longer first stretch for the parents, even if the total number of feeds in the night stays the same.

This is also why timing matters. A dream feed offered at 9pm may be too close to the previous feed and the baby may not take much. One offered at midnight may miss the window where the baby is still in light sleep and rousable enough to feed without fully waking. Most parents find the 10pm to 10:30pm window works best, but there is variation, and it takes a few nights of trial to find the timing that works for your baby.

What the evidence actually says

The evidence base for the dream feed is modest but broadly positive for the early months. Studies looking at infant sleep strategies have found that feeding at the start of the night can shift the timing of subsequent wakings and extend the first sleep stretch. Paediatric sleep researchers generally consider it a reasonable tool for families in the newborn to four-month window.

However, the research also consistently shows that the effect is not universal. A meaningful proportion of babies wake at the same time regardless of whether a dream feed was offered, or wake more frequently after being lifted and disturbed. For these babies, the dream feed is adding a disruption without providing the payoff. This is not a failure of the strategy, it is just normal variation in how babies respond to it.

There is also evidence that the dream feed becomes less effective, and potentially counterproductive, as babies get older and start to consolidate their longest stretch of sleep into the early part of the night naturally. Around four to six months, many babies begin to do this independently. At that point, a dream feed at 10pm can actually interrupt a stretch that was going to run to 2 or 3am on its own. This is why knowing when to stop is as important as knowing how to start.

How to do a dream feed step by step

The goal throughout is to keep stimulation to an absolute minimum. You want the baby to feed and return to sleep without crossing the threshold into full wakefulness. Everything about the process should be slow, quiet, and dim.

Before you go in, make sure the room is dark, that you have everything you need (breast or a prepared bottle, a muslin for wind), and that the cot or bassinet is ready for a quick transfer back. When you are ready, go into the room quietly and lift your baby slowly, supporting their head and body. Do not turn on any lights. A dim red nightlight is fine if you need it to see; bright overhead lights are not.

Offer the breast or teat to your baby's lips without saying anything or making eye contact. Most babies will root and begin feeding. If they do not respond immediately, you can try gently stroking their cheek or lower lip to trigger the rooting reflex. Keep your own body relaxed and still. The energy in the room should stay calm and sleep-adjacent.

After the feed, keep your baby upright for a minute or two to let any wind settle before it causes discomfort. You do not need a full burping session with pats and movement; a gentle, quiet hold is enough. Then transfer them back to the sleep surface slowly, placing their bottom down first and lowering their head last. Keep your hands on them briefly once they are down, then withdraw slowly. Move out of the room without rushing.

Common mistakes that wake the baby fully

The most common mistake is changing the nappy as part of the dream feed routine. Unless a nappy change is genuinely necessary because of discomfort or a full leak, skip it. Laying a baby down, unpopping their sleepsuit, and changing a nappy is enough stimulation to bring many babies to full wakefulness, which defeats the purpose entirely.

Turning on bright lights is the second most common mistake. Light suppresses melatonin and signals to the brain that it is time to wake up. Even a brief exposure to overhead lighting during the dream feed can shift your baby from drowsy to alert. Keep the room dark throughout.

Talking to your baby, making eye contact, or interacting with them socially during the feed is also worth avoiding. It is natural to want to connect when you are holding your baby in a quiet moment, but social engagement is stimulating. Keep the feed calm, quiet, and minimal in interaction. The goal is for your baby to finish the feed and not really know they had it.

Finally, trying to wind too vigorously after the feed can wake a baby who was otherwise going to transfer smoothly. A gentle, unhurried upright hold is usually enough. If your baby does bring up some wind and rouses a little, keep the environment calm and dark and they will usually settle back.

When the dream feed stops working

There are a few clear signs that the dream feed is no longer serving its purpose. The first is when your baby starts waking shortly after the dream feed, rather than extending their stretch. This can mean the disturbance of being lifted and fed is now costing more in sleep disruption than it is buying in extended sleep.

The second sign is when the dream feed no longer shifts the timing of the next waking. If you are consistently offering the dream feed at 10pm and your baby is still waking at 1am regardless, the same time as they would have woken anyway, the dream feed is adding a disruption for no net benefit.

The third sign is when your baby starts to wake at the time of the dream feed even on nights when you have decided to skip it. This can mean the dream feed has become a habit trigger: your baby's body clock has learned to expect a feed at that hour and is now waking in anticipation of it. At this point, the dream feed is creating a waking rather than preventing one, and it is time to wean it.

When to stop doing it and how to wean it

Most parents find that somewhere between four and six months, the dream feed naturally becomes less necessary. As babies mature, they begin to consolidate their longest sleep stretch into the early part of the night, often sleeping from a 7pm bedtime through to 2 or 3am without needing a top-up. When this starts happening, the dream feed can be quietly dropped.

If your baby seems to be managing without the dream feed, the simplest approach is to just stop doing it and see what happens over a few nights. Many babies barely notice its absence at this point and simply continue sleeping through or waking later than they had been.

If you prefer a more gradual approach, or if your baby has been having the dream feed for several months and you expect some adjustment, you can wean it over one to two weeks. Push the dream feed 15 to 30 minutes later every few days until it is sitting at 11pm or later. At that point it is close enough to a natural waking that dropping it entirely tends to cause less disruption. Alternatively, if you are bottle feeding, you can reduce the volume offered each night by roughly 30ml every few days until the feed is small enough that dropping it completely is a minor change.

If you are breastfeeding and have been dream feeding for several months, your body may have adjusted to producing milk at that hour. Dropping the feed gradually over one to two weeks (rather than suddenly stopping) is kinder on your supply and reduces the chance of discomfort or engorgement overnight. Most feeding bodies adjust within a week or two of the final drop.

Frequently asked questions

What is a dream feed?

A dream feed is a feeding given to a drowsy or lightly sleeping baby, typically between 10pm and 11pm, before the parents go to bed. The idea is to top up the baby's milk intake without fully waking them, extending the first stretch of sleep and giving the parents a longer uninterrupted window before the next waking. The baby is never fully roused; instead they feed in a semi-conscious state.

Does the dream feed actually work?

It works well for some babies and not at all for others. Research and clinical experience both support the idea that topping up a baby's milk intake at the start of the night can extend the first stretch of sleep, particularly in the early months. However, the effect varies considerably. Some babies wake at the same time regardless; others extend their first stretch significantly. It is worth trying consistently for a week or two before deciding whether it is helping for your baby.

How do I do a dream feed without waking my baby?

Lift your baby slowly and carefully, supporting their head, and offer the breast or bottle without turning on bright lights or making much noise. Most babies will root and latch or accept the teat without waking fully. After the feed, keep them upright briefly for a gentle wind, then transfer them back to their sleep surface. Move slowly throughout. The whole process should be calm and quiet.

When should I stop doing the dream feed?

Most parents find the dream feed stops being useful somewhere between four and six months. As babies mature and begin to consolidate their longest sleep stretch into the early part of the night independently, the dream feed can actually start disrupting their natural sleep pattern rather than extending it. If your baby is regularly waking shortly after the dream feed, or if the dream feed no longer seems to extend the first stretch, it is probably time to drop it. Wean it gradually over one to two weeks if you prefer.

Can I dream feed a breastfed baby?

Yes. Many breastfeeding parents find the dream feed works well. The baby latches and feeds in a drowsy state, and because they are not fully awake the feed tends to be shorter than a fully awake night feed. Some parents worry that a dream feed will affect supply or confuse the feeding relationship, but this is generally not a concern with an established feeder.

Is the dream feed the same as a night feed?

Not exactly. A night feed happens when a baby wakes and signals hunger; the feed is offered in response. A dream feed is initiated by the parent at a set time while the baby is still asleep. The main difference is who decides when the feed happens. A dream feed is a proactive strategy to consolidate early sleep; a responsive night feed is a reaction to hunger cues.
