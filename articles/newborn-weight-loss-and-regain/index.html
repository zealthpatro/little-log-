Newborn weight loss and regain in the first two weeks

Newborn · Growth · Reviewed 12 June 2026 · All articles

It can be unsettling to hear that your newborn has lost weight in the days after birth, especially when you are still finding your feet as a new parent. The reassuring truth is that this dip is a normal part of a healthy start, and understanding what to expect makes the weighing visits in those first weeks far less stressful.

Why newborns lose weight after birth

Every newborn goes through a period of weight loss in the first days of life. Before birth, a baby carries extra fluid, and shortly after delivery the body sheds this naturally. At the same time, feeding is only just beginning. Whether you are breastfeeding or formula feeding, your baby is learning how to latch, suck and swallow while your supply or your preparation routine is still being established.

This combination of fluid loss and a feeding pattern that has not yet settled means the scales will almost certainly show a lower number than the birth weight at the first check. This is expected, it does not mean anything has gone wrong, and it is not a sign that your baby is hungry or unwell in any serious way.

What weight loss is considered normal

NHS guidance confirms that some weight loss in the first few days is completely normal. Because every baby is different, there is a natural variation in exactly how much weight is lost and how quickly it comes back. The important thing is not to focus on any single number in isolation but to look at the overall pattern alongside your baby's feeding, nappy output and general alertness.

If your midwife or health visitor feels the amount of weight lost is large, they will say so and move quickly to support you rather than leaving you to worry without any guidance.

When weight loss stops and regain begins

After the initial dip, most babies begin putting weight back on within a few days as feeding becomes more regular and more efficient. The turnaround point varies from baby to baby, but the clear goal set out in NHS guidance is that most babies should be at or above their birth weight by three weeks of age.

Reaching that milestone does not require a dramatic surge. Even a steady, gradual climb in weight throughout the second and third weeks is progress. Babies who are feeding well, producing wet and dirty nappies, and sleeping between feeds are usually doing exactly what they should be doing, even if the scales move more slowly than you might hope.

How often your baby will be weighed in the first two weeks

During those critical first two weeks, your baby will be weighed to make sure the expected pattern of regain is happening. These checks are carried out by your midwife or health visitor and give the team a chance to spot any concerns early.

Once the two-week period is over and your baby is back to their birth weight, weighing becomes less frequent. NHS guidance is clear that healthy babies should be weighed no more than once a month up to six months of age. More frequent weighing than that is not routinely recommended, because the natural small fluctuations that happen week to week can cause unnecessary worry without providing meaningful extra information.

What happens if your baby does not regain birth weight by three weeks

If your baby has not returned to their birth weight by three weeks, your midwife or health visitor will step in to help. This is not a crisis, but it is a signal that something in the feeding picture needs a closer look.

The support usually begins with a detailed conversation about how feeding is going: how often, how long each feed lasts, and whether your baby seems satisfied afterwards. If you are breastfeeding, they may ask to observe a full feed so they can see the latch and your baby's swallowing pattern directly. They will also look at your baby's health more broadly, checking for any other signs that might be contributing to slower regain.

Getting this kind of support early is valuable. Most families find that with a few targeted adjustments, weight gain picks up quickly.

Understanding growth charts and the red book

Every baby in the UK receives a personal child health record at birth, widely known as the red book. Weight measurements from the first days and weeks onwards are plotted on the growth charts inside it. These charts show the pattern of growth that healthy babies typically follow and use curved lines called centile lines to show the range of normal.

Centile lines are not targets, they are reference ranges. Your baby does not need to sit on any particular line. It is normal for measurements to shift up or down by one centile line, and it is also normal for a baby's weight centile and length centile to be slightly different from each other. If your baby crosses two centile lines in either direction, your health visitor will want to talk through what might be behind that shift and whether any action is needed.

Boys and girls have separate charts because boys tend to be slightly heavier and taller on average and follow a marginally different growth pattern. This is reflected in the red book from the start.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for a newborn to lose weight after birth?

Yes, completely. Losing a little weight in the first few days is a normal part of a healthy transition to life outside the womb. Feeding is just beginning, and the body is releasing extra fluid it no longer needs. Your midwife will weigh your baby during the first two weeks to confirm that things are heading in the right direction.

When should a newborn be back to their birth weight?

The NHS states that most babies are at or above their birth weight by three weeks of age. If your baby has not reached that point by then, your midwife or health visitor will offer hands-on support and look carefully at feeding to understand what is happening.

How often will my newborn be weighed?

During the first two weeks your baby will be weighed as part of routine postnatal care. After that, NHS guidance recommends no more than once a month up to six months. More frequent weighing is not the norm for healthy babies and can add stress without giving a clearer picture of growth.

What happens if my baby loses a large amount of weight?

Your midwife or health visitor will talk through how feeding is going, possibly watch a full feed if you are breastfeeding, and look at your baby's overall health. You will not be left to figure this out alone. The goal is to find and address the reason quickly so weight gain can get back on track.

Can illness slow down weight regain?

Yes. If a baby is unwell, weight gain can slow for a short period. This usually corrects itself within two to three weeks once the baby has recovered. If you are concerned at any point, your health visitor or GP is the right person to speak to.
