Iwata obi: the Japanese pregnancy belly band tradition

Pregnancy · 2nd trimester · Reviewed 15 June 2026 · All articles

The iwata obi is one of the most recognisable traditions in Japanese pregnancy culture. This long sash, worn wrapped around the abdomen, has been part of Japanese maternity practice for centuries, blending practical support with ceremony and family ritual. Understanding it means understanding both what it is as a physical garment and the cultural moment that marks its first wearing, the Inu no Hi ceremony, which is still observed by many families in Japan today.

What is the iwata obi?

The iwata obi, also known as the hara obi, is a long sash traditionally made from cotton or silk. It is worn wrapped around the abdomen during pregnancy. The wrapping serves two practical purposes: it provides support for the growing belly and helps keep the abdomen and lower back warm, which holds significance in Japanese health traditions where warmth in the body's core is considered important to wellbeing. The obi is notably long, typically somewhere in the range of five to six metres, and is wound in multiple layers to create a firm, supportive band around the midsection. In traditional practice, the way it is wound and the firmness of the wrapping were considered important, and women were often taught or assisted by older female relatives.

Beyond the physical, the iwata obi carries spiritual meaning. The wrapping is not simply about comfort; it is understood as a form of protection and blessing for the unborn child. The sash itself may be a gift, and white cotton is a traditional choice, though the specific material and colour can vary by region and family. Receiving an iwata obi from a mother-in-law or mother is still a meaningful gesture in many Japanese families, carrying with it the older generation's wishes for a safe and easy birth.

The Inu no Hi ceremony

The iwata obi is not simply put on whenever it becomes convenient. Its first wearing is traditionally tied to a specific ritual occasion: the Inu no Hi, or Day of the Dog. The traditional Japanese calendar cycles through twelve animals, much like the Chinese zodiac, and the Day of the Dog occurs on a recurring basis throughout the year. The dog is considered an auspicious animal for pregnancy and birth in Japanese tradition, because dogs are widely believed to give birth with ease and to have large litters. These qualities have made the dog a symbol of safe, uncomplicated childbirth.

The ceremony is typically observed in the fifth month of pregnancy, which in Japanese traditional counting corresponds roughly to weeks 16 to 20 by modern dating. At this point in the pregnancy, the abdomen is beginning to show clearly and the pregnancy is usually well established, making it a natural moment for formal recognition and blessing. The family gathers, and the obi is wrapped around the expectant mother, often with the help of the mother-in-law or the pregnant woman's own mother. Prayers are offered for a smooth pregnancy and a safe delivery. This is not a solitary or silent act; it is a family event, marking the transition into a more visible stage of pregnancy and formally welcoming the coming child.

The timing of the ceremony can vary. Because the Day of the Dog occurs multiple times in a month, families can select the one that falls within the fifth month and that is practically convenient. Some families also visit a shrine associated with safe childbirth on this day to offer prayers alongside the domestic ceremony at home.

Belly support and physical comfort in pregnancy

The practical function of the iwata obi, providing support and warmth to the abdomen, is something that maternity care in many countries now addresses through purpose-designed maternity support belts and bands. These modern garments are typically elasticated, adjustable, and shaped to fit the pregnant abdomen at different stages. They serve a similar purpose to the traditional obi in distributing the weight of the growing belly, reducing strain on the lower back, and supporting the pelvis.

Maternity support belts can be particularly helpful for women who experience lower back discomfort or pelvic girdle pain as the pregnancy progresses. The weight of the uterus and baby shifts the body's centre of gravity, placing additional load on the lumbar spine and pelvic joints. A well-fitted support belt redistributes some of that load, which many women find reduces discomfort during daily activity. If you are experiencing significant pelvic or back pain in pregnancy, it is worth speaking with your midwife or asking for a referral to a physiotherapist, who can advise on the right support and any specific exercises that may help. A support belt on its own is not a substitute for professional assessment of pelvic girdle pain.

Modern practice in Japan

Japan has a well-developed maternity healthcare system, and contemporary Japanese women navigate the iwata obi tradition in various ways. Many families continue to observe the Inu no Hi ceremony as a meaningful cultural and family occasion, even when the traditional obi has been replaced in daily life by a modern maternity belt. The ceremony marks a significant point in the pregnancy and brings family together in a way that is valued regardless of the practical garment choices made afterwards.

The tradition also intersects with the boshi techo, the maternal and child health handbook issued to all pregnant women in Japan. This handbook is carried to every antenatal appointment and becomes a record of both the pregnancy and the child's early health. Together, the iwata obi ceremony and the boshi techo system reflect the way Japanese pregnancy culture combines practical health infrastructure with deeply embedded family ritual. The two exist side by side, one managed by the healthcare system and one carried by family tradition, and most families engage with both.

For women outside Japan who have Japanese heritage or who are interested in Japanese pregnancy customs, the Inu no Hi ceremony offers a meaningful way to mark the transition into the second trimester. The specific form it takes is flexible; what the tradition centres on is the gathering of family, the act of care and wrapping, and the shared wish for a safe birth.

Frequently asked questions

What is the iwata obi?

The iwata obi (or hara obi) is a long cotton or silk sash traditionally wrapped around a pregnant woman's abdomen in Japan. It is worn for abdominal support and warmth, and holds significant cultural and spiritual meaning related to safe childbirth.

What is the Inu no Hi ceremony?

Inu no Hi is the Day of the Dog in the traditional Japanese calendar. It is considered auspicious for pregnancy because dogs are believed to give birth easily. The Inu no Hi in the fifth month of pregnancy is traditionally when the iwata obi is first worn in a family ceremony.

Do Japanese women still wear the iwata obi today?

Many Japanese women observe the Inu no Hi ceremony as a cultural tradition while choosing to wear a modern maternity support belt rather than the traditional long obi in daily life. The ceremonial element remains culturally important as the practical garment has evolved.

Does wearing a belly band or support belt have medical benefits?

Maternity support belts can help relieve lower back pain and pelvic girdle pain by distributing the weight of the abdomen. They are not medically required but many pregnant women find them comfortable. If you have pelvic girdle pain, speak to your midwife or a physiotherapist.
