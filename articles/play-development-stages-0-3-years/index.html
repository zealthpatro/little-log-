How play develops from 0 to 3 years: what to expect at each stage

Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Play is the primary work of childhood. It is how babies and toddlers learn about themselves, about objects, about other people and about the world. But play is not the same thing at every age. It develops through recognisable stages, and understanding those stages can help you appreciate what your child is doing and why, and stop you worrying when their play looks nothing like what you imagined it would.

This guide draws on the developmental framework established by sociologist Mildred Parten, whose stages of social play remain widely used and relevant, and on broader research on what play looks like and what it does for children from birth to age 3.

What are Parten's stages of play?

Mildred Parten observed children's play in the 1920s and described six stages, which she ordered by the degree of social engagement involved. These stages are not strictly sequential in the sense that a later stage replaces an earlier one; rather, they add to one another. An older child or adult still engages in solitary play. The stages describe what becomes possible and what becomes dominant at different ages.

  • Unoccupied play: The child appears to not be playing. They are watching or moving without apparent purpose. This is most common in young babies and is often simply the observation phase before engagement begins.
  • Solitary play: The child plays alone, independent of what others near them are doing. Common from birth through the first two years.
  • Onlooker play: The child watches others play but does not join in. They may comment or ask questions. Common in toddlers who are assessing a new social situation before entering it.
  • Parallel play: The child plays alongside others, with similar materials or in the same space, but without direct interaction or shared goals. Very common in 2 to 3 year olds.
  • Associative play: The child interacts with others while playing, sharing materials and commenting on each other's activity, but without an organised shared goal. Emerges around 3 to 4 years.
  • Cooperative play: The child plays with others toward a shared goal, with roles and rules negotiated together. Typically develops from around 4 years onward.

Play from birth to 6 months: sensory and relational

In the first few months, play is entirely about sensory experience and human connection. Your baby is not yet playing with objects in any purposeful sense; they are exploring the world through their senses and through the responses of the people around them.

  • Your face is their favourite toy. Face-to-face interaction, with its turn-taking of expressions and sounds, is the foundational form of play at this age.
  • High-contrast patterns, moving objects, sounds and varied textures are genuinely stimulating.
  • Social smiling, from around 6 to 8 weeks, is the first social play behaviour: your baby is playing with you.

This is the unoccupied and early solitary stage. There is nothing to worry about and nothing to hurry.

Play from 6 to 12 months: object exploration and early cause-effect

As your baby gains motor control, objects enter the play picture in a meaningful way.

  • Mouthing, banging, shaking and dropping objects are all forms of exploration. Your baby is running experiments: what does this taste like? What sound does it make? What happens if I let go?
  • Peek-a-boo becomes genuinely exciting around 8 to 9 months as object permanence develops. The suspense of your disappearance and return is real play.
  • Social and imitative play grows: your baby watches what you do and tries to copy.

Solitary and onlooker play dominate this stage. Providing a safe floor space and varied safe objects to explore freely is the most supportive thing you can do.

Play from 12 to 24 months: symbolic play emerges

The second year brings one of the most exciting developments in play: the beginning of symbolic or pretend play.

  • Pretend play begins. Around 12 to 18 months, most toddlers begin to use objects symbolically: pretending a block is a phone, feeding a doll, putting a hat on a teddy bear. This is a sign of developing imagination and abstract thinking.
  • Solitary play remains primary. Despite being more social in daily life, most 12 to 24 month olds still play predominantly alone. This is healthy and normal.
  • Parallel play begins to emerge. Toward the end of the second year, you may notice your toddler playing alongside other children, glancing at them and mirroring what they do, without directly interacting.

Imitation is a dominant mode of learning at this age. Providing safe household objects, simple pretend props like toy cups and spoons, and plenty of time watching and joining in daily activities gives rich material for this stage.

Play from 2 to 3 years: parallel play and the beginnings of social play

The two-to-three year age range is the classic parallel play stage. If you watch two 2-year-olds at a playgroup, you will often see them playing side by side with similar toys, occasionally glancing at each other and mimicking, but not truly playing together. This is exactly right for their developmental stage.

  • Parallel play is the expected form of peer play. It is not shyness, not antisocial behaviour and not a sign of delay. It is the developmentally appropriate way a 2-year-old engages with peers.
  • Cooperative play is not expected yet. True cooperative play, with shared goals and negotiated rules, typically develops around 3 to 4 years. Expecting a 2-year-old to share willingly or play cooperatively is unrealistic and unnecessary.
  • Pretend play becomes richer. By age 2 to 3, pretend scenarios become more elaborate. Your child might set up a full tea party, assign roles to toys or act out scenes from their daily life.

Why solitary play is valuable and not a warning sign

One of the most common questions parents ask is whether their child plays alone too much. For children under 3, solitary play is the developmentally expected and healthy primary mode of play. A baby or toddler who plays contentedly alone is demonstrating concentration, independence and the ability to self-direct their activity. These are excellent qualities.

The question worth asking is not whether a child plays alone, but whether they can also engage warmly with familiar adults when the opportunity arises, and whether their play appears purposeful and curious. If yes, solitary play is not a concern.

The importance of unstructured play

Unstructured play is free play that the child directs themselves, without adult-defined goals, rules or outcomes. The child chooses what to play and how. It is the opposite of a structured activity like an organised game or a craft project.

Research on unstructured play in early childhood consistently shows significant benefits:

  • Greater creativity and problem-solving flexibility
  • Better emotional regulation
  • Stronger executive function, including planning and impulse control
  • More sophisticated language when children narrate their own play

In a world where baby classes, structured activities and educational toys are abundant, it is worth protecting time for your child to simply play freely with open-ended materials: blocks, water, sand, household objects, and time outside. This kind of play is not wasted time. It is some of the most valuable time in a young child's day.

The role of parents in play

The most effective parental role in play is often the one that feels the least productive: being nearby, available and responsive without directing or taking over.

  • Follow your child's lead. Join their game on their terms rather than steering it toward a more educational or tidy outcome.
  • Narrate without directing. "You are stacking the red one on top" is connecting without controlling. "Now put the blue one" is directing.
  • Floor time matters. Getting down on the floor at your baby or toddler's level is one of the most connecting things you can do. Physical presence at eye level communicates availability.
  • Resist the urge to entertain constantly. Boredom in a safe environment is one of the conditions under which children develop the ability to generate their own play. You do not need to fill every moment.

Outdoor play: simple, important and underrated

Outdoor play provides a different quality of experience than indoor play. Natural variation in terrain, light, temperature, wind and sound offers sensory richness that indoor environments rarely replicate. The physical challenges of uneven ground, hills and varied surfaces support gross motor development. Time outside is also associated with better sleep and reduced stress in young children.

Even brief time outdoors every day is worthwhile. Rain and cold are not barriers; appropriate clothing makes almost all weather navigable and interesting.

Screen time and play quality

Current guidance from the WHO and AAP recommends avoiding screens other than video calls for babies under 18 to 24 months, and limiting to around one hour of high-quality content per day for children aged 2 to 5. Heavy screen use in the early years is associated with less time in active, exploratory play and fewer language interactions, both of which matter for development.

This is not about guilt. It is about ensuring that screen time, when it happens, does not crowd out the things that matter more at this age: physical play, language interaction, exploration and human connection.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my toddler play next to other children instead of with them?

This is called parallel play and it is the developmentally expected form of peer play for children aged 2 to 3. Cooperative play, where children play together with shared goals, typically emerges around 3 to 4 years.

Should I be worried that my 2-year-old prefers to play alone?

Solitary play is healthy, normal and valuable at all ages, especially in the first two years. A 2-year-old who plays contentedly on their own is demonstrating good concentration and independence. The question to ask is whether they can also interact with familiar people when the opportunity arises.

What is unstructured play and why does it matter?

Unstructured play is free play without adult-defined goals or rules. The child chooses what to do and how to do it. Research consistently shows that unstructured play supports creativity, problem-solving, emotional regulation and executive function in ways that structured activities do not.

How much should parents be involved in their baby or toddler's play?

The most effective parental role is to be available and responsive without directing. Follow your child's lead. Narrate what they are doing. Join in when invited. Avoid taking over or turning play into a lesson. Presence and warmth matter more than active participation.

Does screen time affect play quality?

Heavy screen use in the under-2s is associated with less time in active, exploratory play and reduced language interactions. Current guidance recommends avoiding screens other than video calls for babies under 18 to 24 months, and limiting to one hour per day of high-quality content for 2 to 5 year olds.

What are the benefits of outdoor play for babies and toddlers?

Outdoor play provides sensory experiences, physical challenge and natural variation that indoor environments rarely match. Even brief time outdoors every day is beneficial for development, sleep and wellbeing.
