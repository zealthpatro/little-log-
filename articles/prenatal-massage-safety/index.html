Prenatal massage: is it safe, what to avoid and how to find a qualified therapist

Second and third trimesters · Pregnancy · Updated July 2026 · All articles

A good pregnancy massage can feel like one of the kindest things you do for yourself in the whole nine months. Your back aches, your hips feel pulled in five directions, your ankles are puffed up by evening, and your shoulders carry more tension than you realise. Massage can genuinely help with all of those things. But it is reasonable to wonder whether it is safe, which positions are used, which areas a therapist should avoid, and how to find someone who actually knows what they are doing when you are pregnant. This article covers all of that.

Is pregnancy massage safe?

For most people, from the second trimester onward, prenatal massage with a qualified therapist is considered safe. Most practitioners advise waiting until after 13 to 14 weeks, not because there is strong evidence that massage causes problems in early pregnancy, but because the risk of miscarriage is highest in the first 12 weeks and many therapists prefer not to treat during that window as a precaution. The caution is protective rather than evidence-based, and that distinction matters: it is not that massage is known to cause harm in the first trimester, it is that the timing makes practitioners cautious.

From the second trimester onward, the evidence is reassuring. Research supports prenatal massage for reducing stress hormones, easing musculoskeletal discomfort and improving sleep. Let your midwife know you are planning to have regular massage, especially if you have any pregnancy complications, and always choose a therapist with specific prenatal training rather than a general massage therapist who has not worked with pregnant clients before.

What makes a prenatal massage different

The most immediate difference is position. You will not lie face-down on a standard massage table during pregnancy: the growing bump makes that impossible and potentially uncomfortable from around 16 to 20 weeks onward. Instead, a qualified prenatal therapist will position you on your side, supported by bolsters or a pregnancy pillow, or semi-reclined at an angle. Some specialist tables have a cut-out designed for a pregnant belly, but side-lying is generally considered the safest and most comfortable approach for most of pregnancy.

Pressure is also different. Prenatal massage is gentler than a standard deep tissue massage. The therapist will avoid deep pressure on the lower legs (for reasons explained below), focus on areas most strained by pregnancy, and adapt their techniques to what feels right at each stage. The areas that benefit most are the lower back, hips, glutes, shoulders, neck and feet. Many people find that even 45 minutes focused on those areas produces a noticeable improvement in comfort that lasts several days.

Pressure points said to trigger contractions

You may have heard that certain pressure points should be avoided during pregnancy. This is true, and a trained prenatal massage therapist will already know about them. The most commonly cited is SP6, an acupressure point located on the inner leg about three finger-widths above the ankle bone. Another is Pericardium 6, located on the inner wrist. Both are traditionally believed in acupressure and reflexology to stimulate uterine contractions.

The clinical evidence that brief accidental pressure on these points causes any harm in a standard massage context is limited. But the precautionary approach taken by reputable practitioners is to avoid sustained deep pressure on these specific points during pregnancy. Importantly, this does not mean all foot massage or leg massage is off-limits: a gentle effleurage stroke along the calf or a slow foot rub is very different from targeted acupressure. If you are ever unsure what your therapist is doing, ask them to explain the technique and why they are using it.

Benefits of prenatal massage

The research base for prenatal massage is genuinely encouraging. A 2004 study by Tiffany Field and colleagues, published in the Journal of Psychosomatic Obstetrics and Gynaecology, found that twice-weekly massage during pregnancy reduced anxiety, improved mood and reduced leg pain in pregnant women. The study also found lower cortisol levels (a key stress hormone) in the massage group compared with controls. Reduced cortisol is significant: chronically elevated cortisol in pregnancy is associated with poorer sleep, increased anxiety and, in some research, higher rates of preterm birth.

Beyond the hormonal evidence, the practical benefits are easy to understand: the lower back and hips bear enormous strain as the centre of gravity shifts and the pelvis widens in preparation for birth. Gentle, targeted massage of these areas can reduce the muscular tension that contributes to pregnancy back pain and hip discomfort. Swelling in the lower legs and ankles, a very common feature of the third trimester, can also be eased by gentle massage that encourages lymphatic drainage. Regular sessions tend to compound in benefit: the body learns to release tension more readily with repeated treatment.

How to find a qualified prenatal massage therapist

The most important thing to check is whether a therapist has specific training in prenatal massage, not just a general massage qualification. Prenatal massage is a specialist skill: it requires knowledge of which conditions contraindicate treatment, which positions are safe at each stage of pregnancy, which techniques and pressure points to avoid, and how the body changes across the trimesters. A general massage therapist without prenatal training may not know any of this.

When you contact a therapist, ask directly: do you have specific prenatal massage training? How many weeks into pregnancy will you treat? What conditions would prevent you from treating me? A therapist who is confident and specific in their answers is a good sign. Avoid deep tissue massage, hot stone massage and high-pressure techniques during pregnancy: these are not appropriate for prenatal work. Your midwife may be able to recommend a local practitioner, particularly if you are in an area with an active birth community or pregnancy wellness network.

When to avoid massage entirely in pregnancy

There are conditions where massage should not go ahead without explicit clearance from your doctor or midwife. These include placenta previa, pre-eclampsia or uncontrolled high blood pressure, deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or a history of blood clots, unexplained vaginal bleeding, a risk of preterm labour, and significant or sudden swelling that has not been assessed by a healthcare professional. Sudden swelling in pregnancy, particularly in the face or hands, can be a sign of pre-eclampsia and needs medical attention before anything else.

Always tell your massage therapist your full pregnancy health history before a session. A responsible prenatal therapist will ask you directly about these conditions. If any of them apply to you, get sign-off from your midwife first. And if your therapist does not ask about your health before treating you, that is itself a red flag.

Self-massage and partner massage at home

You do not need to see a professional therapist to get some of the benefit. Safe areas for self-massage or partner massage at home include your feet, hands, scalp, shoulders and neck. The technique to use is effleurage: long, slow, stroking movements with gentle but consistent pressure, following the natural lines of the body rather than pressing into specific spots.

The one area to treat gently and with awareness is the lower legs. Because pregnancy increases the risk of DVT, particularly in the calves, deep pressure in that area is best avoided at home unless you have been specifically shown a safe technique. A warm foot soak followed by gentle foot massage is a particularly lovely way for a partner to help at the end of the day: it eases swelling, improves circulation and is a genuinely restful ritual to build into the evening. Use a carrier oil such as sweet almond or coconut, warmed slightly in your hands before applying, for a more comfortable glide.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to have a massage in the first trimester?

Most therapists wait until after 13 weeks as a precaution. There is no strong evidence that massage causes miscarriage, but the risk of miscarriage is highest in the first trimester and many practitioners prefer not to treat during this window. If you are in any doubt, it is reasonable to wait and to check with your midwife before booking.

Which pressure points should be avoided during pregnancy massage?

SP6 on the inner ankle and certain wrist points (Pericardium 6) are traditionally said to stimulate uterine contractions. A qualified prenatal massage therapist will know to avoid sustained deep pressure on these points. Regular leg and foot massage is not dangerous: only specific acupressure targeting these points should be avoided.

Can massage trigger labour?

There is no strong clinical evidence that standard pregnancy massage triggers premature labour. Certain acupressure techniques used deliberately by trained practitioners can encourage contractions at term, but this is a very specific, intentional use that is entirely different from a regular prenatal massage session.

How is prenatal massage different from a regular massage?

You lie on your side or semi-reclined rather than face-down. Pressure is gentler than in a standard deep tissue massage, certain areas and pressure points are avoided, and the therapist needs specific prenatal training. The session focuses on areas most strained during pregnancy: the lower back, hips, shoulders and feet.

Can my partner give me a back massage during pregnancy?

Yes, gentle shoulder, back and foot massage from a partner is safe throughout pregnancy. Use long, gentle strokes rather than deep pressure, avoid the lower calves and skip any deep acupressure. A warm carrier oil such as sweet almond or coconut is a lovely addition for comfort.

What should I tell my massage therapist before a session?

Tell them how many weeks pregnant you are, any complications or risk factors your midwife has mentioned, any areas of particular pain or discomfort, and whether you have had any bleeding, swelling or other symptoms. A good prenatal therapist will ask all of this before beginning your session.
