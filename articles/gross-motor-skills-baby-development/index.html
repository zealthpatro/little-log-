Gross motor skills in babies: rolling, sitting, crawling and walking timeline

Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Watching your baby move from a newborn who cannot lift their own head to a toddler taking their first independent steps is one of the most awe-inspiring things about the first year of life. Gross motor skills, the large-muscle movements that allow a person to roll, sit, crawl, stand and walk, develop in a broadly predictable sequence, though the exact timing varies considerably between healthy babies.

This guide walks through the gross motor milestones of the first year and a little beyond, with honest guidance on the wide range of normal, what supports development, and when it is worth checking in with your health visitor.

What are gross motor skills?

Gross motor skills are the movements that involve the large muscle groups of the body: the neck, trunk, arms and legs. They are the foundation for almost all physical activity and include holding the head steady, rolling over, sitting, crawling, standing, walking and eventually running and jumping.

Gross motor development follows a head-to-toe pattern. Neck and head control comes first, then trunk and core strength, then arm and leg coordination for crawling, then the balance and leg strength for walking. Each stage builds on the last.

Head control: birth to 4 months

A newborn has very little control over their head and needs it supported at all times. Over the first few months, neck strength grows rapidly.

  • 1 month: Briefly lifts head when on tummy.
  • 2 months: Holds head up for longer during tummy time and begins to hold it steady when upright in arms.
  • 4 months: Head control is mostly established. Most babies have steady control of their head by around 4 months.

Tummy time from birth is the key practice for building head and neck strength. Even a few minutes per session, several times a day, makes a real difference.

Rolling: 4 to 6 months

Rolling is often the first big motor milestone that surprises parents, sometimes happening when least expected.

  • Tummy to back usually comes first, typically between 3 and 5 months. Your baby uses their arms to push off and the momentum tips them over.
  • Back to tummy follows, usually between 4 and 6 months, and takes more deliberate core engagement.

Once rolling begins, never leave your baby on a raised surface unattended. Some babies roll both ways within days; others master one direction weeks before the other. Both are normal.

Sitting: 6 to 9 months

Independent sitting takes considerable core strength and balance. It develops in stages.

  • Sitting with full support: from around 4 to 5 months, propped by cushions or held.
  • Sitting with hands for support: most babies can do this by around 6 months, using their arms as props.
  • Sitting independently: typically established between 6 and 9 months. Some babies achieve it closer to 6 months; others are still working on balance at 9 months.

Plenty of supervised floor time, where your baby can practise balancing, is the best support for sitting development. Limiting time in bouncer seats and swing chairs helps too, as these do not challenge the core muscles the way free floor time does.

Crawling: 7 to 10 months

Crawling is one of the most variable gross motor milestones. The range of normal is genuinely wide, and the styles of crawling vary just as much as the timing.

  • Classic hands-and-knees crawling typically emerges between 7 and 10 months.
  • Belly or commando crawling often appears first, with the baby pulling themselves along on their tummy.
  • Bottom shuffling is used by some babies instead of crawling, sitting upright and propelling themselves along the floor.
  • Not crawling at all is also a normal variant. Some babies go directly from sitting to pulling to stand to walking, skipping crawling entirely.

Crawling is not a developmental prerequisite for walking. Most research does not support the idea that skipping crawling causes later developmental problems. What matters is that a baby is exploring their environment in some form of purposeful movement.

Pulling to stand: 8 to 10 months

Once babies can sit well, many begin pulling themselves up to standing using furniture or a person's legs. This usually happens between 8 and 10 months. Early on, many babies can get up but cannot yet get back down, leading to frustrated standing. They work this out over the following weeks.

Cruising, taking sideways steps while holding on to furniture, typically follows pulling to stand. It is an important bridge between supported standing and independent walking.

First independent steps: 9 to 15 months

The range for first independent steps is genuinely broad. Some babies take their first steps at 9 or 10 months; others are still cruising at 14 or 15 months. Both ends of this range are normal.

  • The AAP considers walking by 18 months to be within the typical range.
  • If your baby is not walking independently by 18 months, mention it to your health visitor.
  • Early walkers are not necessarily more advanced overall; late walkers are not behind. The timing of walking is largely independent of other aspects of development.

The role of tummy time in gross motor development

Tummy time is the single most important thing you can do to support your baby's gross motor development. It builds the neck, shoulder, back and core muscles that underpin every subsequent milestone: rolling, sitting, crawling and walking all depend on the strength built during tummy time.

  • Start from birth, even if sessions are very short at first.
  • Aim for a total of around 30 minutes per day by 3 to 4 months, broken into multiple sessions.
  • Make it more enjoyable by getting down on the floor yourself, using a mirror, or placing a toy just within reach.
  • Tummy time while awake and supervised is safe and beneficial. Back to sleep, tummy to play.

Floor time versus container devices

Bouncers, swings, jumpers, exersaucers and car seats all have their place. But extended time in these devices limits the free movement that builds muscle strength and motor skills. A baby strapped into a bouncer cannot roll, pivot, push up or practise any of the movements that build toward the next milestone.

The best environment for gross motor development is a safe, clear floor space where your baby can move freely. This does not need to be large or elaborate. A blanket on a clean floor with a few safe objects is plenty.

When to talk to your health visitor or GP

Developmental ranges are wide, and most differences in timing are nothing to worry about. Do mention it to your health visitor if:

  • Your baby has poor head control at 4 months
  • Your baby cannot sit with support at all by 9 months
  • Your baby is not using their legs at all when supported in standing by 12 months
  • Your baby is not walking independently by 18 months
  • You notice your baby strongly avoids using one side of their body
  • Your baby has lost gross motor skills they previously had

Loss of previously acquired skills is always worth raising promptly. For concerns about timing, raise them at any scheduled developmental check or whenever something feels off.

Frequently asked questions

When should a baby have full head control?

Most babies have steady head control by around 4 months. Before then, the head needs to be supported whenever you hold your baby. If your baby still has very poor head control at 4 months, mention it to your health visitor.

Do all babies crawl before they walk?

No. Some babies skip crawling entirely, moving directly from sitting to pulling to stand to walking. Others bottom-shuffle or roll to get around. These are all normal variations. Crawling is not a developmental prerequisite for walking.

How much tummy time does a baby need?

Aim for around 30 minutes of tummy time total per day from the early weeks, broken into short sessions. Tummy time builds the neck, shoulder and core strength that underpins rolling, sitting and eventually crawling.

Is it bad to put babies in bouncers and jumpers for long periods?

Sitting devices are fine for short periods, but extended time in them limits the free movement babies need to build strength and motor skills. Floor time, especially tummy time, is more beneficial for motor development than time in any container device.

When is the upper limit for independent walking?

The AAP considers walking by 18 months to be within the normal range. If your baby is not walking independently by 18 months, it is worth mentioning to your health visitor or GP. Many babies who walk later than average do so without any underlying issue.

Is it a problem if my baby skipped crawling?

Skipping crawling is generally not a problem if your baby is meeting other motor milestones and is exploring their environment through some form of movement. Most research does not support the idea that crawling is essential for later development.
