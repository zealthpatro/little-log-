When your baby won't eat during illness: what to do and what not to worry about

0 to 3 years · Health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

One of the most unsettling things about a sick baby is watching them turn away from feeds they would normally take eagerly. If your baby is unwell and barely eating, you are likely worried and searching for reassurance at an hour when everything feels bigger than it probably is. The short version: a reduced appetite during illness is completely normal, and in most cases the priority is hydration, not calories. This guide explains what is happening, what to offer and when you actually need to act.

Why appetite drops when a baby is ill

There are several straightforward reasons why a sick baby eats less, and none of them are cause for immediate concern on their own.

  • The immune system requires a great deal of energy when fighting an infection, and the body redirects its resources accordingly. Digestion takes a back seat.
  • Fever raises body temperature, which can suppress appetite and make food feel unappealing. This happens in adults too.
  • A sore throat, a blocked nose or mouth ulcers from a viral illness can make eating and swallowing genuinely uncomfortable. Your baby is not being fussy; they are in discomfort.
  • Nausea from gastroenteritis makes the idea of food unappealing for anyone, including babies. If your baby has vomited, it makes complete sense that they are not reaching for their next meal.

None of this means anything is going wrong. Your baby's body knows what it is doing. The body's response to illness is well-designed, and a short period of reduced eating is part of that process, not a problem to fix.

What actually matters during short illnesses: hydration first

During a short illness of a few days, the priority is keeping your baby hydrated, not counting calories or worrying about whether they have eaten enough. The body has reserves, and a few days of reduced eating will not cause harm in an otherwise healthy baby.

  • Breastfed babies: the breast is the best place to start. Offer feeds more frequently and let your baby decide how much they want. Do not set a timer or worry about the length of each feed.
  • Formula-fed babies: continue normal-strength formula and offer it more often if your baby seems willing. Never dilute formula, even if your baby's stomach seems unsettled.
  • Babies on solids: do not worry if they refuse all solid food. Focus on milk feeds and fluids. Solids can wait.

Keeping a rough mental note of wet nappies is the most practical way to check hydration at home. If nappies are staying wet, you have good evidence that your baby is taking in enough fluid.

Breastfeeding during illness

The NHS and the World Health Organization both recommend continuing to breastfeed through illness. Do not stop breastfeeds because your baby is unwell; this is the opposite of what helps.

Breast milk contains antibodies specific to the illness that your baby and you are both being exposed to. As your body mounts its own immune response, your milk adapts to provide immune support alongside fluid and nutrition. There is nothing that replaces that.

Your baby may want to feed more frequently during illness, partly for nutrition and partly for comfort. Both are valid reasons, and both are genuinely helpful. You are not creating a bad habit by feeding a sick baby on demand.

If your baby is vomiting frequently, offer shorter but more frequent feeds rather than long feeds that may trigger vomiting. A small amount, often, is usually better tolerated than a full feed.

Formula and solids during illness

Continue normal-strength formula during illness. Do not dilute it, even if your baby's tummy seems off. Offer it more often in smaller amounts if your baby is willing. Small, frequent amounts work much better than larger amounts that may overwhelm a queasy stomach.

For older babies on solids, the approach is simple: offer soft, bland and familiar foods if they show any interest at all. This is not the moment to introduce anything new.

Good options if your baby is willing to eat include:

  • Porridge or baby cereal
  • Mashed potato
  • Banana
  • Plain yoghurt
  • Mashed vegetables

If your baby shows no interest at all, that is fine too. Do not force food. A baby who is genuinely nauseated will not benefit from being pressed to eat and may become more distressed as a result. You cannot speed up recovery by getting more food in.

What not to do

There are a few things that feel intuitive but are not actually helpful and are worth knowing about.

  • Do not force feed. This can create a negative association with eating and does not speed up recovery. It may also cause vomiting if your baby's stomach is unsettled.
  • Do not offer calorie supplements or high-energy drinks without specific advice from a doctor or dietitian. These are not appropriate for routine short illnesses.
  • Do not stop breastfeeds. Breast milk is the best thing for a sick breastfed baby, full stop.
  • Do not try to compensate with sugary foods or fruit juice. These are not helpful and fruit juice in particular can worsen diarrhoea if that is part of the illness.
  • Do not panic if your baby eats almost nothing for two or three days during a clear illness where they are staying hydrated and wet nappies are still appearing. The appetite will return.

When reduced appetite is a concern

Most of the time, a reduced appetite during illness is not a red flag. There are specific situations where you should contact your GP or call 111, though.

  • Your baby has refused all feeds, including breast or formula, for more than three to five hours (younger babies) or six to eight hours (older babies), and you are seeing signs of dehydration such as dry nappies, sunken eyes or no tears when crying.
  • Appetite has not improved at all within three to five days of the illness starting.
  • Your baby appears to be losing weight or seems significantly weaker than usual.
  • The illness seems to have resolved but appetite has not returned within 24 to 48 hours of your baby feeling better.
  • Your baby is under three months old and is feeding significantly less than usual. At this age, any notable change in feeding warrants same-day medical attention. Do not wait and see.

If you are worried for any reason that is not on this list, that is also enough reason to call. You do not have to tick a box before seeking reassurance.

Return of appetite after illness

The good news is that most babies bounce back quickly once a fever breaks or vomiting stops. Appetite typically returns within 24 to 48 hours of your baby starting to feel better, often quite suddenly.

Some babies go through a catch-up phase after illness and eat noticeably more than usual for a few days. This is completely normal. The body is replenishing what it used during the illness, and you can follow your baby's lead without worrying about overfeeding.

If your baby was on solids before the illness, reintroduce them gradually and let your baby guide the pace. There is no need to work back through a sequence of foods; just offer familiar things and build back up as their interest returns.

Frequently asked questions

Should I force my baby to eat when they are ill?

No. Forcing food creates a negative association with eating and does not speed up recovery. A baby who is genuinely nauseated or uncomfortable will not benefit from being pressed to eat. Focus on fluids and let your baby's appetite return on its own.

Is it safe to stop formula feeds during illness?

No. Continue offering formula at normal strength throughout the illness. Offer it more often in smaller amounts if your baby seems willing. Never dilute formula. If your baby is refusing all feeds for several hours alongside signs of dehydration, contact your GP or call 111.

How long is it normal for appetite to be reduced?

During most short illnesses, reduced appetite is normal for two to three days. If appetite has not improved at all after three to five days of illness, or if it has not returned within 24 to 48 hours of the illness resolving, contact a doctor.

What can I give a sick baby to eat?

For younger babies, breast milk or formula remains the priority. For babies on solids, offer soft, bland and familiar foods if they show any interest: porridge, mashed potato, banana, yoghurt and mashed vegetables are all gentle options. Do not introduce new foods while your baby is unwell.

Should I offer extra milk if my baby refuses solids during illness?

Yes. If your baby is refusing solid food, increasing milk feeds is a sensible and reassuring approach. Breast milk in particular provides fluids, nutrition and immune support all at once. Solids are not the priority during a short illness, and your baby can go back to them once they are feeling better.

When should I be worried that my baby is not eating?

Contact your GP or call 111 if your baby has refused all feeds for more than three to five hours (younger babies) or six to eight hours (older babies) alongside signs of dehydration. Also seek advice if appetite has not returned within three to five days, if your baby is losing weight, or if they are under three months and feeding significantly less than usual. Always call if you are worried, even if none of these boxes are ticked.
