Object permanence in babies: what it is, when it develops and why peek-a-boo matters

6 to 12 months · Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

There is a moment, somewhere in the middle of the first year, when a toy rolling behind the sofa becomes the most fascinating thing in the world. Your baby cranes forward, reaches out, and searches. Before this moment, out of sight really did mean gone. Now they know better. That shift is called object permanence, and it is one of the most significant leaps in early cognitive development.

What object permanence is

Object permanence is the understanding that people, objects, and animals continue to exist even when they cannot be seen, heard, or touched. The psychologist Jean Piaget, who spent decades studying how children's thinking develops, identified this concept as a cornerstone of what he called the sensorimotor stage, the first two years of life.

In the earliest weeks and months, a baby's world is entirely made up of what they can perceive right now. If a toy is covered by a cloth, it simply ceases to exist from the baby's perspective. There is no "hidden toy" in their mental model, so there is no reason to search for it. This is not a limitation to worry about. It is simply where development begins.

As the brain matures and experience accumulates, babies gradually build a mental representation of the world, an internal map that includes things they cannot currently see. That process takes most of the first two years to complete, and it unfolds in stages rather than all at once.

When object permanence develops

There is no single moment when object permanence switches on. It is a gradual process that follows a broad and variable timeline.

The earliest stirrings appear around four to five months, when babies begin to look briefly in the direction of an object that has moved out of view. By six to seven months, many babies will search for a partially hidden object, pulling away a cloth that covers only part of a toy. Between eight and 12 months, most babies will actively search for a toy that has been completely hidden, which is the most widely recognised sign that object permanence is developing well.

The CDC's developmental milestones note that looking for hidden objects is a typical behaviour to watch for around nine months, and the AAP confirms that this kind of searching behaviour is a healthy cognitive indicator in the second half of the first year. Full maturity, where a baby can track an object moved through several hiding places without getting confused, continues to develop until around 18 months to two years. The range is wide, and there is considerable variation between individual babies.

What it looks like at different ages

Watching object permanence emerge in real life is one of the quieter joys of the first year. Here is a rough guide to the kinds of behaviour you might notice:

  • Four to five months: Your baby watches a toy move, then looks briefly at the spot where it disappeared. They do not search actively.
  • Six to seven months: If a cloth covers half a favourite toy, they will reach for the visible part or bat the cloth away. A fully hidden object still confuses them.
  • Eight to nine months: Your baby will lift a cloth or look under a cup to find a hidden toy. This is a reliable sign that object permanence is taking hold.
  • Ten to twelve months: They begin to understand that a toy moved from one hiding place to another is now in the new location, not the old one.

None of these ages are hard rules. Some babies arrive at each stage a little earlier or later, and they all get there in their own time.

The A-not-B error: a charming window into development

Around eight to ten months, babies display a behaviour that Piaget found particularly illuminating. Researchers call it the A-not-B error, and it is worth knowing about because it looks puzzling but is completely normal.

Here is how it works: you hide a toy under a cup at location A, and your baby searches for it there and finds it. You do this a few times. Then, while your baby watches, you move the toy to a new hiding spot at location B. When you let your baby search, they very often go back to location A, the place where they found it before, even though they just watched you move it.

This is not a mistake born of inattention. It reflects the early state of working memory and the difficulty of overriding a previously successful action. The baby's motor memory says "A worked last time" and that pull is stronger than the new visual information. By around 12 months, most babies are able to update their search and go directly to the correct new location.

If you notice your baby making this error, it is not a cause for concern. It is a diagnostic window into a brain in the middle of something remarkable.

Importantly, the A-not-B error is a performance limitation, not a knowledge gap. Research by Adele Diamond (1985) demonstrated that the error is driven by the immature prefrontal cortex's difficulty inhibiting a previously rewarded reaching action, not by failure to understand that the object moved. Separately, Renée Baillargeon (1987) used looking-time studies to show that babies as young as three and a half months already appear surprised when objects violate physical expectations, suggesting some implicit object knowledge exists well before babies can demonstrate it through reaching. Piaget's original timeline was based on what babies could do with their hands; what they understand begins earlier.

Why peek-a-boo is so powerful

Peek-a-boo is probably the oldest baby game in the world, and it turns out to be almost perfectly designed for this stage of development. Every time your face disappears behind your hands and reappears, your baby gets to practise the exact cognitive task that object permanence requires: holding a mental image of something they cannot currently see and predicting its return.

In the early months, the "boo" moment produces genuine surprise. Your baby's face lights up partly because each appearance still feels like a small miracle. By around eight to nine months, the game shifts. Your baby begins to anticipate your return before you reveal yourself. They may lean forward, smile in expectation, or even pull your hands down, which is a clear signal that their mental model is robust enough to predict what is coming.

There is a language dimension here too. "Where is it?" is one of the earliest communicative questions babies respond to with a searching glance or gesture. The back-and-forth of peek-a-boo, hiding and returning, also builds early conversational skills: the idea that interactions have turns, that something goes away, and that it comes back.

Other play that supports object permanence

You do not need a specialist toy kit to support this stage. Simple, everyday play is all it takes.

  • Hiding toys under a cloth: Start with partial hiding, then progress to full coverage. Let your baby do the uncovering themselves.
  • Cups and containers: Hide a small toy under one of two cups and let your baby find it. Nesting cups, where smaller cups disappear inside larger ones, are a natural extension of this.
  • Pop-up toys: The action of something disappearing and reappearing is engaging and developmentally rich.
  • Treasure basket play: A basket of everyday objects, a wooden spoon, a small tin, a scarf, invites babies to take things out, put them back, and rediscover them.
  • Your own disappearing act: Hiding your face behind a book, a pillow, or even just your hands gives your baby the same practice as peek-a-boo in a different form.

Follow your baby's lead. If they lose interest in one game, try another. The goal is enjoyment, not drilling a skill.

Object permanence and separation anxiety

There is a tender irony in this milestone. Once your baby fully grasps that you exist when you are out of sight, separation anxiety often intensifies. Before this understanding developed, out of sight meant gone, which in its own way was peaceful. Now your baby knows you are somewhere else, and they have no way of knowing when you will be back.

The NHS notes that separation anxiety is common from around six months and often peaks around nine months, which maps closely onto the development of object permanence. This is not regression. It is the predictable emotional consequence of a significant cognitive leap. Your baby is not becoming more anxious in general. They are becoming more aware of how much you matter.

Separation anxiety typically eases as language develops, roughly from 18 months onward, because a baby who can understand "Mama is coming back after lunch" has the cognitive and linguistic tools to manage the wait. Until then, warm and consistent goodbyes, brief separations, and predictable returns are the most helpful things you can offer.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do babies develop object permanence?

Object permanence begins to emerge around five to six months, but most babies show clear, reliable signs between eight and 12 months. It continues to mature well into the second year of life. The range is wide and individual variation is normal. If your baby is showing some searching behaviour by the time they approach their first birthday, that is well within the typical range.

What is the A-not-B error in babies?

The A-not-B error is a behaviour seen in babies around eight to ten months. After finding a hidden toy at location A several times, the baby will search at A even after watching you move the toy to location B. This reflects the state of working memory at this age, not a gap in intelligence. Most babies grow out of it by around 12 months as their ability to update a mental model improves.

Does peek-a-boo help with object permanence?

Yes. Peek-a-boo is one of the most natural ways to practise object permanence. Each time your face disappears and reappears, your baby gets gentle, repeated practice at holding a mental image of something they cannot see. By around eight to nine months, babies start to anticipate your return before you reveal yourself, which is a clear sign the skill is developing well.

Why does my baby cry when I leave the room?

Once your baby understands that you still exist when you are out of sight, they also understand that you are somewhere else and they do not know when you will return. That uncertainty is what drives separation anxiety, which typically peaks around nine months. It is a sign of healthy attachment and normal cognitive development, not a problem that needs to be fixed.

How do I know if my baby has developed object permanence?

The most practical sign is that your baby searches for something that has been hidden. If they lift a cloth to find a hidden toy, look behind your back for something you have put there, or scan the room for you when you leave, they are demonstrating object permanence in action. Separation anxiety when you leave is another everyday indicator.

Is there anything I can do to help my baby develop object permanence?

Regular play is the most effective and enjoyable route. Peek-a-boo, hiding toys under cloths or cups, pop-up toys, and nesting cups all provide gentle, repeated practice. You do not need special equipment, and there is no need to push ahead of your baby's pace. This milestone develops naturally across the first two years as the brain matures.
