Baby food textures: moving from first foods to family meals

Feeding · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Texture progression is one of the least-discussed aspects of weaning, and one of the most important. Offering the right texture at the right stage is not just about practicality; it shapes how your baby learns to chew, how their speech muscles develop, and whether they grow into a child who accepts a wide variety of foods. Staying on smooth purees for too long has measurable consequences. Moving through textures at a reasonable pace, on the other hand, sets your baby up for a lifetime of varied, enjoyable eating.

This guide takes you stage by stage from first foods at 6 months through to near-family food at 12 months, with practical examples and advice for the moments when things do not go smoothly.

Why texture progression matters

Between 6 and 9 months, babies are in a sensitive window for accepting new food textures. Their brains and mouths are primed to learn how to manage different consistencies. A study published in the British Medical Journal found that babies who were not offered lumpy food until after 9 months had significantly more feeding difficulties at 15 months and at 7 years of age, compared with babies who had been introduced to lumps earlier.

This is about more than stubbornness or pickiness. The muscles used to chew and move food around the mouth are the same muscles used in speech and articulation. Varied texture exposure during the weaning period supports oral motor development in ways that have lasting effects. Purees do not give those muscles a workout. Lumps, mashed food, and soft finger foods do.

Texture progression also builds tolerance and acceptance. Babies who experience a narrow range of textures during weaning are more likely to reject unfamiliar textures later. Variety early on pays dividends in the toddler years.

6 months: smooth puree or very soft mashed food and finger foods

At 6 months, two textures work equally well as starting points.

The first is a smooth, thick puree. Think the consistency of thick yoghurt or soft hummus. Avoid making purees too watery; thin, runny food is actually harder for a baby to manage and does not build useful oral motor skills. If a food blends to a very liquid consistency, strain it or add a little expressed milk or formula to thicken it.

The second is a soft, mashable food large enough to grip. A ripe banana half, a stick of well-steamed sweet potato, a large floret of soft-cooked broccoli. These are the beginning of finger foods. At 6 months, hands can grip large objects but cannot pick up small pieces, so size is important: pieces should be about the length and width of an adult finger.

Both approaches are valid starting points, and combining them is common. What matters is that the food is very soft, either thoroughly blended or squishable between finger and thumb with no effort. If you cannot squish it easily, it is too firm for a 6-month-old.

7 to 8 months: soft lumps, mash, and firmer finger foods

By around 7 months, most babies are ready to move beyond perfectly smooth food. Now is the time to introduce:

  • Soft lumpy mash: mashed potato with small soft lumps remaining, roughly mashed sweet potato, fork-mashed cooked lentils
  • Minced or finely flaked food: minced soft-cooked meat, flaked fish, crumbled soft-cooked egg
  • Firmer finger foods: a stick of ripe avocado, a strip of well-cooked chicken thigh, a finger of soft bread, a stick of ripe mango

The NHS guidance recommends moving to mashed and soft lumpy foods from around 7 months. If you started weaning with smooth purees, this is the point to begin introducing imperfect texture into your purees. A good technique: mash rather than blend, or blend briefly leaving some texture. Alternatively, serve the puree alongside a soft finger food so your baby experiences two textures in one meal.

Gagging is very common when lumps are first introduced, even if your baby has been happily eating smooth food for weeks. This is normal. The tongue is encountering something it has not managed before and is working out what to do. Stay calm and let your baby gag through it. Gagging reduces quickly as skills develop.

9 to 10 months: minced, chopped, and firmer self-feeding pieces

Between 9 and 10 months, most babies develop a more precise grip, called the pincer grasp, which lets them pick up smaller pieces of food between finger and thumb. This opens up a wider range of finger food options.

Foods at this stage might include:

  • Soft pieces of pasta, rice dishes, couscous
  • Small pieces of well-cooked vegetables: peas, sweetcorn, diced soft-roasted courgette
  • Minced meat or finely flaked fish in small portions
  • Scrambled or soft-cooked egg pieces
  • Small pieces of ripe fruit: diced pear, peeled and diced kiwi, diced soft peach
  • Small pieces of soft cheese such as mozzarella

By now, you can begin to move toward simplified family food. Cook vegetables a little softer than you would for adults, cut meat finely, and avoid added salt and strong seasonings. But the food itself can be very close to what the rest of the family eats.

11 to 12 months: near family food

By 11 to 12 months, most babies are ready for food that is very close to what the family eats with minimal modification. The main adjustments are still:

  • No added salt or sugar
  • No honey
  • Cut into appropriately small, soft pieces
  • Avoiding hard raw vegetables, whole grapes, whole cherry tomatoes, popcorn, and whole nuts

Beyond those rules, a 12-month-old can generally share the family meal. This is a practical milestone: shared mealtimes reduce cooking burden and help babies experience the social side of eating, which supports positive food relationships long term.

Introducing new textures alongside familiar ones

A reliable strategy for moving through textures without resistance is to pair a new texture with a familiar and accepted flavour. If your baby loves carrot puree, try offering lightly mashed carrot next to the puree. If they love dal, serve it at a slightly coarser mash rather than blended. Keeping the flavour familiar while changing the texture reduces the total number of things that are new at once, which lowers the chance of refusal.

This same principle applies when offering new finger foods. Place them alongside accepted foods at the same meal. Your baby may ignore the new texture for many meals before trying it. That is fine. Repeated exposure to a food, even without eating it, contributes to eventual acceptance.

What to do if your baby gags a lot on new textures

Frequent gagging when moving to new textures is common and not a reason to retreat back to smooth food. A few things that help:

  • Slow down the transition. Rather than switching abruptly from smooth to lumpy, add a tiny amount of soft texture to a smooth puree and increase it incrementally over days.
  • Check the food is genuinely soft. Many texture-related gag episodes happen because the food was firmer than intended. The squish test: if you cannot squish the food easily between your finger and thumb, it is too firm for your baby's current stage.
  • Let your baby touch and explore the food before it goes in their mouth. Pre-exploration reduces surprise and can make the texture less confronting.
  • Keep mealtimes calm. A tense caregiver can make a baby more anxious about food. Your relaxed response to gagging signals to your baby that everything is fine.

If your baby gags so severely that they vomit at almost every meal, or if they appear to have strong sensory aversions to any texture at all, speak to your health visitor. Feeding difficulties with a sensory component can benefit from specialist support.

Frequently asked questions

When should I move from smooth puree to lumpy food?

The NHS recommends starting to move away from perfectly smooth purees from around 7 months. Most babies are ready for soft lumps and mashed textures by 7 to 8 months. Staying on smooth purees beyond 9 months is associated with more difficulty accepting varied textures later, so progressing gradually through textures is more important than it may seem.

My baby gags a lot on textured food. Is this normal?

Yes. Gagging is a protective reflex that helps babies manage food safely in their mouths. It is very common when new textures are introduced. As long as your baby can breathe, cough, and recover within a few seconds, this is normal. Stay calm and let them work through it. Gagging reduces significantly over time as oral motor skills develop. If gagging is so frequent that your baby is distressed at every meal, or if there is any concern about choking, speak to your health visitor.

Can I offer finger foods and purees at the same time?

Absolutely. Most families combine both. You might offer a soft finger food to explore alongside a spoonful of mashed food. This gives your baby experience with self-feeding and texture variety at the same time. There is no need to choose one method exclusively.

What does near-family food mean at 12 months?

By 12 months, most babies can manage small, soft pieces of the foods the rest of the family eats, with minimal modification. This means food that is soft enough to squish between your fingers, cut into small pieces, and not seasoned with added salt or sugar. Hard raw vegetables, whole grapes, and large chunks of meat are still not appropriate, but the range of foods a 12-month-old can share at the family table is broad.

What if my baby refuses lumpy food after months of purees?

This is more common than you might think. Gradually introducing texture rather than switching abruptly helps most babies. Try adding a small amount of soft lump to a familiar smooth puree and increasing the proportion of texture gradually over days. Offering familiar-tasting food in a new texture works better than introducing both a new flavour and a new texture at the same time.

Do I need to buy special food to offer different textures?

No. Most of the textures appropriate for babies at each stage come from cooking ordinary family food to a softer consistency, mashing it to the right degree, or cutting it into appropriate shapes. Soft-cooked carrot, ripe banana, well-cooked pasta, soft-boiled egg, and lentil dal are all inexpensive and widely available foods that work across the texture stages.
