Baby flu: how to tell it apart from a cold, and what to do

0–12 months · Health · Updated July 2026 · All articles

When your baby goes from their normal self to burning hot and very unhappy in just a few hours, it is frightening. Flu can hit babies hard and fast, and knowing what you are dealing with helps you respond calmly and confidently. This guide walks you through how flu differs from a cold, what to do at home, and, crucially, when to get help without delay.

Flu vs a cold: the key differences

The single most useful thing to know is this: flu arrives quickly, and a cold does not. A cold usually develops gradually over one or two days, often starting with a runny nose before moving to a mild cough or low temperature. Flu, by contrast, can go from nothing to very unwell within a matter of hours.

Flu typically brings a high fever, often reaching 39 or 40°C, along with extreme tiredness and, in older babies, body aches. These features are not part of a typical cold. A baby with a cold will usually still have moments of playing or engaging; a baby with flu often seems flat, miserable and floppy in a way that is quite different from their normal state.

The NHS puts it plainly: flu feels much worse than a cold, even in adults who can describe their symptoms. In babies, that "feels much worse" shows up as unusual limpness, not wanting to feed, and a high fever that does not budge easily.

What flu looks like in babies

Because babies cannot tell you how they feel, you are reading their behaviour and body. The signs to watch for include a high temperature that comes on suddenly, flushed or hot skin, and a clear loss of appetite. Your baby may cry more than usual, seem difficult to comfort, or become unusually quiet and still when they are normally active. Lethargy, where they seem uninterested in things they usually enjoy, is a key signal.

Older babies (from around six months) may also tug or rub their ears, not because they have an ear infection, but because flu causes general discomfort and congestion that makes the ears feel odd. They may also be more unsettled at night as the fever tends to spike in the evenings.

Some babies with flu develop a cough, but this is not usually the main feature early on. The fever and the sudden change in how your baby seems are what distinguish flu from a cold or mild virus.

How to care for your baby at home

Most babies with flu can be managed at home with good supportive care. The priority is keeping your baby well hydrated. If you are breastfeeding, offer the breast more often than usual, as breast milk provides antibodies as well as fluids. For babies over six months who are already drinking water, offer small sips regularly throughout the day.

Paracetamol or ibuprofen can reduce fever and make your baby more comfortable. Always check the packaging for the correct dose for your baby's age and weight, and never give both at the same time without medical advice. Ibuprofen should not be given to babies under three months. Do not give aspirin to babies or children.

Keep your baby's room cool and comfortable, around 18°C if possible. Avoid overdressing them or piling on blankets when they have a fever, as this traps heat. Light clothing and a single blanket are usually enough. Let your baby rest as much as they need to.

When to seek help right away

Some signs mean you need emergency help immediately. Call 999 or go to A&E without delay if your baby:

  • Is struggling to breathe, or breathing very fast
  • Has blue or pale lips, tongue or skin
  • Has skin that is drawing in around their chest with each breath (this is called retractions)
  • Is unresponsive, floppy or very difficult to wake

Also seek urgent help (from your GP, urgent treatment centre, or 111) if your baby is under three months old with any temperature of 38°C or above, if they have a very high fever that is not coming down with paracetamol or ibuprofen, if they are refusing all fluids, or if you notice a bulging fontanelle (the soft spot on top of their head). When in doubt, call 111 and they will advise you on what to do next.

Antiviral medication

Most babies with flu do not need antiviral medicine. However, oseltamivir (Tamiflu) can be prescribed for babies over two weeks old who are considered high risk, such as those with heart or lung conditions, or during confirmed flu outbreaks. It works by reducing how long the virus is active in the body, but it is only effective if started within 48 hours of symptoms appearing.

Your GP or a hospital doctor will assess whether antiviral treatment is right for your baby. You do not need to ask for it routinely; for most healthy babies, rest, fluids and fever management are the most important treatments.

Flu vaccine for babies

The standard annual flu vaccine is not offered to all babies, but it is recommended for certain groups. Babies from six months old who have a long-term health condition (such as a heart condition, lung disease, or a condition that weakens the immune system) are eligible for an injected flu vaccine each year. Babies who live with someone who is immunocompromised may also be recommended vaccination.

From the age of two, all children in the UK are offered the nasal spray flu vaccine as part of the routine childhood immunisation programme. If you think your baby might qualify for early vaccination, speak to your GP or health visitor. They can check your baby's eligibility and arrange the vaccine before flu season begins.

Frequently asked questions

How do I tell the difference between flu and a cold in my baby?

The biggest sign is speed: flu comes on within hours and causes high fever, extreme tiredness and, in older babies, body aches. A cold creeps in over a day or two and mainly affects the nose and throat. If your baby goes from fine to very unwell within a few hours, with a temperature over 39°C and unusual listlessness, flu is more likely.

What temperature counts as a fever in flu?

Any temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or above counts as a fever. In flu, it often rises quickly to 39 or 40°C. NHS guidance says any fever in a baby under three months needs same-day medical attention, regardless of how unwell they seem.

Can babies get the seasonal flu vaccine?

Yes. Eligible babies from six months can receive an injected flu vaccine, usually offered to those with underlying health conditions. From age two, all children are offered the nasal spray as part of the NHS childhood schedule. Ask your GP or health visitor whether your baby qualifies.

What is Tamiflu and when is it used for babies?

Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) is an antiviral medicine that can reduce how long flu lasts and how severe it is. It is sometimes prescribed for high-risk babies over two weeks old, but it needs to start within 48 hours of symptoms beginning to be effective. Your GP or hospital doctor will decide whether it is appropriate for your baby.

How long does flu last in babies?

Most babies improve within seven to ten days, though tiredness can linger for up to two weeks even after the fever has gone. If your baby seems to be getting worse after the first three or four days rather than slowly improving, contact your GP to rule out a secondary infection such as a chest or ear infection.

Should my baby's siblings stay home from nursery if baby has flu?

Siblings who are well can usually continue going to childcare. If they develop flu symptoms themselves, keep them home and follow the same guidance. Flu spreads mainly through droplets from coughs and sneezes, so encouraging good handwashing for everyone in the household is the most practical way to reduce spread.
