Finger foods for babies: safe shapes, textures and ideas for 6 to 12 months

Feeding · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Finger foods are one of the most talked-about topics in baby feeding, and also one of the most anxiety-provoking. Choking is a real concern and a reasonable one. But understanding exactly what makes a finger food safe, and what the difference is between the gagging you will almost certainly see and actual choking, makes the whole experience a great deal less stressful.

This guide covers gagging versus choking, safe shapes and textures from 6 to 12 months, how the type of food your baby can manage changes as they grow, specific ideas at each stage, and a clear list of foods to avoid in the first year.

Gagging versus choking: the most important distinction

Every parent who starts offering finger foods will witness their baby gagging. It looks alarming. The face scrunches, the mouth opens wide, the baby makes a retching or coughing sound and may go red. In the first weeks of weaning, it can happen multiple times per meal.

Gagging is normal and protective. It is a reflex that pushes food forward in the mouth when it reaches the back before the baby has properly positioned it for swallowing. In young babies, the gag reflex is triggered quite far forward in the mouth, which means even small amounts of food can trigger it. Over the first weeks and months of eating, the reflex migrates backward as oral motor skills improve and gagging becomes less frequent. You do not need to intervene during gagging. Stay calm, make eye contact, and let your baby work through it.

Choking is different and requires immediate action. A choking baby goes quiet or makes very little sound because the airway is partially or fully blocked. They cannot cough effectively, they may turn blue or go limp, and they look genuinely distressed. Choking is a medical emergency.

Every caregiver should learn the infant choking first-aid sequence before starting solids. For babies under 1: give five firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand while the baby faces down over your forearm. If the obstruction does not clear, turn the baby over and give five chest thrusts. Call emergency services immediately. Most infant first-aid courses cover this in detail, and taking one before starting weaning is strongly recommended.

The squish test: the most useful safety tool

For any finger food you plan to offer a baby under 10 months, apply the squish test before it reaches the tray. Hold the piece of food between your finger and thumb and press firmly. If it squishes into a paste with minimal effort, it is appropriate. If it resists, springs back, or requires real force to crush, it is too firm.

This test works because a baby's gums, even without teeth, are capable of breaking down anything that squishes easily under gentle finger pressure. What a baby cannot break down with their gums, but then swallows, is what causes choking problems. The squish test catches most dangerous textures before they reach your baby's mouth.

As babies approach 9 to 10 months and early teeth begin to appear, the range of safe textures expands. The squish test becomes slightly less strict: food that offers a little resistance but still breaks down is now manageable. By 12 months, near-family food texture is appropriate.

Safe shapes: sticks and strips, not rounds

Shape is the other critical safety variable. Round or coin-shaped foods are the highest choking risk because a round piece can lodge neatly over the opening to the airway, creating a seal. Stick or strip shapes do not have this property.

The safest finger food shape for babies under 12 months is a strip or spear roughly the length and width of an adult finger. The baby grips this in their fist with a portion sticking out at the top, bites off a small amount, and lets the larger portion drop. This is the shape used in baby-led weaning from the start, and it works because it gives the baby control.

For round foods, always cut or prepare them to remove the round cross-section:

  • Grapes: cut in half lengthways, then each half again lengthways to make quarters
  • Cherry tomatoes: cut in quarters lengthways
  • Blueberries: halved or quartered
  • Strawberries: offered by the hull end with the berry bitten into by the baby, or halved lengthways
  • Peas: lightly crush them so they are no longer round and firm

6 to 7 months: very soft, large grip pieces

At 6 months, the palmar or raking grasp means babies close their whole hand around food. Food must be large enough to stick out of the fist and soft enough to squish easily. Good first finger foods:

  • Ripe banana: held as half a banana with the peel left on as a grip, or cut into large chunks
  • Well-steamed sweet potato spears (steamed until very tender)
  • Well-steamed broccoli florets (offered held by the stem)
  • Soft-steamed carrot sticks (steam until a knife goes through with no resistance)
  • Ripe avocado spears
  • Soft silken tofu pieces
  • Strips of tender slow-cooked chicken thigh
  • Flaked well-cooked salmon or trout in large soft pieces
  • Fingers of soft bread (a day old is slightly easier to grip than very fresh)
  • Soft toast strips
  • Ripe mango spears
  • Well-steamed green bean pieces

8 to 9 months: slightly firmer options and early pincer grip foods

From around 8 months, babies begin to develop a more pincer-like grip, though it is not yet refined. They can manage slightly firmer soft foods and begin to pick up somewhat smaller pieces. Foods to add at this stage:

  • Soft pasta shapes (penne, fusilli, rigatoni, cooked very soft)
  • Well-cooked potato cubes (mash with a fork to double-check softness)
  • Small soft pieces of well-cooked chicken or fish
  • Scrambled egg pieces
  • Soft cubes of mild cheese such as mozzarella
  • Quartered grapes (lengthways)
  • Soft ripe pear pieces
  • Soft-cooked apple pieces (cooked until tender)
  • Soft pieces of ripe kiwi
  • Lightly crushed peas
  • Diced soft-roasted courgette or aubergine

10 to 12 months: close to soft family food

By 10 to 12 months, the pincer grasp is well established, most babies have some teeth, and the range of safe textures has expanded significantly. Babies at this age can generally share a version of the family meal with minimal modification:

  • Small pieces of soft-cooked meat (not tough, chewy cuts)
  • Most cooked vegetables cut into small pieces
  • Soft cooked fish
  • Rice, couscous, and small grain dishes
  • Beans and lentils
  • Soft cubes of most fruit
  • Small pieces of bread, crackers (check they do not break into hard shards)
  • Bite-sized pieces of pancake, French toast, soft oat-based foods

Foods to avoid giving babies under 12 months

Some foods should not be offered to babies under 12 months regardless of texture:

  • Honey: risk of infant botulism, never before 12 months in any form
  • Whole nuts: choking hazard for all children under 5; ground nut butters spread thinly are fine from 6 months
  • Hard raw vegetables: raw carrot sticks, raw celery, raw apple in large pieces, raw pear in large pieces all fail the squish test
  • Whole round fruits: whole grapes, whole cherry tomatoes, whole blueberries as noted above
  • Popcorn: unpredictable shape and texture, can break into hard pieces
  • Marshmallows: soft but expand in moisture and can block the airway
  • Large globs of nut butter: very sticky and difficult to clear from the airway; a thin spread on food is fine
  • Added salt and sugar: not a choking concern, but not appropriate for babies under 12 months nutritionally
  • Cows milk as a main drink: not appropriate to replace breast milk or formula before 12 months

Frequently asked questions

When can I start offering finger foods?

Soft finger foods can be introduced from 6 months, alongside or instead of purees, as soon as your baby shows the three signs of weaning readiness: sitting upright with minimal support, good head and neck control, and loss of the tongue-thrust reflex. At this age, babies use a raking or palmar grasp and can manage large, very soft pieces. You do not need to wait until 7 or 8 months to introduce finger foods.

How do I know if a finger food texture is safe?

Use the squish test. If you can squish the food easily between your finger and thumb with no real effort, it is appropriate for a baby under 10 months. If it resists squishing, it is too firm. Food that squishes easily in an adult's fingers will squish easily in a baby's gum, even before teeth arrive. Gums are surprisingly effective at breaking down soft food.

What is the difference between gagging and choking?

Gagging is a normal, protective reflex. The baby coughs, sputters, makes retching sounds, and may go red. They can breathe, make noise, and recover within seconds. Gagging protects the airway by pushing food forward before it is ready to be swallowed. Choking is different: the baby goes quiet or makes very little sound, cannot cough effectively, may turn blue, and is in visible distress. Choking is a blocked airway and requires immediate first aid action.

Do babies need teeth to eat finger foods?

No. Babies do not need teeth to eat soft finger foods. Gums are harder than they look and can break down soft food effectively. Many babies do not get their first teeth until 8 to 10 months or later, yet can manage soft finger foods from 6 months. Teeth do help with firmer foods, so the progression to slightly firmer textures often follows the appearance of teeth, but soft food is fully manageable without them.

Are rice cakes safe for babies?

Plain, unsalted rice cakes are often suggested as a finger food, but they dissolve into a sticky paste in the mouth rather than breaking into manageable pieces, which some babies find easier and others find more difficult. They are low in nutrition and not the most useful weaning food. If you offer them, choose unsalted varieties and offer them one at a time rather than in a large piece that could break off unexpectedly.

What foods should I avoid giving my baby as finger foods?

Avoid whole grapes, whole cherry tomatoes, whole blueberries (cut all round foods lengthways into quarters), hard raw vegetables such as raw carrot or celery, whole nuts, large seeds, popcorn, marshmallows, and large globs of nut butter. Also avoid any food with added salt or honey. Foods that break into hard shards, like some crackers, can be an issue; check how they behave in your fingers before offering them.
