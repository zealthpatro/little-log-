Your baby at 11 months: development, play and what to expect

Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Eleven months feels like standing at the edge of something big. Your baby is gaining confidence on their feet, communicating with growing clarity, feeding themselves with real skill and watching everything you do with intense, imitative attention. The first birthday is just around the corner, and the person your baby is becoming is unmistakably, wonderfully themselves.

This guide covers what most babies are doing around 11 months. Developmental timelines are wide, and most variations within that range are completely normal. If something specific is worrying you, your health visitor or GP is always the right person to speak to.

Motor development: brief moments of standing alone

Walking independently is close for many babies, but the approach to it looks different for everyone.

  • Standing independently for brief moments. Many 11-month-olds are achieving fleeting moments of free-standing, letting go of the furniture and balancing for a second or two before grabbing on again. Some are doing this regularly; others have not yet let go.
  • Walking with one hand held. A good number of babies at this age are walking confidently when holding just one hand, which is a clear step toward independence. The transition to walking alone typically happens when a baby has the confidence as much as the physical ability.
  • Climbing. Some 11-month-olds are beginning to climb, whether onto the sofa, up stairs, or onto any low furniture in reach. Supervision and safe environments matter more than ever.

Communication: gestures are doing the heavy lifting

At 11 months your baby's communication is rich, even if words have not fully arrived yet.

  • Pointing, waving and clapping. These gestures are the main communication tools at this age. Your baby points to request things and to share interest. They wave hello and goodbye. They clap in response to approval or excitement. Each of these is a meaningful communicative act.
  • Following simple instructions. Your baby is understanding far more than they can say. A simple request like "can you give that to me?" or "wave bye-bye" is often understood and acted on.
  • Reaching with intent. Reaching toward something with a sound or a meaningful look is a clear communicative request, even without words.

Language: first words may be here

The boundary between babble and language is crossed differently for every baby.

  • First words may have emerged. Some 11-month-olds have one, two or a handful of consistent words. Others are still building toward that. Both are normal. A word counts if your baby uses the same sound consistently to mean the same thing, such as "da" always meaning their father.
  • Rich, communicative babble. Even without words, your baby's babble is increasingly conversational in rhythm and tone. They use rising intonation for questions and emphatic patterns to insist.
  • Understanding familiar words. Your baby's passive vocabulary (the words they understand) is well ahead of what they can say. They likely recognise the names of familiar people, common objects and simple action words.

Social development: imitation and social referencing

Your baby is a devoted observer and imitator of the people they love.

  • Imitation play is growing. Watching you stir a pot and wanting to stir too, holding a phone to their ear because they have seen you do it, brushing their own hair: imitation is an 11-month-old's primary learning strategy. Offering safe household objects as props encourages this beautifully.
  • Social referencing. When something uncertain or new happens, your baby looks to your face for a cue. Your calm, reassuring expression tells them it is safe to explore. Your worried expression may make them hesitate. This is social referencing and it is a sophisticated cognitive skill.
  • Strong attachment behaviours. Separation anxiety may still be active, and your baby may have a very clear preference for one primary carer, particularly when tired or unwell. This is healthy attachment, not a sign of something wrong.

Feeding: self-feeding at almost every meal

Mealtimes at 11 months are increasingly independent, even if they are also increasingly messy.

  • Self-feeding at most meals. Your baby can pick up finger foods, move food around their tray, and get a significant proportion of their meal into their mouth without help. This independence is worth encouraging even when a spoon would be faster.
  • Family food texture. Most 11-month-olds can manage food similar to what the family eats, modified where needed: soft, not too salty, cut into manageable pieces. There is no need for special baby food at this stage.
  • Moving toward a cup. If your baby is still using a bottle, now is a good time to introduce a cup as the primary vessel, especially for drinks other than milk. The 12-month review is often when health visitors discuss transitioning away from bottles.

Sleep: two naps, most of the time

Most 11-month-olds are still on two naps a day, though some are beginning to show signs that the second nap is becoming harder to settle. The transition to one nap usually happens between 13 and 18 months. Night sleep remains variable between babies.

Play ideas for an 11-month-old

Play at 11 months is richly social and imitative.

  • Household object play. Safe duplicates of things you use, such as a small brush, a wooden spoon, a soft cloth, let your baby imitate your activities. This is some of the most genuinely engaging play for this age.
  • Simple sorting and stacking. Stacking rings, simple shape sorters and nesting cups offer just enough challenge to be rewarding without being frustrating.
  • Books with pointing. Look at board books together and point to pictures, naming them. Your baby may start pointing too, especially at familiar things. These shared pointing moments are wonderful for language and joint attention.
  • Ball rolling. Sitting on the floor and rolling a ball back and forth is a perfect early social game that also builds motor skills and turn-taking.
  • Music and movement. Simple songs with actions, clapping and bouncing are still enormously enjoyable. Your baby may start to anticipate the movements before they happen.

Preparing for the 12-month developmental review

The 12-month review with your health visitor is a good opportunity to discuss development, feeding and sleep. It is helpful to have a sense of where your baby is across these areas, but do not feel you need to have answers to every question perfectly prepared. The health visitor's job is to support you, not to test you.

When to talk to your health visitor or GP

Do mention it to your health visitor if by around 12 months your baby:

  • Is not using any gestures such as pointing or waving
  • Does not respond to their name
  • Is not babbling with varied sounds
  • Has no words at all by 16 months
  • Is not pulling to stand or cruising
  • Has lost skills they previously had

Frequently asked questions

Should my 11-month-old be standing alone?

Many 11-month-olds can stand independently for a few seconds, but it is not a universal milestone at this age. Standing without support typically develops between 9 and 12 months. If your baby is cruising confidently and pulling to stand well, they are right on track.

Is it normal to not have any words at 11 months?

Yes, completely. First words can arrive anywhere from 9 to 15 months. Many babies have no clear words at 11 months and go on to develop language perfectly typically. What to look for at this age is communicative intent: pointing, waving and varied babble.

What is social referencing?

Social referencing is when your baby looks to your face for cues about how to feel about something new or uncertain. Your calm expression tells them it is safe to explore. It is a sophisticated cognitive and social skill emerging strongly around 10 to 12 months.

How much should an 11-month-old be eating?

Three small meals a day with one or two snacks is a typical pattern at 11 months, alongside breast milk or formula. Appetite varies day to day and that is normal. Follow your baby's hunger and fullness cues rather than aiming for a specific volume.

Why does my 11-month-old copy everything I do?

Imitation is how babies learn. At 11 months your baby is carefully watching what you do and trying to replicate it. Offering safe household objects to imitate with supports this development and is more engaging for them than purpose-built toys.

When should I worry about walking?

Independent walking is expected to be achieved by 18 months. At 11 months there is a very wide range of normal, from babies already taking first steps to those still building up to cruising. If your baby is not walking independently by 18 months, mention it to your health visitor.
