Teaching toddlers to share: realistic expectations and strategies that work

Behaviour · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Two toddlers at a playdate. One has a toy car. The other wants it. The automatic adult response is "share with your friend." Before you say it, it is worth knowing that genuine sharing is a skill most children are not developmentally ready for until they are 3 or 4. The goal for the toddler years is not sharing. It is something more achievable: turn-taking.

Why young toddlers cannot truly share

Sharing in its fullest sense requires three separate cognitive capacities. The first is theory of mind: understanding what another person thinks or feels. The second is empathy: caring about that experience. The third is impulse control: the ability to override your own immediate desire in favour of someone else's need. None of these are reliably in place before the age of 3 or 4.

Theory of mind, the ability to hold in your mind that another person has a perspective different from yours, is typically not consolidated until around age 4. Impulse control, governed by the prefrontal cortex, is one of the last brain functions to mature. Asking a 2-year-old to voluntarily give up a toy because another child wants it is asking for a cognitive performance their brain simply cannot yet deliver.

A toddler who refuses to share is not being selfish or badly raised. They are on track. The expectation is the problem, not the child.

Object permanence and ownership

Toddlers are still consolidating object permanence: the understanding that an object continues to exist even when it leaves their sight. This milestone develops across the first two years but is not fully stable until around 18 to 24 months. For a younger toddler, handing something to another child can feel, at the level of felt experience, as though the object has vanished permanently.

Alongside this, the concept of ownership is one of the most exciting new discoveries of toddlerhood. "Mine" is one of the early words many children produce because the idea that things can belong to a person is genuinely new. The categories of mine, yours, and theirs take years to build and stabilise. Expecting a toddler to cheerfully release an item into someone else's hands while these concepts are still being constructed is asking for more than the developmental stage allows.

Why forced sharing often backfires

The instinct to insist on sharing comes from a good place. The outcome, though, often runs counter to the intention. When an adult intervenes in a toy dispute by insisting a child hand the toy over, the sense of threat around that object typically intensifies. The moment someone tries to take something from you, you want it more. This is not a toddler quirk. It is basic human psychology, regardless of age.

Second, the lesson learned is compliance with authority, not empathy for the other child. The child who surrenders a toy under pressure responds to the power dynamic, not to a genuine understanding of the other child's feelings. Research from developmental psychologists suggests this is one reason why forced sharing does not reliably build the generosity it is intended to teach.

Third, the child can begin to associate the other child with the loss of the object, creating resentment rather than connection. Playdates that repeatedly end in forced sharing can make the other child feel like a threat rather than a friend. The opposite of the intended outcome.

Turn-taking as the achievable goal

If sharing is beyond most toddlers' developmental reach right now, turn-taking is well within it. Turn-taking has a simple, concrete structure that even young toddlers can grasp: you have it for a while, then I have it for a while. There is no permanent loss. The object comes back. The rules apply equally to everyone.

This is the skill worth building in the toddler years. It is also the foundation on which later, more nuanced sharing and genuine generosity are built. Children who learn reliable turn-taking early are better placed to develop the more complex social skill of sharing as their cognitive abilities mature.

The timer method explained

One of the most effective tools for teaching turn-taking is a visual timer. The timer, rather than the parent, becomes the neutral authority that signals when a turn ends. The parent is not the person taking the toy away. The rule is.

Set the timer for 3 to 5 minutes. The child who has the toy keeps it until the timer rings. When it rings, the toy moves to the other child and the timer is reset. Both children learn that the same rule applies to them equally, which removes the sense of favouritism or unfairness.

The visual element matters, particularly for younger toddlers. Seeing the time actively disappearing is more concrete than being told "two more minutes." Sand timers and colour-wheel countdown timers both work well at this age. The child can see the end approaching rather than having to take an adult's word for when it will arrive.

Scripts that help

Children benefit from having specific language to use when they want to hold onto something. Adults can introduce these phrases outside of conflict moments and then offer them as gentle reminders when disputes arise.

Useful phrases include: "You can have it when I am done," "I am still using it," and "It will be your turn soon." These phrases acknowledge the other child's desire while also asserting a legitimate claim. They give the child a way to hold their ground without snatching or screaming.

Parents can also model turn-taking in their own everyday actions, narrating as they go. "I am using the scissors right now. I will give them to you when I am done." Watching adults navigate the same dynamic, calmly and without drama, is one of the most effective teachers available to a young child.

Playgroup and playdate strategies

Before guests arrive, offer your toddler the chance to put away a few special toys they would find particularly hard to let go of. This removes conflict before it starts, without forcing anything. What is put away is not available. What is left out is on the table for turn-taking.

Setting expectations before the playdate, not in the heat of a dispute, is far more effective. A simple, warm briefing at home helps: "Your friend is coming today. If you both want the same toy, we will use the timer." This primes the toddler for what is coming without putting them under immediate pressure.

At playgroups, offering parallel setups, where each child has access to the same type of material or toy, reduces competition significantly. Two trays of playdough, two sets of blocks, two bowls of water. The novelty is available to all without any specific object becoming a flashpoint.

Parallel play is developmentally normal for under-2s

Children under 2 typically engage in parallel play: playing alongside another child rather than with them. This is developmentally expected and entirely appropriate. Two toddlers sitting near each other, each focused on their own activity, occasionally glancing at what the other is doing and sometimes imitating them from a distance, is healthy and productive social contact at this age.

Cooperative play, the kind that involves negotiation, role-taking, and sharing in its fuller sense, does not reliably emerge until around 2.5 to 3 years old. Trying to push children into cooperative play before they are ready tends to produce friction rather than friendship.

Knowing this can ease the pressure parents sometimes feel at playgroups when other children appear to be playing together more smoothly. Proximity and parallel engagement is the real developmental task at this age. It is enough.

Frequently asked questions

My toddler grabs toys from other children. How do I handle it?

Calmly intervene and return the toy to the other child. Name the feeling without shaming: "You really wanted that toy" is more helpful than "That was naughty." Offer an alternative if one is available and stay close to coach rather than to punish. The grabbing impulse is developmentally normal at this age, and the response is to redirect, not to shame.

Should I force my toddler to share at family gatherings?

Forced sharing tends to create more resistance rather than more generosity. A gentler approach is to prepare your toddler in advance, frame any sharing as turn-taking with a clear expectation of getting the object back, and invite family members to model patience with the process. Most relatives are relieved when you explain what is developmentally realistic at this age.

Is it okay for my toddler to have toys that are theirs and not for sharing?

Yes, completely. Having some personal belongings that are not available to others is developmentally healthy and builds a sense of self and ownership. Putting special toys away before playdates removes the source of conflict without forcing anything. The child is not refusing to share. The toy is simply not in play for that session.

When will my toddler start sharing more willingly?

Most children begin to share more naturally and spontaneously between ages 3 and 4, as empathy, language, and impulse control develop together. The process is gradual and cannot be reliably rushed. Pushing harder before the child is developmentally ready usually creates more resistance, not less. Trust the pace.

The other child's parent expects my toddler to share immediately. What do I do?

A calm, matter-of-fact explanation is usually enough: "They are still learning turn-taking, which is right on track for this age." Holding the developmental reality clearly and warmly, without apology, often diffuses the situation. Most parents are also relieved to hear that the expectation itself is typically too high for both children's age.

My toddler shares fine at home but not at playgroup. Why?

Group settings are more stimulating and higher-stakes than home. The novelty and excitement increase the drive to hold onto objects. This is not regression. It is the natural difficulty of applying a developing skill in a harder environment. The child is not going backwards. They are encountering the limits of a skill still being built, in one of the more challenging places to practise it.
