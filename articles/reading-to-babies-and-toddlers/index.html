Reading to babies and toddlers: why it matters and how to make it work

Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Few things you can do with your baby are as simple or as powerful as reading to them. The evidence is clear and consistent: children who are read to from early in life have larger vocabularies, better comprehension and stronger literacy skills when they reach school. The activity itself is free, takes as little as five minutes, and doubles as warm, connected time together.

This guide covers why reading matters from the very beginning, what kinds of books work at different ages, how to read with a baby who will not sit still, and how to build a reading habit that sticks.

Why language exposure in early life matters so much

Long before a child can read a word themselves, they are building the linguistic foundations that make reading possible. Vocabulary, understanding of sentence structure, familiarity with narrative, and awareness that spoken words correspond to written ones are all built through years of language exposure before school begins.

Research by Hart and Risley in the 1990s identified a striking gap in the number of words children heard before age 3, which predicted measurable differences in vocabulary and school readiness. Later research has refined and debated their findings, but the core message has held up: rich language exposure in the early years matters. Reading aloud is one of the most effective ways to provide it.

When you read to your baby, you expose them to a different kind of language than everyday conversation. Books use a wider vocabulary, more complex sentence structures, and more varied topics than the average exchange about nappy changes and lunch. This breadth of language is part of what makes reading so valuable.

When to start: from day one

You can start reading to your baby from the very first day of life. A newborn cannot understand the words, of course, but they are attuned to the sound of your voice. They hear its rhythm, warmth and cadence. Being held close and hearing a familiar voice is comforting and connecting, regardless of whether the voice is reciting a picture book or reading a novel aloud.

Starting early also means reading becomes a normal, expected part of daily life before your baby has an opinion about it. Many families find that a baby who has been read to since birth is more receptive to books at 9 or 12 months than one for whom books are a newer experience.

What kinds of books work: by age

The best book for a baby is often the one in front of them, but different types work better at different stages.

  • Newborn to 3 months: high-contrast and sensory. Newborns' visual acuity is still developing; they see best at a distance of about 20 to 30 cm and are most drawn to high contrast. Black and white board books with bold simple patterns are genuinely engaging for very young babies. Any book you enjoy reading aloud also works, because your voice and face are the main event.
  • 3 to 6 months: colour and faces. As colour vision matures, books with clear, colourful images of faces, everyday objects and animals start to capture attention. Simple text or no text at all is fine. Your commentary on the pictures is the language input.
  • 6 to 12 months: touchable, durable and interactive. Board books that survive being chewed, dropped and thrown are ideal. Touch-and-feel books, books with simple repetitive text and books with clear pictures of familiar things all work well. At this age, books are objects to interact with as much as texts to listen to.
  • 12 to 24 months: repetition and rhythm. Toddlers love repetitive books with predictable patterns they can anticipate. Repetition is not boring to a toddler; it is how they learn. Books with rhyme, rhythm or a repeated phrase that the child can fill in are particularly engaging.

How to read with a wriggly baby

One of the most common frustrations parents describe is trying to read to a baby who grabs the book, tries to eat it, or simply will not look at the pages. This is completely normal, and it does not mean reading is not working.

  • Follow their lead. If your baby grabs the book, let them hold it while you name what they are touching. If they flip to the last page, go with it. The goal is a positive association with books, not a cover-to-cover read.
  • Short is fine. One page, two pages, one picture: all of these count. A one-minute book interaction is better than no book interaction, and better than a battle to finish a story.
  • Make it a conversation. Point to pictures and name them. Ask "where is the dog?" and wait. Describe what you see. Respond to whatever your baby does or says. The back-and-forth is as valuable as the text.
  • Try different positions. Some babies settle better on a lap; others prefer lying beside you while you hold the book overhead. Try the bath, the floor, the pram.

Dialogic reading: a more active approach for toddlers

From around 18 months, when toddlers are beginning to talk and can engage more actively with pictures and stories, an approach called dialogic reading can significantly increase the language benefits of book-sharing.

Dialogic reading turns reading into a conversation. Instead of reading straight through the text, you:

  • Ask open questions about the pictures: "What is the bear doing?"
  • Wait genuinely for a response, even if it is just a point or a sound
  • Repeat and expand what your child says: "Yes, the bear is eating! He is eating honey."
  • Praise attempts at engagement warmly

Research on dialogic reading consistently shows larger vocabulary gains than passive reading aloud, particularly for children in the toddler years. You do not need to turn every book into a full lesson; even occasional questions and expansions add up over time.

Building a reading habit that sticks

The most effective way to make reading a lasting part of family life is to build it into an existing routine rather than treating it as a separate activity to fit in.

  • Bedtime is a natural anchor. Many families read one or two books as part of the bedtime routine from early infancy. This association between books and winding down is a powerful one that can last for years.
  • Make books accessible. Keeping a small basket of board books within your baby's reach from around 6 months, so they can pull them out themselves, builds an independent relationship with books alongside the shared reading.
  • Use the library. A library card is one of the best things you can get for a young child. It provides variety without cost and models that books are a normal part of life.
  • Read what you enjoy too. Your enthusiasm is contagious. If you are bored rigid by a particular book, your baby will sense it. Finding books you genuinely enjoy reading makes the whole thing more sustainable.

Does it matter if you read in a different language?

If you speak a different language at home from the one in your local library's picture books, reading in your own language is entirely valid and valuable. Reading in any language builds the same foundational language skills and the same love of books. Bilingual families can read in both languages, alternating by day or by book.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start reading to my baby?

You can start from day one. Newborns cannot understand the words but they respond to the sound of your voice, its rhythm and warmth. Early reading is less about the content and more about shared time, your voice, and the foundations of a habit.

What kind of books are best for babies under 6 months?

High-contrast black and white books, simple bold patterns and books with large clear images are ideal for young babies whose visual acuity is still developing. Any book you enjoy reading aloud works too, because your voice is the main draw at this age.

What is dialogic reading?

Dialogic reading turns shared book time into a conversation. Instead of reading the text straight through, you ask questions about the pictures, respond to what your child says, and expand on their answers. It is particularly effective for toddlers from about 18 months.

How do I read to a baby who will not sit still?

Follow their lead. Name one picture and close the book. Read two pages and put it down. Babies and toddlers absorb more than you might think even from brief, partial reads. The goal is a positive association with books, not completion.

How many books does a child need?

Quantity matters less than accessibility and variety. A small collection of board books your baby can reach and hold is better than a large library stored out of reach. Libraries are an excellent way to provide variety without cost.

Does reading to a baby really make a difference to language development?

Yes. Research consistently shows that children who are read to regularly have larger vocabularies, better language comprehension and stronger literacy skills when they start school. The effect begins from the very earliest months.
