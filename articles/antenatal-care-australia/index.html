Antenatal care in Australia: your options and what is covered

Pregnancy · 1st trimester · Reviewed 15 June 2026 · All articles

Navigating antenatal care for the first time can feel overwhelming, particularly when you discover there is not one single path but several, each with different providers, costs and levels of continuity. In Australia, pregnant women can choose between public hospital care, GP-led shared care, private obstetric care, or midwifery group practice, depending on their location, preferences and circumstances. This article explains how each pathway works, what routine tests and checks are included across the pregnancy, and what costs Medicare covers.

Public hospital antenatal care

All pregnant women in Australia are entitled to antenatal care through the public hospital system at no out-of-pocket cost. You do not need private health insurance to access it. Care in the public system is typically led by midwives, with obstetricians involved when a complication arises or when the pregnancy is considered higher risk. Your antenatal appointments will usually take place at a hospital clinic or an associated community health centre, and you will generally see different clinicians at each visit rather than a single named provider throughout your pregnancy.

The public system provides a full schedule of appointments, blood tests and ultrasound scans. The birth itself takes place at the public hospital, and postnatal care is also included. For straightforward pregnancies, the public pathway provides comprehensive, evidence-based care at no cost, and it is the most commonly used option across the country.

Shared care with your GP

Shared care is a popular arrangement in which your regular GP takes responsibility for most of your antenatal appointments, while a public hospital provides blood tests, ultrasound scans and the birth itself. Your GP and the hospital work together so that your records are shared and any concerns can be escalated quickly.

The main appeal of shared care is continuity: rather than seeing a different midwife or doctor at every hospital clinic visit, you have most of your appointments with a GP you already know and trust. This can feel reassuring, particularly for first-time parents. Not every GP is accredited to provide shared care, so it is worth asking your GP early in your pregnancy whether they offer it. If they do not, they can usually refer you to a nearby practice that does. The cost to you is typically just the standard GP consultation gap, if any, and the hospital portion remains free.

Private obstetric care

Choosing a private obstetrician means you are seen by the same specialist throughout your pregnancy, at the birth and during the immediate postnatal period. This level of continuity and personal attention appeals to many families, particularly those with a history of complications or those who want a named specialist present at the birth. Private obstetricians practise from private hospitals or from rooms that are affiliated with both public and private hospitals.

Medicare provides a rebate for obstetrician consultations and standard ultrasound scans, but private obstetric care nearly always involves a gap fee: the difference between what Medicare (and private health insurance, if you have it) covers and what the obstetrician charges. The size of the gap varies between practitioners and depends on your level of private cover. It is sensible to ask any obstetrician you are considering about their fees at the first consultation, and to check with your health fund what your policy covers for obstetrics before making a decision.

Midwifery group practice and caseload midwifery

Midwifery group practice (MGP), sometimes called caseload midwifery, is a model in which the same midwife or a small team of midwives provides care throughout pregnancy, during labour and birth, and in the postnatal period. It is increasingly available at larger public hospitals around the country, though access varies significantly by location. Because MGP is provided through the public hospital system, it is free to eligible women. Research consistently supports this model for improving outcomes and satisfaction, and it is particularly valued by women who want continuity of carer without the cost of private obstetrics.

Places in MGP programs are often limited and tend to fill quickly. If this model appeals to you, contact your local public hospital maternity unit as early in your pregnancy as possible to enquire about availability and eligibility criteria.

What Medicare covers

Medicare, Australia's universal health insurance scheme, covers a broad range of antenatal services. GP consultations attract a Medicare rebate, and standard ultrasound scans (including the dating scan and the morphology scan) are covered for eligible women. Obstetrician consultations also attract Medicare rebates, though as noted above there is typically a gap to pay with a private specialist. Pathology services, including the blood tests that form part of the standard antenatal schedule, are covered by Medicare when requested by your GP or treating specialist.

It is worth noting that Medicare coverage applies regardless of whether you are an Australian citizen or a permanent resident, provided you are enrolled. If you hold a temporary visa, your entitlements may differ, and it is worth checking your specific situation with Services Australia.

Routine antenatal tests and checks

Regardless of which care pathway you choose, the schedule of tests and appointments across a typical pregnancy follows a broadly similar pattern. At your first visit, usually in the first trimester, a full set of blood tests is taken. These assess your full blood count, blood type and rhesus factor, immunity to rubella, and screen for hepatitis B, HIV and syphilis. These results provide a baseline and allow your care team to identify any issues that need attention early.

Ultrasound scans include an early dating scan to confirm the pregnancy and estimate the due date, followed by a morphology scan (also called the anatomy scan) at around 18 to 20 weeks to assess fetal development in detail. First-trimester combined screening for chromosomal conditions, including nuchal translucency measurement and a blood test, is offered to all women who wish to have it.

Gestational diabetes screening is offered to all pregnant women in Australia between 24 and 28 weeks, using either a glucose challenge test or an oral glucose tolerance test. This is a broader approach than in some other countries, where screening is offered only to women with identified risk factors. Early identification of gestational diabetes allows for dietary guidance and, where needed, medication to protect the health of both mother and baby.

The pertussis (whooping cough) vaccine is recommended in every pregnancy, usually given between 20 and 32 weeks. It is funded under the National Immunisation Program and is available at no cost. The vaccine works by producing antibodies in the mother that pass to the baby through the placenta before birth, giving the newborn some protection in the early weeks of life before they are old enough to receive their own vaccinations. Partners and close family members who have not had a pertussis booster in the past ten years are also encouraged to be vaccinated.

Choosing the right pathway for you

The best care pathway is the one that suits your health needs, personal preferences and practical circumstances. For a low-risk pregnancy, any of the pathways described above can provide excellent care. If you have a pre-existing health condition, a history of pregnancy complications, or a higher-risk pregnancy, your GP can advise on whether additional specialist involvement is appropriate. Cost, location, the desire for continuity with a known provider, and whether you want to give birth in a public or private hospital are all factors worth considering when making your choice.

Starting the conversation early, ideally as soon as you know you are pregnant, gives you the most options. Some pathways, such as MGP, have limited places. Private obstetricians can book up well in advance, particularly in major cities. If you are unsure where to start, your GP is the right first call: they can assess your situation, discuss the options available in your area, and refer you to the appropriate service.

Frequently asked questions

Is antenatal care free in Australia?

Public hospital antenatal care is free for all pregnant women in Australia. A Medicare card provides access to subsidised GP care. Private obstetric care involves gap fees even with Medicare and private health insurance.

What is shared care in Australian maternity?

Shared care means your antenatal appointments are split between your regular GP and a public hospital. Your GP handles most routine check-ups, while the hospital provides scans, blood tests and specialist care. It is popular because it allows continuity with a familiar GP while accessing hospital resources.

Is gestational diabetes screening universal in Australia?

Yes. In Australia, a glucose challenge test or oral glucose tolerance test is offered to all pregnant women at 24 to 28 weeks, whereas in the UK it is only offered to those with identified risk factors.

Is the whooping cough vaccine free in pregnancy in Australia?

Yes. The pertussis (whooping cough) vaccine is recommended in every pregnancy (usually between 20 and 32 weeks) and is available free under the National Immunisation Program. Immunity passes to the baby before birth, protecting them in the first weeks of life.
