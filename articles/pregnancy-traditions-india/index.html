Pregnancy traditions and beliefs in Indian culture

Pregnancy · 1st trimester · Reviewed 15 June 2026 · All articles

Pregnancy in India is embedded in a rich web of family, community and religious customs. These traditions vary considerably by region, religion, caste background and individual family practice, so no single account can speak for the full breadth of Indian experience. What follows is an overview of some widely documented practices and the beliefs behind them, alongside what current medical guidance says. Understanding where tradition and evidence align, and where they diverge, helps you make informed decisions that work for your own health and your family's values.

A tradition rooted in community and care

In many Indian families, pregnancy is understood as a time when the wider community steps in. Grandmothers, mothers-in-law, aunts and neighbours often share advice, prepare specific foods and observe particular customs alongside the expectant mother. This communal support can be genuinely beneficial: knowing that people around you are engaged in your wellbeing, that meals will be prepared and that someone will accompany you to appointments, contributes to a lower-stress pregnancy. The social fabric around pregnancy in many Indian households reflects a deep understanding that new parents need support, even if the specific practices associated with that support vary from place to place.

It is also worth noting that many traditions have practical origins. Advice to rest more, to eat certain nutritious foods and to avoid heavy physical strain during the early months reflects accumulated observation across generations. The fact that a practice is traditional does not automatically make it harmful, nor does it make it medically validated. The most useful approach is to look at each custom on its own terms.

Dietary beliefs and what the evidence says

Food is one of the areas where Indian pregnancy traditions are richest and most varied. Many families encourage pregnant women to eat more ghee, milk, dried fruits, nuts and warming spices during pregnancy. Ghee and dairy are nutrient-dense foods and form a sensible part of a varied diet. Dried fruits and nuts provide iron, zinc and healthy fats. From this perspective, many traditional dietary recommendations align reasonably well with evidence-based nutritional guidance.

Ayurvedic traditions introduce the concept of heating and cooling foods, and some families advise avoiding foods believed to be excessively heating or cooling during pregnancy. Cold beverages are sometimes discouraged based on this framework. There is no strong clinical evidence that the temperature of a drink harms a pregnancy, but the underlying concern about digestive comfort is understandable, and drinking water at a comfortable temperature is entirely reasonable.

Certain specific foods come up regularly in traditional advice. Raw or unripe papaya is often advised against during pregnancy in Indian and other South Asian traditions. Some laboratory studies have suggested that the compounds in unripe papaya could theoretically affect uterine tissue in very large amounts, though ripe papaya in ordinary dietary quantities is generally considered safe by mainstream health authorities. If you are unsure about a specific food, your midwife is the right person to ask.

Sesame seeds are another food sometimes avoided in the first trimester based on traditional belief. Again, there is no strong clinical evidence of harm from sesame seeds in normal dietary quantities, but the guidance of your midwife should take priority over any single traditional rule.

What evidence-based pregnancy nutrition does emphasise is a varied, balanced diet that includes sufficient iron, folic acid, vitamin D and iodine. Folic acid supplementation is recommended before conception and for at least the first 12 weeks of pregnancy. Vitamin D is recommended throughout pregnancy. Your midwife or GP can advise on any supplements relevant to your circumstances, including whether your dietary pattern means you need additional support for any specific nutrient.

Activity and physical customs

Many families in India advise pregnant women to avoid lifting heavy objects, making long journeys or exerting themselves physically, particularly in the first trimester when the pregnancy is considered most fragile. The concern behind this advice reflects a genuine wish to protect the pregnancy during the period before it is firmly established.

Current medical guidance on physical activity during pregnancy takes a more nuanced position. Moderate exercise throughout pregnancy is associated with a range of benefits, including better cardiovascular fitness, reduced risk of gestational diabetes and improved mood and sleep quality. Walking, swimming and pregnancy-appropriate yoga are examples of activities widely considered beneficial. What is generally advised against is very high-intensity exercise that significantly raises heart rate, or activities that risk falling or direct abdominal impact.

Avoiding genuinely heavy lifting, particularly in the first trimester, is not in conflict with medical guidance. If you have any concerns about a specific activity, raise them with your midwife, who can give you personalised advice based on your own health history.

Eclipses and other beliefs

In some Indian families, particularly in communities with strong astrological traditions, pregnant women are advised to remain indoors during a solar or lunar eclipse. This belief holds that the eclipse may affect the developing baby if the mother is exposed to it. There is no medical or scientific basis for this advice. Remaining indoors during an eclipse does not cause any harm, however, so if following this practice is important to your family or brings you peace of mind, there is no clinical reason to act against it.

Similar superstitions exist in many cultures around the world. When evaluating any belief of this kind, the key question is whether following it causes harm. If it does not, then the cultural and emotional value of the practice is a legitimate consideration alongside the medical picture.

Spiritual and devotional practices

Prayer, reading religious texts, listening to devotional music and meditation are all common during pregnancy in many Indian households. The practice known as garbha sanskar, which involves directing positive thoughts, music and scriptural recitation toward the developing baby, is followed by many families across different religious traditions. The underlying belief is that positive stimuli during pregnancy shape the child's character and wellbeing.

From a medical perspective, practices that reduce maternal stress and promote a sense of calm and purpose are generally positive for pregnancy. Chronic high stress is associated with poorer outcomes, so anything that meaningfully reduces anxiety, including prayer, meditation or listening to music you find comforting, is likely to be beneficial in that indirect sense. The claim that specific content can be transmitted to the fetus through these practices is not supported by clinical evidence, but the wellbeing benefit for the mother is real.

Community participation in spiritual practices also provides connection and social support, which are independently associated with better wellbeing during pregnancy. The communal dimension of garbha sanskar, where family members gather and participate, has value beyond any individual practice.

Godh bharai: the seventh-month ceremony

The godh bharai is a widely observed ceremony typically held during the seventh month of pregnancy. The name translates roughly as "filling the lap" and the ceremony is a celebration of the pregnancy and the family's welcome of the arriving baby. Family and friends gather to bless the expectant mother, give gifts, share food and mark the transition into the later stage of pregnancy. In many communities it plays a similar social role to a baby shower, though its structure and rituals vary by region and family background.

The godh bharai is a moment of community gathering and emotional support at a stage of pregnancy when the demands of the coming weeks become more present. The reassurance and excitement that a well-supported ceremony provides can be genuinely meaningful for expectant parents. There are no medical concerns associated with attending or participating in such a ceremony.

Frequently asked questions

Is it true I should avoid papaya during pregnancy?

Raw or unripe papaya contains compounds that some studies suggest could affect uterine contractions in large amounts. Ripe papaya in normal dietary quantities is generally considered safe by most health authorities. If you are uncertain, mention it to your midwife.

Should I follow traditional Indian dietary restrictions during pregnancy?

A varied, balanced diet as recommended by the NHS is the evidence-based approach. Many traditional Indian foods are nutritionally excellent. Some traditional restrictions have no medical basis, while others reflect sensible hygiene principles. Discuss any specific restrictions with your midwife if uncertain.

What is garbha sanskar?

Garbha sanskar is the practice of positive input during pregnancy -- listening to devotional music, reading scriptures, meditation, positive thinking -- with the belief that it benefits the developing baby. Practices that reduce maternal stress and increase wellbeing are generally positive.

Is the belief about staying indoors during eclipses medically relevant?

No. Staying indoors during a solar or lunar eclipse has no medical basis in relation to pregnancy. If it is an important practice for your family, following it causes no harm.
