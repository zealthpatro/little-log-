Baby and toddler sleep environment: temperature, darkness and white noise

Sleep · Updated July 2026 · All articles

When parents ask what they can do to help their baby sleep better, the conversation often jumps straight to routines and sleep training. Those things matter, but the environment your baby sleeps in is a quieter, more foundational layer that deserves attention first. A room that is too warm, too bright, or too noisy in the wrong way can undermine even a well-timed bedtime. Getting the basics right will not guarantee long stretches, but it removes a source of friction that is entirely within your control.

Why sleep environment matters (though less than routine)

The sleep environment is best thought of as the stage on which everything else happens. It cannot substitute for a consistent routine, age-appropriate wake windows, or simply waiting for your baby's nervous system to mature. But it can make those things work better, and there are specific environmental factors that have real safety implications beyond just sleep quality.

Overheating is an independent risk factor for sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). Room temperature and appropriate clothing are therefore not just comfort questions. They are safety ones. Similarly, a room that is flooded with light at 5am in summer will reliably produce an early waker, regardless of how well everything else is going. These are problems worth solving because they are solvable.

The good news is that most of the changes involved are inexpensive. A room thermometer, a blackout blind, and a sleeping bag in the right TOG rating cover most of what you need. The rest is about removing things that do not belong in the sleep environment rather than adding new ones.

Room temperature: the 16 to 20 degree range

NHS guidance recommends keeping the room your baby sleeps in between 16 and 20 degrees Celsius. This is a wider range than many parents expect. The lower end, around 16 to 17 degrees, feels cool to an adult standing in the room, but a baby dressed appropriately and in a sleeping bag of the right warmth will be perfectly comfortable and at lower risk of overheating.

The best way to monitor room temperature is with a simple digital room thermometer placed near the cot. Avoid positioning it in direct sunlight, near a radiator, or close to a window, as all of these will give you a reading that does not reflect the temperature where your baby is actually sleeping. A thermometer that sits on a shelf at cot level, in the middle of the room, is more reliable.

In summer, keeping a room within this range can be genuinely difficult, especially in older homes. A fan circulating air in the room (not pointed directly at the baby) can help. Keeping blinds or curtains closed during the day to prevent the room from heat-soaking is more effective than trying to cool it down after bedtime. If the room is consistently above 24 degrees in warm weather, lighter bedding and fewer layers underneath the sleeping bag become more important.

Dressing your baby for sleep: TOG ratings explained

TOG stands for Thermal Overall Grade, and it is a measure of warmth applied to sleeping bags and duvets. The higher the TOG, the more insulating the item. For babies, sleeping bags are strongly preferred over loose blankets, which can cover a baby's face and are a risk factor for suffocation and overheating.

As a general guide: in a room at 16 to 20 degrees Celsius, a 2.5 TOG sleeping bag is right for most of the year. In a room at 20 to 24 degrees, a 1 TOG bag is more appropriate. Above 24 degrees, a 0.5 TOG bag or a light swaddle (for very young babies not yet rolling) is usually enough. These are starting points, not rules, and every baby runs slightly different in terms of how warm they feel.

The key thing many parents miss is that the TOG of the sleeping bag interacts with whatever your baby is wearing underneath. A 2.5 TOG bag over a thick sleepsuit and a vest is going to be significantly warmer than a 2.5 TOG bag over just a short-sleeved bodysuit. Most sleeping bag manufacturers publish combination guides showing what to dress your baby in underneath at different room temperatures. These are worth bookmarking during the summer months when room temperature varies more than in winter.

Check your baby's temperature by feeling the back of their neck or their chest. These are more reliable than hands or feet, which can feel cool even when a baby is perfectly warm. A comfortable sleeping temperature means the skin feels warm and dry, not hot, clammy, or sweaty.

How darkness helps your baby sleep

Melatonin, the hormone that signals to the body it is time to sleep, is suppressed by light. This is true for adults, and it is especially true for babies, whose circadian rhythms are still developing. A dark room is not just a nice-to-have for better sleep. It is directly supporting the hormonal conditions that make sleep possible.

Blackout blinds or blackout liners make the biggest practical difference in two situations: early mornings in spring and summer when sunrise is early, and at bedtime in summer when there is still daylight at 8 or 9pm. In both cases, without blackout covering, many babies will either resist going to sleep or wake far earlier than their body needs. A room that is near-total darkness at both of these times can shift things noticeably.

Portable blackout blinds that attach with suction cups are useful for travel or for temporarily blacking out a room that does not have a blind fitted. They are not as effective as a properly installed blackout roller or blackout lining behind an existing curtain, but they are a reasonable short-term fix. If you find your baby is waking consistently at dawn and your room has light coming in at the edges of blinds or around curtain rails, foam blackout tape along the edges is an inexpensive way to close the gaps.

A very faint nightlight for your own navigation when you need to go in during the night is fine. It will not disrupt your baby's melatonin production in any meaningful way. If you use one, keep it very dim and positioned away from the cot, not aimed at the baby's face.

White noise: the evidence, the benefits and the limits

White noise works because it masks sudden sounds that might startle a baby awake, and because constant ambient sound can help prevent the kind of noise differential (going from quiet to sudden sound) that interrupts light sleep. In the womb, babies were surrounded by constant noise at around 80 to 90 decibels, a continuous rumble of blood flow, digestion, and movement. Postnatal silence is, in that sense, a new experience for a newborn.

Research supports white noise as a tool to help babies settle, particularly in the early months. Studies have found it can reduce crying and shorten settling time. However, the same research has also flagged a concern: some commercially sold white noise machines, when placed close to a cot, can produce sound levels above 85 decibels. This is above the safe limit for sustained exposure in infants and raises concerns about potential effects on hearing development.

The practical guidance is straightforward. Keep any white noise source at least 2 metres from the cot. Keep the volume at around 50 decibels, which is roughly the level of a quiet shower or a quiet conversation in the next room. A free decibel meter app on your phone is accurate enough to check this. If you cannot hear the machine from the doorway, it is probably at about the right level. White noise is a useful tool for many families, particularly those in busy environments or with older siblings, but it is not essential and some babies sleep just as well without it.

Humidity and air quality

Room humidity is less often discussed than temperature or light, but it is worth a mention. Very dry air (common in centrally heated homes in winter) can dry out a baby's nasal passages and contribute to congestion, which makes breathing and therefore sleeping harder. The ideal range for indoor humidity is generally considered to be 40 to 60 percent. A basic hygrometer (often combined with a room thermometer) can tell you where your room sits.

If your home is consistently very dry in winter, a cool-mist humidifier can help. Choose a model without a light or sounds function to keep the environment as dark and quiet as possible. Clean it regularly according to the manufacturer's instructions. A dirty humidifier can harbour mould and bacteria, which negates any benefit.

Good ventilation matters too. A slightly cracked window in mild weather, or a well-ventilated room, helps maintain air quality without creating a draught. A draught directed at a sleeping baby is uncomfortable and can cause temperature fluctuations, so keep windows on a side of the room away from the cot if you have that option.

Checking your bassinet or cot is safe

The sleep environment includes the sleep surface. Safe sleep guidelines recommend a firm, flat, clean mattress in a cot or bassinet that meets current safety standards. The mattress should fit snugly with no gaps around the sides. Gaps between a mattress and the cot frame are a potential entrapment hazard.

The sleep surface itself should be clear. No pillows, positioners, bumpers, soft toys, or loose bedding in the cot. These items, regardless of how they are marketed, increase the risk of suffocation and overheating and are not recommended. A fitted sheet and a sleeping bag are everything your baby needs on the sleep surface itself.

If you are using a bassinet or bedside crib in the early months, check that it meets current safety standards for the country you are in. Second-hand bassinets can be fine, but it is worth checking the mattress specifically. Old mattresses can be compressed in ways that make them less firm and less flat than the original product.

Fragrance, screens and other things to remove

Strong scents in the sleep environment are worth avoiding. Plug-in air fresheners, scented candles, essential oil diffusers, and fabric softeners with heavy fragrance can all irritate a baby's sensitive airways and cause sneezing or congestion that disrupts sleep. If you are using any of these in the nursery, removing them is a quick and costless change.

Screens deserve a mention, not because of their light emission (which is real but secondary), but because they represent a source of stimulation and noise in the sleep environment. A screen playing shows or music in the background may seem like it helps, but the unpredictable changes in volume and light that come with video content are likely to cause more disruptions than they prevent.

Finally, think about what else might be in the room that creates noise or light you have not noticed because you are used to it. Standby lights on electronics, sounds from outside the window that only start at certain hours, or the hum of heating or cooling equipment cycling on and off. None of these are necessarily problematic, but a quiet audit of the room can sometimes surface a simple fix.

Sleep monitors: what to look for and what you need

Sleep monitors range from simple audio monitors to video monitors with room temperature readouts to wearable devices that track heart rate and movement. All of these can provide reassurance, and for many parents they reduce overnight anxiety. They are not, however, a substitute for a safe sleep environment, and no monitor replaces following safe sleep guidelines.

For most families, a video monitor with a room temperature display is genuinely useful. Being able to check on your baby without opening the door, and being able to see the room temperature at a glance, are both practically helpful. More sophisticated wearable monitors can be reassuring for families with specific health concerns, but they carry a risk of increasing anxiety rather than reducing it if every normal variation in movement or breathing becomes a source of worry. If in doubt about whether a monitor is helping or adding stress, it is worth asking yourself honestly which direction it is pulling you.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature should a baby's room be for sleep?

NHS guidance recommends keeping the room your baby sleeps in between 16 and 20 degrees Celsius. This range reduces the risk of overheating, which is an independent risk factor for SIDS. The easiest way to monitor this is with a simple room thermometer placed near the cot, away from direct sunlight or heat sources.

Do I need a completely dark room for my baby to sleep?

Near-total darkness is genuinely helpful for sleep. The absence of light triggers the release of melatonin, the hormone that cues the body to sleep. Blackout blinds or blackout liners make a particularly big difference in summer when evenings stay light, and for early morning wake-ups caused by dawn light. That said, a very faint nightlight for your own navigation is fine and will not disrupt sleep in most babies.

Is white noise safe for babies?

White noise can help some babies settle and stay asleep, but volume matters. Research has shown that some commercially available white noise machines can exceed 85 decibels at close range, which is above safe limits for infants. Keep any white noise source at least 2 metres from the cot and cap the volume at around 50 decibels (roughly the level of a quiet shower). It is a useful tool for some families but not essential for all.

What TOG rating does my baby need?

TOG rating measures warmth. In a room at 16 to 20 degrees Celsius, a 2.5 TOG sleeping bag is suitable for most of the year. At 20 to 24 degrees, a 1 TOG bag is more appropriate. Above 24 degrees, a 0.5 TOG or a lightweight swaddle is usually enough. Always combine the TOG of the sleeping bag with the vest or sleepsuit underneath when assessing whether your baby will be comfortable.

Does white noise need to stay on all night?

It does not have to. Some parents use white noise only during the settling period and let it switch off once the baby is asleep. Others run it all night as a way of masking household sounds that might cause waking. Both approaches work. If you run it all night, the 50 decibel volume limit and 2-metre distance from the cot still apply.

Can I use essential oils or air fresheners in my baby's room?

It is best to avoid fragrance in the sleep environment altogether. Strong scents can irritate a baby's sensitive airways and may trigger sneezing or congestion that disrupts sleep. Plug-in air fresheners, scented candles, essential oil diffusers, and heavily fragranced fabric softeners are all worth avoiding in the room where your baby sleeps.
