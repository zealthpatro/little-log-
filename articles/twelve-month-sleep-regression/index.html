12-month sleep regression: why it happens and what helps

10-14 months · Sleep · Updated July 2026 · All articles

You have made it through nearly a year. Your baby was sleeping in longer stretches and life was starting to feel manageable again. Then, just as their first birthday approached, the nights got hard again. More waking, more crying out, harder to settle, possibly fighting naps they used to go down for easily. If this sounds familiar, you are not imagining it. The 12-month sleep regression is a real and well-recognised phase, and it tends to arrive right in the middle of one of the biggest developmental bursts your baby will go through in their first two years.

Why sleep gets harder around 12 months

The disruption around the first birthday is rarely caused by just one thing. Several big changes tend to happen at roughly the same time, and together they can turn a reasonably settled sleeper into one who is waking repeatedly and taking much longer to go back down.

Walking is starting. Many babies take their first steps between 9 and 14 months, and the drive to practise this new skill does not always switch off at bedtime. Babies who are on the verge of walking often spend time in their cot pulling to stand, walking along the cot rail, or simply being too wired to settle. New physical skills are exciting, and the brain does not easily leave them alone.

Language is coming on fast. The months around the first birthday often bring a burst of understanding and early words. Your baby is processing an enormous amount of information about how the world works and what words mean. This kind of cognitive activity can make sleep lighter and settling harder, even if your baby cannot yet tell you why they are awake.

Separation anxiety is at or near its peak. This is perhaps the biggest driver of the night waking that parents find hardest. Between 10 and 18 months, babies develop what is called object permanence: the understanding that people and things continue to exist even when they cannot be seen. This is a wonderful cognitive milestone, but it comes with a downside. When your baby wakes in the dark at midnight and you are not there, they now understand that you exist somewhere, and they want you. This is not manipulation. It is a completely understandable emotional response to a new and important understanding of the world.

The nap transition that often comes at the same time

One of the most practically significant things happening around 12 months is a change in how many naps your baby needs. Most babies move from two naps to one nap somewhere between 12 and 18 months, and many start showing signs of readiness right around the first birthday.

When a baby still on two naps starts needing less daytime sleep, the morning nap can begin to interfere with night sleep by pushing bedtime too late, or the afternoon nap can become a struggle because the morning nap took the edge off the tiredness. The result is a kind of daytime sleep chaos that makes nights harder too.

Signs your baby may be ready to move to one nap include:

  • Consistently refusing or taking a long time to fall asleep for the morning nap
  • Napping well in the morning but then refusing the afternoon nap
  • Taking both naps fine but then taking ages to settle at bedtime
  • Waking earlier in the morning than usual

The nap transition and the regression can happen at the same time, which is why this phase can feel particularly chaotic. The good news is that once your baby is settled into one nap, daytime sleep often becomes much more predictable again.

How long the regression typically lasts

Most families find the 12-month regression lasts somewhere between two and six weeks. It can feel longer, especially because tired parents lose their sense of time fairly quickly, but for the majority of babies this is a phase rather than a new normal.

The key factor in how long it lasts is usually consistency. Babies whose routines stay broadly the same during the regression tend to find their way back to better sleep more quickly. When parents understandably start making big changes at night (bringing their baby into the parental bed, feeding more often than before, or sitting in the room until their baby is asleep), this can sometimes extend the difficult period by giving the baby new associations around falling asleep.

That is not to say you should never respond to your baby at night. Reassurance is important, especially during a phase when separation anxiety is running high. The aim is to offer comfort without creating habits that are hard to undo.

Wake windows and timing at this age

Getting the timing of sleep right becomes more important around 12 months. A baby who is overtired at bedtime often has a harder night, because overtiredness can cause more frequent waking, not less. Equally, a baby who has not been awake long enough before bed will not have built up enough sleep pressure to settle easily.

At 12 months, most babies do well with a wake window of around three to four hours before bedtime. If your baby is still on two naps, the second nap needs to end early enough to allow this window. If they are transitioning to one nap, that single nap will usually need to fall around midday so that there is enough awake time before a reasonably early bedtime, typically somewhere between 6:30 and 8pm.

Logging your baby's naps and wake times for a few days can make patterns visible that are hard to spot in the fog of tiredness. Seeing the timing on paper or on screen often makes it easier to identify where the schedule might need a small adjustment.

What actually helps at night

During the regression, it helps to think of your goal as offering reassurance without creating new sleep associations that will be hard to move away from later.

Keep your bedtime routine consistent. A calm, predictable sequence of events before bed, such as a bath, pyjamas, a feed or milk, a book, and a goodnight, gives your baby a reliable cue that sleep is coming. Consistency matters more than length. Even a short routine that happens the same way every night works well.

Settle your baby in the cot while they are drowsy but still awake. If your baby falls asleep on you or at the breast and then wakes to find themselves alone in the cot, the change in circumstances can cause distress. Putting them down drowsy rather than fully asleep gives them the chance to practise the skill of falling asleep in their own space.

When they wake at night, try a brief calm check-in first. Going in, offering a quiet word or a hand on the back, and leaving before your baby is fully settled can sometimes be enough to reassure them. This approach takes patience and consistency, and it does not always work quickly, but it avoids the pattern of needing long overnight intervention every time.

Do not rush to sleep train during the regression itself. If you have been thinking about making bigger changes to how your baby falls asleep, it usually makes sense to wait until the regression has settled before introducing them. Adding new sleep training on top of an already unsettled phase tends to be harder for everyone. Once things have steadied a little, you are in a much better position to make changes that stick.

Separation anxiety and the night waking cycle

Because separation anxiety is such a big part of what drives the 12-month regression, it is worth thinking about how you respond to night waking in a way that addresses the emotional need without extending the habit.

Your baby needs to know you are there. That is real and legitimate. The goal is not to ignore them. What tends to help is responding in a way that is calm, brief, and consistent rather than variable. If your baby knows that you will always come but that the visit will be short and quiet, they gradually learn that waking is not a route to prolonged company. That learning takes time, especially when anxiety is high, but it does happen.

During the day, you can also help with separation anxiety by practising small separations. Brief periods of playing in a different room, or leaving and coming back while they watch, can help build your baby's confidence that you always return. This daytime work can translate into slightly less anxious nights over time.

Looking after yourself through this phase

The 12-month regression can hit hard precisely because many parents thought the worst of the sleep disruption was behind them. Finding yourself back in the territory of very broken nights when your baby is nearly a year old is genuinely difficult, and it is worth being honest with yourself and the people around you about that.

If you have a partner, try to share the night wakings where you can. Even splitting the wakings unevenly, with one person taking the early hours and the other taking the stretch closer to morning, can mean each of you gets a longer unbroken stretch of sleep, which is more restorative than many short fragments.

If you are managing alone or if the disruption is affecting your own health and wellbeing significantly, speak to your health visitor. They are well placed to assess what is happening, rule out anything physical going on with your baby, and offer support for you as well as guidance on sleep.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 12-month sleep regression a real thing?

Yes. Many parents notice their baby's sleep becoming more disrupted around the first birthday. This is a recognised pattern linked to major developmental changes: walking is often beginning or just starting, language is coming on quickly, and separation anxiety tends to peak around this age. All of these things affect sleep, and the disruption is a normal part of how your baby grows.

How long does the 12-month sleep regression last?

For most families, the disruption lasts somewhere between two and six weeks. It can feel longer because the nights are hard, but it does pass. Keeping a consistent routine and avoiding big changes to how you respond to your baby at night tends to shorten the difficult period.

Why is my baby suddenly fighting naps around 12 months?

Around 12 months, many babies are ready to drop from two naps to one. The change in nap needs and the regression can happen at the same time, which can make daytime sleep feel chaotic. Signs your baby may be ready for one nap include consistently refusing the morning nap, taking a long time to fall asleep for naps, or waking very early in the morning.

Could separation anxiety be causing my baby to wake more at night?

Very likely, yes. Separation anxiety typically peaks between 10 and 18 months. Your baby now understands that you exist when you are not visible, so when they wake in the night they may call or cry because they want the reassurance of knowing you are there. Brief, calm check-ins without picking up or bringing them into your bed can help reassure them without reinforcing waking as a way to get company.

Should I start sleep training during the 12-month regression?

Most sleep experts suggest waiting until the regression has passed before introducing significant changes to how your baby falls asleep. Trying to sleep train during a period when your baby is already unsettled developmentally tends to be harder for everyone. Once things have steadied, usually after two to six weeks, it is a better time to make changes if you want to.

When should I speak to my health visitor about my baby's sleep?

If the disruption goes on significantly longer than six weeks, if your baby seems unwell or is not eating well, or if the sleep deprivation is really affecting your own wellbeing, do speak to your health visitor. They can help rule out anything else going on and offer support tailored to your situation.
