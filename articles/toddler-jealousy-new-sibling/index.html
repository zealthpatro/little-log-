Toddler jealousy and a new baby: regression, acting out and what helps

Behaviour · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Bringing a new baby home is one of the most significant events in a toddler's life. Everything familiar changes. The parent who was previously available is now unavailable for significant stretches. A new small person has appeared and seems to require constant attention. For a toddler, this is a great deal to absorb. The behaviour that follows, from clinginess to regression to the occasional push toward the Moses basket, is not misbehaviour. It is a completely understandable response to a major upheaval.

Why regression is normal and what it looks like

Regression is one of the most common responses to a new sibling. The toddler observes that the baby receives feeding on demand, constant holding, and maximum parental attention. At some level, the toddler's brain computes that returning to baby behaviour might restore access to that level of care.

Common regressions include toilet regressions (a previously dry child starting to have accidents again), requesting a bottle or breastfeed after months without one, returning to baby talk, increased clinginess, changes in sleep, and renewed separation anxiety. None of these are permanent. They are the toddler communicating that they need more reassurance than the current situation is providing.

The most helpful frame is to see regression as information rather than a problem. The child is telling you something. The response is more connection and reassurance, not more pressure to "be the big one."

What acting out looks like

Acting out and regression often arrive together. Acting out includes aggression toward the baby (hitting, pinching, or rough handling), extreme clinginess with one parent, deliberate ignoring of the other, increased tantrums that seem to have no obvious trigger, and intensified demands in all areas of daily life.

None of this is evidence of a disturbed child or a character flaw. It is the toddler's emotional system under pressure, and the behaviour is communication. The underlying message is almost always the same: I need to know I am still important to you.

Naming the feeling behind the behaviour is often more effective than addressing the behaviour itself. "It looks like you are having a really hard time since the baby came. That makes sense. You are still my important one."

How to prepare before the birth

Age-appropriate preparation before the baby arrives can ease the transition meaningfully. Books about new babies that are honest about what a newborn is actually like are more useful than books that make the arrival sound immediately joyful. A real description helps: the baby will sleep a lot, cry a lot, and will not be able to play for a long time.

Involving the toddler in preparation builds ownership rather than exclusion: helping to choose where the baby's things will go, being present for conversations about what is coming, picking something to give the baby. A special "big kid" gift that arrives on the day of the birth can make the occasion feel like something for the toddler too, not just something happening to them.

An honest, simple conversation about what the early weeks will actually look like sets more realistic expectations. "The baby will need a lot of my time at first. But you are my big one and nothing changes that."

The first weeks home: the most intense period

The adjustment typically peaks in the first four to six weeks after the baby comes home. This is when the physical reality of the change hits, when the parents are most exhausted, and when the toddler's behaviour is most likely to be at its most challenging. Knowing this in advance does not make the first weeks easier in the moment, but it does help to know that the intensity will ease.

The first weeks are hard for everyone. The parents are physically stretched and running on depleted reserves. The toddler is having the biggest adjustment of their short life. Expectations on all sides, including on yourself, need to be kept as low as possible. Survival mode is a legitimate phase.

Special one-on-one time as the most powerful tool

Research consistently shows that even short, completely undivided periods of one-on-one time with a toddler make a significant difference to their adjustment when a new sibling arrives. Ten to fifteen minutes of time that belongs entirely to the toddler, with the phone down, no baby to attend to, and the parent fully present, can shift the emotional temperature of a whole day.

This is not about quantity. It is about quality and reliability. The toddler needs to know the time is coming and that it belongs to them. It can be reading together, playing on the floor, a short walk, or simply sitting close. The activity matters far less than the quality of the attention. Even a small, predictable window of unshared time communicates: you are still seen. You are still important.

Involving the toddler in baby care

Giving the toddler a real role in the baby's care reduces jealousy and begins to build sibling connection. Being the one who fetches the nappy, who sings to help settle the baby, who helps at bath time, who announces when the baby is awake, transforms the toddler from an observer of a rival to a participant in a family.

Supervision is essential, particularly in the early weeks when the toddler's feelings are most volatile. But supervised participation, even in small contributions, builds a sense of ownership over the baby that jealousy cannot easily survive. The toddler who helped choose the baby's toy is less likely to resent the baby than the toddler who watched from the side.

Holding two needs at once without guilt

One of the harder truths of having two young children is that you cannot meet both children's needs in every moment simultaneously. Being honest with your toddler about this, warmly and without guilt, is kinder than pretending otherwise.

"I know you want me right now. The baby needs me first this minute. I will be with you very soon." This does several things at once: it names the toddler's need, it is honest about the immediate constraint, and it makes a specific promise. Toddlers handle honest acknowledgment better than they handle being ignored without explanation.

Guilt is a common companion of parents navigating this adjustment. Some of the toddler's feelings are legitimate responses to a genuine change in parental availability. Those feelings deserve acknowledgment, not minimisation. But acknowledging the reality of the change is not the same as overriding every baby need in compensation. You are doing enough. The adjustment takes time for everyone, including you.

How long the adjustment takes

Most toddlers adjust to a new sibling within three to six months. Some move through it faster, some more slowly. The first month or two is typically the most intense. Progress is rarely linear: there will be good days and difficult days throughout the adjustment period, and a difficult day after several good ones is not a sign that things are going backwards.

What helps throughout is not perfection but consistency: predictable one-on-one time, honest warm communication, low expectations on the hard days, and the steady message that the toddler is loved and important. The relationship between siblings that begins in jealousy and confusion very often develops, over time, into something neither of them could have imagined at the start.

Frequently asked questions

My toddler was gentle with the baby then suddenly became aggressive. What do I do?

Stay close and supervise carefully. Never leave a toddler who is struggling with jealousy alone with a newborn, even briefly. When aggression happens, name the feeling without labelling the child as bad: "You are having very big feelings about the baby right now." Remove the toddler gently and give them some calm one-on-one time if you can. The behaviour is communication, not character.

My toddler has started wetting the bed again after months of being dry. Is this the new baby?

Almost certainly, yes. Toilet regression under stress is extremely common and a well-recognised response to a significant change in family structure. Respond with calm and no shame, protect the mattress, and do not make it a topic of pressure or disappointment. It usually resolves as the toddler adjusts, typically within a few weeks to a couple of months.

Should I talk about the baby with my toddler before they are born?

Yes. Age-appropriate preparation helps, particularly when the conversation is honest. Use simple language: the baby will cry a lot and sleep a lot and will not be able to play for a long time. Avoid overpromising how wonderful it will all immediately be. A realistic picture prepares the toddler better than an idealised one, and it builds trust.

Is it okay for my toddler to feel jealous?

Yes, fully. Jealousy is a normal human emotion and a reasonable response to a significant change in family life. The goal is not to eliminate the feeling but to name it, accept it, and help the toddler express it in safe ways. Acknowledging the feeling directly, rather than trying to talk the child out of it, is far more effective and far kinder.

My toddler says they want to send the baby back. How do I respond?

With warmth and some lightness rather than alarm: "I hear that. Having a new baby around is a really big change." Avoid shaming the feeling. It is completely normal, it is not a sign of something wrong, and it will pass as the adjustment settles. Toddlers who say this are not predicting their future relationship with their sibling.

How do I make time for my toddler when I am feeding the baby every two hours?

Make feeding time a shared time: read to the toddler while feeding the baby, or prepare a special basket of activities only available during feeds. Use audiobooks or music together as a backdrop. Small moments of acknowledged connection throughout the day add up significantly. The quality of the contact matters more than the quantity of uninterrupted time, and brief moments of genuine presence are not nothing.
