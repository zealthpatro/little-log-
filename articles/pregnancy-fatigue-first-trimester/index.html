First trimester fatigue: why you are so tired in early pregnancy and how to cope

Pregnancy · Updated July 2026 · All articles

If you are in your first trimester and finding yourself asleep on the sofa at 7pm or struggling to get through a normal working day without feeling completely wrung out, you are not imagining it and you are not being soft. First trimester fatigue is genuinely one of the most intense experiences of pregnancy, and it happens when your body is doing some of its most significant work. Understanding why it happens can help, and there are practical steps that make a real difference.

Why first trimester fatigue is so intense

The exhaustion of early pregnancy has several converging causes, all of which hit at the same time during weeks four to twelve.

Progesterone: from the moment of implantation, progesterone levels rise steeply. Progesterone is essential for maintaining the pregnancy, but it also has a powerful sedative effect. It is not a coincidence that the hormone keeping your pregnancy safe is the same one making you feel as if you could sleep at any moment. This is the single biggest driver of first trimester fatigue and there is no way around it.

Blood volume expansion: pregnancy triggers an increase in blood volume that will ultimately reach 40 to 50 percent above your pre-pregnancy level by the third trimester. The early stages of this expansion begin in the first trimester, and the cardiovascular system working to support it takes a real metabolic toll. Your heart is working harder even when you are resting, which is one reason rest feels so necessary.

Building the placenta: the placenta is a new organ, and building it from scratch in the first twelve weeks requires an enormous amount of energy. By twelve weeks the placenta is fully formed and takes over the hormone production that was previously handled by the corpus luteum. This transition is part of why many people feel a meaningful improvement in energy as they move into the second trimester.

Nausea and poor sleep: if nausea is disrupting your ability to eat normally or to sleep through the night, the resulting undernourishment and broken sleep compound the fatigue significantly. This is a vicious cycle that is hard to break, and it is one of the reasons that severe pregnancy nausea (hyperemesis gravidarum) is taken seriously as a medical condition rather than just a minor inconvenience.

How long does first trimester fatigue last

For most people, the intensity of first trimester fatigue improves noticeably around weeks 12 to 16, as progesterone levels plateau, the placenta becomes fully functional, and hCG levels begin to fall. Many people describe weeks 14 to 20 as a welcome window of more normal energy, which is part of why the second trimester has a reputation as the easiest of the three.

That said, there is real variation. Some people feel significantly better from week 10. Others find the fatigue persists into the second trimester, particularly if they are carrying twins, have low iron, have significant ongoing nausea, or are managing pregnancy alongside demanding work or caring responsibilities. The timeline is not a rule, and if your fatigue is still overwhelming at 16 weeks, it is worth speaking to your midwife rather than simply waiting it out.

What actually helps

There are several things that make a genuine difference to first trimester fatigue, and a few that are often suggested but do not really help.

Rest when you can: this sounds obvious but is the single most effective thing. If you can sleep or lie down when the tiredness hits, do it. Treating first trimester rest as a legitimate need rather than laziness makes a real difference, both physically and in how you feel about yourself. Early pregnancy is not the time to prove how much you can push through.

Check your iron: if your fatigue feels extreme, heavy, or is accompanied by breathlessness on minimal exertion, heart palpitations or looking pale, ask your midwife to check your full blood count. Iron deficiency anaemia is common in pregnancy, worsens tiredness significantly, and is easily treated with iron supplements. Your booking appointment usually includes a blood test that checks this, but if you are between tests and concerned, it is worth asking.

Eat small, regular meals: blood sugar instability makes fatigue worse. Even if nausea is putting you off eating, trying to eat small amounts frequently, rather than large meals, helps maintain steadier blood sugar levels. Bland, carbohydrate-rich foods are often the easiest option in the first trimester, and eating something is always better than eating nothing.

Stay hydrated: dehydration is a surprisingly common contributor to fatigue and one of the easiest to address. Pregnancy increases your fluid needs, and if nausea is making you reluctant to drink, sipping cold water through a straw, drinking diluted squash or trying ice lollies can help you get fluids in.

Light exercise: on days when the fatigue is not completely flooring you, a gentle walk or a swim can actually improve energy by supporting circulation and releasing endorphins. Light exercise in early pregnancy is safe and beneficial. The emphasis is on gentle and regular rather than intense.

What does not help

Caffeine is worth addressing specifically. While one cup of coffee a day is generally considered acceptable in pregnancy (the NHS recommends keeping total caffeine under 200mg per day), leaning on caffeine to push through pregnancy fatigue is not a useful strategy. Caffeine does not address the underlying causes and can disrupt sleep, making nighttime rest less restorative. If you are using caffeine to function at a level that your body is genuinely asking you to step back from, it is worth reconsidering the caffeine rather than the rest.

Similarly, pushing through the fatigue as if you are not pregnant and expect the same output as before is not a helpful approach. The body is doing significant work in the first trimester. Ignoring what it is telling you tends to result in crash exhaustion rather than anything productive.

Managing fatigue at work

Navigating first trimester fatigue at work is one of the most common practical challenges of early pregnancy, particularly because most people have not yet told their employer they are pregnant. If you work in an office environment, scheduling the most demanding work for your higher-energy periods (often mid-morning), using your lunch break to rest somewhere quiet, and reducing optional social or evening commitments can all help manage the workload while protecting your energy.

If your job involves shift work, physical labour, long hours or commuting, the first trimester can be genuinely very hard. You do not have to disclose your pregnancy to your employer at this stage, but you are legally entitled to adjustments once you do tell them. If the demands of your work are making you significantly unwell, speaking to your GP about a sick note or a referral to occupational health is a legitimate option.

The tension around telling people early

One of the complications of first trimester fatigue is that it often arrives before most people are ready to tell others about their pregnancy. The traditional twelve-week rule means that many people are trying to function normally while quietly struggling through debilitating exhaustion, which is one of the harder aspects of early pregnancy.

There is no obligation to wait until twelve weeks to tell anyone. Telling a trusted person, whether a close friend, a partner, a sibling or a manager, can reduce the pressure of performing normality when you do not feel normal. You can ask for support and discretion without making a wider announcement. The twelve-week tradition exists because miscarriage risk falls significantly after this point, but it was never meant to require silence about your physical experience.

When fatigue might be more than just pregnancy

If your fatigue is extreme even compared to other pregnant people's experiences, is accompanied by low mood, or is not improving at all by the second trimester, it is worth speaking to your midwife or GP. Thyroid conditions (particularly underactive thyroid, hypothyroidism) can both cause and worsen pregnancy fatigue and are screened for in higher-risk pregnancies. Anaemia, as discussed, is another common and treatable cause. Antenatal depression can also manifest primarily as exhaustion and lack of motivation rather than overt sadness, and it responds well to support when identified.

Frequently asked questions

Why am I so exhausted in early pregnancy?

First trimester fatigue is driven by a surge in progesterone, a rapid expansion of blood volume, and the enormous energy demands of building the placenta. These processes are invisible but metabolically intensive, which is why you can feel completely drained without having done anything strenuous.

When does first trimester tiredness improve?

Most people notice a significant improvement in energy by weeks 13 to 16 as progesterone stabilises and the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum. For some this happens earlier; for others it takes a little longer. It is not universal, and some people feel tired throughout pregnancy.

Is it safe to nap during pregnancy?

Yes, napping during pregnancy is safe and often beneficial. Short naps of 20 to 30 minutes can help reduce the tiredness without disrupting night sleep too much. Long naps late in the day may make it harder to sleep at night, so earlier and shorter tends to work better.

Does iron deficiency make pregnancy tiredness worse?

Yes. Iron deficiency anaemia is common in pregnancy and significantly worsens fatigue. If your exhaustion feels particularly extreme or is accompanied by breathlessness or heart palpitations, mention it to your midwife. A simple blood test checks your iron levels and anaemia is straightforwardly treatable.

What foods help with pregnancy fatigue?

Eating small, regular meals helps maintain blood sugar and prevents the crashes that make tiredness worse. Iron-rich foods (meat, lentils, dark leafy vegetables) support red blood cell production. Staying hydrated is also important as even mild dehydration increases fatigue.

Can I exercise if I am exhausted in the first trimester?

Light exercise such as walking, swimming or gentle yoga can actually improve energy by supporting circulation and releasing endorphins. The key is gentle and regular, not intense. On days when the tiredness is overwhelming, rest is the right choice. Listen to your body rather than pushing through.
