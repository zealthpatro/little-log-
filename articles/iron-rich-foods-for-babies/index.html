Iron for babies: why it matters at 6 months and the best food sources

Feeding · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Iron is the nutrient most likely to be in short supply in a baby's diet during the first year, and the one with the most significant developmental consequences if it falls short. Yet it rarely gets the same airtime as other weaning concerns. This article explains why iron status becomes critical at exactly 6 months, what the best food sources are, how to make plant-based iron work harder, and what signs of deficiency look like.

Why 6 months is the critical window

Babies are born with a store of iron accumulated during pregnancy, primarily in the final trimester. Premature babies miss some of this accumulation, which is why they often need iron supplementation from birth. For full-term babies, stored iron is broadly sufficient for the first 6 months of life.

At around 6 months, those stores run low. Breast milk contains only small amounts of iron, and while what is present is highly bioavailable, the volume is not adequate to meet a growing baby's increasing iron needs beyond this point. Formula is fortified with iron, but similarly falls short as the baby's requirements increase.

Solid foods introduced at 6 months become the primary mechanism for meeting iron needs from this point forward. This is the reason health authorities across the NHS, CDC, WHO, and AAP are consistent in stating that iron-rich foods should feature from the very first days of weaning, not after a few weeks of sweet vegetable purees. The window matters.

Haem iron vs non-haem iron

Not all dietary iron is equal. There are two types, and they behave very differently in the body:

Haem iron comes from animal sources: red meat (beef, lamb, pork), poultry (particularly dark meat), fish and shellfish. The body absorbs haem iron at a rate of approximately 15 to 35 percent, and this absorption is relatively stable regardless of what else is eaten at the meal. Haem iron is the most efficient dietary route to meeting iron needs.

Non-haem iron comes from plant sources and from eggs and dairy products. It includes the iron in lentils, beans, tofu, fortified cereals, leafy vegetables, nuts, and seeds. The body absorbs non-haem iron at a much lower rate, around 2 to 20 percent, and this rate varies significantly depending on what else is consumed at the same meal. Non-haem iron can be made to work harder, but it requires some attention to food pairing.

The best iron-rich foods for babies

Animal sources (haem iron):

  • Beef, lamb, and pork: well-cooked and pureed or finely minced; slow-cooked cuts become very tender and easy to blend
  • Chicken and turkey: particularly the dark thigh meat, which contains more iron than white breast meat
  • Oily fish: salmon, sardines, mackerel, and trout are good iron sources as well as providing omega-3 fatty acids; flake finely and check carefully for bones
  • Shellfish such as clams and oysters are among the highest iron-density foods available, though less commonly offered to babies; check with your health visitor

Plant and other sources (non-haem iron):

  • Cooked lentils: red split lentils are easy to blend for younger babies and have a mild flavour; 100g cooked contains around 3mg of iron
  • Cooked chickpeas, kidney beans, black beans: all good sources; blend or mash well for babies under 9 months
  • Tofu: soft or silken varieties are easy to incorporate; calcium-set tofu is also a good calcium source
  • Iron-fortified baby cereals: oat or rice-based baby cereals fortified with iron are a convenient and reliable source, particularly useful as a first food
  • Eggs: the yolk contains iron in a form that is more bioavailable than most plant sources; hard-cook both white and yolk for babies under 1
  • Tahini (sesame paste): can be stirred into purees or spread thinly; good iron and calcium content
  • Dark leafy greens: cooked spinach, kale, and chard contain useful iron, though spinach also contains oxalic acid which limits absorption somewhat; pair with vitamin C
  • Ground pumpkin seeds: stir into porridge or puree; high iron per gram

How vitamin C doubles non-haem iron absorption

Consuming vitamin C alongside non-haem iron sources is one of the most effective dietary strategies available for improving iron status in babies who eat largely plant-based diets. Vitamin C converts non-haem iron from the ferric form, which the body absorbs poorly, into the ferrous form, which it absorbs well. Even a modest amount of vitamin C has a meaningful effect.

Practical vitamin C pairings at meals:

  • Lentil dal with a squeeze of fresh orange juice
  • Fortified oat porridge with soft kiwi or strawberry puree
  • Mashed egg with soft-cooked tomato
  • Bean puree with roasted soft red pepper
  • Spinach with soft-cooked tomato and a small amount of lemon juice

You do not need large amounts. A tablespoon of orange juice stirred into dal, or a few soft pieces of tomato alongside a legume-based puree, is sufficient to produce a meaningful enhancement in iron absorption.

Foods that reduce iron absorption

Some foods and drinks can reduce non-haem iron absorption when consumed at the same time. The most important ones for parents to be aware of:

  • Calcium-rich foods: calcium and non-haem iron compete for the same absorption pathway. Drinking large amounts of milk immediately before or alongside the iron-rich component of a meal can reduce iron uptake. The practical implication: serve dairy as part of a different course or at a different time from the main iron source, rather than, for example, offering a large cup of cows milk with a lentil-based meal.
  • Tannins from tea: tannins in tea are potent inhibitors of non-haem iron absorption. Babies should not be given tea at any age. This includes herbal teas unless specifically recommended by a health professional for a medical reason.
  • Phytates in wholegrains: phytates bind iron and reduce absorption. This is relevant primarily for babies eating large amounts of unprocessed wholegrains. Soaking legumes and grains before cooking reduces phytate content. The benefit of wholegrains in the diet as a whole means this should not lead to avoiding them, but it is a reason to ensure vitamin C is also present at grain-heavy meals.

Iron-fortified baby cereals

Iron-fortified infant cereals (baby rice, baby oatmeal) are a convenient and reliable iron source during the early weeks of weaning. Many brands provide a significant proportion of a baby's daily iron needs per serving. They are a particularly useful tool for families not yet serving meat or fish, or for vegetarian and vegan babies.

One caveat: commercial baby cereals vary widely in their ingredient quality. Choose options without added sugar and with a short ingredient list. Wholegrain versions provide more iron per serving than refined ones.

Signs of iron deficiency in babies

Iron deficiency develops gradually, and mild deficiency often has no obvious symptoms. Signs to watch for include:

  • Pallor: pale skin, pale inner eyelids (pull the lower eyelid gently down), pale gums and lips
  • Fatigue and reduced activity: a baby who seems less energetic than usual
  • Poor appetite
  • Slower weight gain
  • Increased susceptibility to infections
  • Irritability

Severe iron deficiency anaemia produces more pronounced symptoms including breathlessness, rapid heartbeat, and developmental delay. By the time these symptoms are visible, the deficiency has often been present for some time. This is why prevention through diet, rather than waiting for symptoms, is the recommended approach.

When to ask for a blood test

If you have any concerns about your baby's iron intake or status, speak to your GP. A simple blood test (a finger prick or heel prick in infants) can measure haemoglobin and ferritin (stored iron) levels. Blood tests for iron status are routinely offered to some groups considered at higher risk, including premature babies, babies from families with restricted diets, and babies with signs of deficiency.

Do not give iron supplements without medical guidance. Iron is a nutrient where the therapeutic dose and the toxic dose are not far apart, particularly in infants. Supplements should be prescribed or recommended by a health professional who has assessed your baby's iron status.

Frequently asked questions

Why does iron matter so much at 6 months?

Babies are born with iron stores that last approximately 6 months. Breast milk contains very little iron, and formula, while fortified, provides less than a growing baby needs once those stores begin to run low. At 6 months, solid foods become the primary route for meeting iron needs. This is why health authorities recommend including iron-rich foods from the very first meals of weaning, rather than starting with sweet vegetables and fruit.

What is the difference between haem and non-haem iron?

Haem iron comes from animal sources: red meat, poultry, fish, and shellfish. It is absorbed efficiently by the body, with absorption rates of around 15 to 35 percent. Non-haem iron comes from plant sources: lentils, beans, tofu, fortified cereals, leafy greens, nuts, and seeds. It is absorbed less efficiently, at around 2 to 20 percent depending on what else is eaten at the same meal. Combining non-haem iron sources with vitamin C at the same meal significantly boosts absorption.

How does vitamin C improve iron absorption?

Vitamin C converts non-haem iron from the form the body cannot use well into a form that it can, improving absorption by up to two to three times. Practical examples: lentil dal with a squeeze of orange juice, mashed egg with soft-cooked tomato, fortified porridge with kiwi or strawberry puree. Even a small amount of vitamin C alongside an iron-rich food makes a meaningful difference for babies on plant-rich diets.

What foods reduce iron absorption?

Calcium competes with non-haem iron for absorption when both are eaten together. Large amounts of dairy at the same meal as the main iron-rich food can reduce how much iron is absorbed. Tannins in tea also inhibit iron absorption significantly, which is why babies should not be given tea. Phytates in wholegrains reduce iron availability, though soaking beans and lentils before cooking helps reduce phytate content.

What are the signs of iron deficiency in a baby?

Iron deficiency can develop gradually and signs are often subtle. They include pallor of the skin, inner eyelids, and lips; tiredness and reduced energy; poor appetite; slow weight gain; and increased susceptibility to infections. More severe deficiency causes anaemia, with symptoms including breathlessness, rapid heartbeat, and developmental delay. If you notice any of these signs or are concerned about your baby's iron intake, speak to your GP, who can arrange a simple blood test.

Should I give my baby an iron supplement?

Most babies do not need an iron supplement if they are eating iron-rich foods regularly from weaning. Premature babies may be advised to take iron supplements from birth, as they have not had as long to build iron stores. If blood tests show iron deficiency or anaemia, your GP will prescribe a supplement. Do not give iron supplements without medical guidance, as too much iron is also harmful.
