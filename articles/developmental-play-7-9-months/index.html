Developmental play for babies aged seven to nine months: activities that actually help

7 to 9 months · Development · Updated July 2026 · All articles

Between seven and nine months, your baby shifts from being a largely passive observer to someone who is actively experimenting with the world. They are learning that their actions have consequences, that things exist even when they cannot see them, and that the noises they make actually get responses. Play is not a nice extra at this age: it is the main vehicle through which all of this learning happens.

The good news is that the most developmentally useful play does not require any special equipment, structured sessions, or screen-based apps. Your baby learns best through everyday interaction with you and with safe, varied objects. What follows is a practical guide to what your baby is working on and which activities support each area.

What babies are learning at this age

At seven to nine months, several significant cognitive and physical shifts are underway at the same time. Understanding what your baby is working on makes it easier to choose activities that match where they actually are.

Cause and effect is one of the biggest discoveries of this period. When your baby drops something from a high chair, bats a toy, or bangs two objects together, they are not being naughty or random: they are running a small experiment. "I do this, that happens." Each repetition reinforces an early logical understanding of the world. The fact that they want to do the same thing ten times in a row is a feature, not a bug.

Object permanence is beginning to emerge. Before around six months, when something disappeared from view it essentially stopped existing for a baby. Now, your baby is starting to understand that things continue to exist even when they cannot see them. You may notice them looking for a toy that has rolled behind you, or following your gaze to see what you are looking at. Peek-a-boo directly plays with this understanding and that is why babies this age find it endlessly satisfying.

Physically, babies are becoming much more deliberate in how they use their hands. They are refining their grasp, learning to transfer objects from hand to hand, and practising intentional reach. Babbling is becoming richer too, with strings of consonant sounds like "ba ba ba" and "da da da," and they are beginning to recognise their own name and a handful of common words from the daily routine.

Cause-and-effect toys and games

Any activity that reliably produces the same result when your baby does the same thing is a cause-and-effect game. The loop of "I do this, that happens" is what makes these activities so compelling and so developmental.

One of the simplest and most effective versions is the high chair drop. Put a few soft objects on the tray and let your baby push them off. Pick them up, put them back, repeat. Your baby is not testing your patience: they are testing a theory about gravity and about your reliable response. The consistency of your "that happened again" reaction is part of what makes this so rich.

Other good cause-and-effect activities include banging on pots and pans with a wooden spoon (the louder the result, the better), stacking rings that topple when pushed, simple pop-up toys where pressing a button reveals a character, and balling up and releasing a piece of scrunchy paper. You do not need to buy anything purpose-made: most kitchens already contain the best cause-and-effect toys available.

Treasure basket play

Treasure basket play is one of the most enduring and well-supported ideas in early childhood development. The concept is simple: a low, stable container filled with everyday objects of varied textures, weights, and shapes, offered to a baby who is old enough to sit (or be propped) and reach in independently.

The basket works because it gives your baby genuine choice and genuine novelty. Unlike a prescribed toy, the basket invites exploration without a correct way to play. Babies at this age use mouthing as a primary sense, so everything in the basket must pass the mouthing test: nothing smaller than a toilet roll tube, nothing with sharp edges, no strings, nothing that could break into small pieces.

Good items to include are a wooden spoon, a small smooth cardboard box, a silicone cup, a piece of folded fabric in an interesting texture, a metal tablespoon, and a small hairbrush with soft bristles. Vary the contents occasionally to maintain interest. Your role is to be nearby and present, not to direct the play: watching your baby make choices, discard objects, and return to favourites is itself a rich form of shared attention.

Language games

The most powerful thing you can do for your baby's language development at this age costs nothing and requires no preparation: narrate your day. Running commentary on what you are doing ("Now we're putting on your socks. One sock. Two socks.") gives your baby a constant stream of language tied to real-world context. This is how vocabulary is built, long before a baby can produce a single word.

Songs are especially effective because the rhythm and repetition make language patterns easier to notice and remember. Songs with a gap at the end of a phrase, where you pause and look at your baby expectantly, give them space to "fill in" a sound. Over time this becomes an early form of back-and-forth. Action songs with gestures (like clapping or waving) connect sound to movement and are particularly engaging at this age.

Calling your baby's name from different places in the room is a good way to build sound localisation, which is part of both language development and spatial awareness. Keep your voice warm and interested, and follow their gaze when they look toward something: naming what they are looking at connects words to objects in a way that supports vocabulary growth.

Movement play

The physical foundations for crawling are being laid throughout these months, and movement play supports the core strength, balance, and coordination that crawling and eventually walking will require.

Rolling a soft ball back and forth is a classic for good reason: it invites your baby to reach, shift their weight, and track a moving object, all at once. Tummy time remains valuable even as your baby becomes more mobile, particularly if you place interesting objects just within reach so they have a reason to push forward and stretch. Supported sitting on your lap or on the floor, reaching in different directions for toys, builds both core stability and the coordination needed to shift weight safely.

Being carried in different positions (upright, facing out, facing in) and moving through different environments is also movement play, even though it does not look like it. The vestibular input from being carried and the visual variety of moving through the world are genuinely enriching at this age.

Social games

Social play at seven to nine months is not just fun: it is laying the structural foundations of communication. The turn-taking pattern of a good social game is a direct precursor to conversation.

Peek-a-boo is the gold standard social game at this age for good reason: it plays directly with object permanence, it has a predictable structure, and it produces a joyful reaction. The anticipation before the reveal is part of what makes it work. Mirror play is equally captivating: babies at seven to nine months are often fascinated by their own reflection, and looking in a mirror together, making faces and naming them, combines social engagement with early self-awareness.

Simple sound turn-taking, where you make a sound, pause, wait for your baby to respond, and then reply to their sound, is a form of proto-conversation that directly builds the communication structures they will use for the rest of their lives. Keep the pauses genuine: give your baby enough time to respond before jumping back in. This "serve and return" pattern, as it is often called by researchers, is one of the most powerful things a caregiver can do to support early development.

What play does not need to look like

One of the most reassuring things to know at this stage is that "enriching" your baby's development does not require purchasing toys, attending classes, or scheduling screen time. The evidence consistently points in the opposite direction.

Babies at this age learn as much from watching you cook, being carried while you move around the house, exploring a patch of grass in the garden, or sitting in a bathroom watching water pour from a tap as they do from any purpose-built toy. The variety of textures, sounds, smells, and visual experiences that everyday life provides is extraordinarily rich. Bath time, with its warm water, splashing, and one-to-one attention, is one of the most developmentally packed activities of the day.

You do not need to fill every moment with structured play. Boredom, in small doses, is actually useful: it prompts babies to attend to their environment more carefully and find their own interests. The most important ingredient in any play is your warm, responsive presence. That presence, and the secure attachment it builds, is the foundation that makes all other development possible.

Frequently asked questions

What should a seven-month-old baby be doing during play?

At seven months, most babies are actively reaching for and grasping objects, transferring items from hand to hand, and beginning to show interest in cause and effect. During play they will often drop objects deliberately to watch them fall, mouth everything they pick up, and respond to your face and voice with smiles and babbling. There is a wide range of what is typical at this age, so follow your baby's lead rather than comparing to a checklist.

What are the best toys for babies aged seven to nine months?

The most developmentally useful toys at this age are simple ones: stacking rings, soft balls, containers with lids, small board books, and everyday household objects that are safe to mouth, like a silicone cup or a wooden spoon. Babies this age do not need electronic toys or screen-based products. Your face, your voice, and your presence are far more stimulating than any purchased toy.

What is a treasure basket and how do I set one up?

A treasure basket is a low container filled with everyday objects of varied textures, weights, and shapes. Good items include a wooden spoon, a small cardboard box, a silicone cup, a folded piece of fabric, a metal tablespoon, and a small hairbrush with soft bristles. Every item must pass the mouthing test: nothing smaller than a toilet roll tube, nothing with sharp edges, nothing that could break off in small pieces. Sit nearby while your baby explores and let them choose what to investigate.

How long should play sessions be at this age?

There is no set duration. Play sessions are naturally short at seven to nine months because babies have limited sustained attention. A few minutes of focused interaction is completely normal and healthy. The most important thing is that you follow your baby's cues: when they look away, fuss, or seem tired, the session is over. Several short sessions spread through the day are more effective than one longer one.

Is screen time okay for babies at seven to nine months?

The AAP advises avoiding screen-based media for babies under 18 to 24 months, with the exception of video calls. Screens do not provide the back-and-forth interaction that develops language and attachment, and the sensory input they offer is narrow compared to handling real objects, being outside, or interacting with a caregiver. Everyday life is far richer for brain development than any screen.

How do I encourage my baby to babble more?

The single most effective thing you can do is respond to every sound your baby makes. When they babble, look at them, smile, and make a sound back. This back-and-forth, often called serve and return, tells your baby that communication works. Narrating what you are doing throughout the day also provides a steady stream of language input. Songs with gaps where your baby can fill in sounds are especially effective.

Keep track of your baby's development in one calm place

Log milestones, play observations, and everyday moments as they happen, so nothing gets lost and you always have something to look back on.
