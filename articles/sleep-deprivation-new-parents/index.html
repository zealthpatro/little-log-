Sleep deprivation as a new parent: effects and how to cope

Newborn · Parent wellbeing · Reviewed 12 June 2026 · All articles

Looking after a newborn is one of the most rewarding things you can do, and one of the most tiring. Babies wake every two to three hours day and night, and the broken sleep that follows affects almost every new parent. Understanding what is happening to your body and what you can do about it makes the hardest nights a little more manageable.

Why newborn nights are so disruptive

Newborns have very small stomachs and need feeding frequently, so waking through the night is not a problem to solve but a biological reality to work around. They also have no sense of day and night yet, which means their sleep is spread in short stretches across the full twenty-four hours. This is completely normal, and it means the night waking is not a sign that anything is wrong with your baby.

The good news is that the situation is temporary. The NHS notes that as babies get older, they sleep for longer periods. The intensity of those early weeks gradually eases as your baby grows and their stomach capacity increases, even if that reassurance can be hard to hold onto at three in the morning.

What tiredness does to you

Broken sleep has real, measurable effects on how you think and feel. Irritability, difficulty concentrating, and a low mood that sits with you through the day are all recognised consequences of persistent tiredness. You may find it harder to make decisions, to stay patient, or to feel pleasure in things you normally enjoy. These are predictable responses to a body and brain running below their required maintenance level, not a sign of failing as a parent.

Stress adds to the load. The NHS identifies stress as a significant drain on energy in its own right. When you are anxious about feeding, weight gain, or simply whether you are doing everything right, that mental weight compounds the physical exhaustion from broken nights. Even mild dehydration, which is easy to fall into when your attention is entirely on a baby, can deepen fatigue further.

Practical ways to get more rest

There is no single fix, but several strategies used together can make a genuine difference to how you feel day to day.

  • Rest when your baby rests. The NHS is direct on this: sleep when your baby sleeps and treat it as a higher priority than housework or other tasks. If you worry about missing your baby's cries, set a gentle alarm so you can drop off without anxiety.
  • Try an earlier bedtime. Going to bed much earlier than usual for about a week can help you bank more sleep during the longer stretches your baby tends to give at the start of the night.
  • Use relaxation if you cannot drop off. If your mind races when you lie down, the NHS suggests a warm bath before bed and allowing yourself at least five to ten minutes of deep relaxation, which can refresh you even without full sleep.
  • Share nighttime duties with your partner. A partner can take on nappy changes and the morning care routine to give you extra rest. If breastfeeding is established, you can express milk so your partner handles at least one night feed, giving you a longer uninterrupted stretch.
  • Accept help from family and friends. Ask people you trust to sit with the baby while you nap. If you are parenting without a partner, arranging for someone to stay with you for several days at a time can provide the sustained rest that brief visits cannot.

Exercise, food, and energy

It sounds counterintuitive when you are already exhausted, but regular physical activity genuinely helps. The NHS recommends exercise as one of the ways to reduce tiredness, and walking is highlighted as one of the easiest forms to build in. A daily walk with the pram gets you moving without requiring anything complicated, and the fresh air benefits both of you.

What you eat and drink matters too. Eating something small and steady every few hours keeps your energy more even than relying on large, infrequent meals. Staying hydrated is particularly important; mild dehydration can make you feel noticeably worse. Caffeine can help in the short term, but its effects can linger for several hours and disturb the sleep you do manage to get, so cutting off caffeine by early afternoon is worth trying.

When tiredness becomes something more

Ordinary tiredness and postnatal depression are not the same thing, but they can look similar and each can make the other worse. The NHS describes postnatal depression as a recognised condition that responds well to treatment, and tiredness and stress are among the factors that can contribute to it.

The signs to watch for include being unable to sleep even when your baby is sleeping, feeling persistently hopeless, losing enjoyment in things you usually like, or feeling that you simply cannot cope. If these feelings do not lift or get worse, that is a signal to reach out rather than push through. You do not need to reach a particular level of suffering before asking for help.

Support and when to seek help

Contact your GP or health visitor if tiredness is making daily life very difficult, or if you notice any signs of postnatal depression. Early support tends to make a meaningful difference, and your care team will not judge you for getting in touch.

Several helplines are also there if you need to talk before your next appointment. The Cry-sis helpline (0800 448 0737) supports parents struggling with a crying or sleepless baby and is open every day from 9am to 10pm. Family Lives (0808 800 2222) takes calls Monday to Friday 9am to 9pm and Saturday to Sunday 10am to 3pm. Home-Start is a volunteer-led scheme that can arrange a trained parent helper to visit you at home on a regular basis, which can be particularly valuable if you are parenting alone or without nearby family.

Frequently asked questions

How long does newborn sleep deprivation last?

The most intense period is in the early weeks and months. Newborns wake every two to three hours because their stomachs are small and they have not yet developed a sense of day and night. As your baby grows, they gradually begin sleeping for longer stretches and need fewer night feeds, so things do get easier in time, even if that can be hard to believe in the thick of it.

Is it really okay to sleep when the baby sleeps?

Yes. The NHS specifically advises resting when your baby sleeps and treating that rest as a higher priority than housework or other tasks. Even short naps during the day help reduce your overall sleep debt. If you worry about sleeping through your baby's cries, setting a gentle alarm can give you peace of mind while you rest.

How can a partner help with night feeds when I am breastfeeding?

Once breastfeeding is established, you can express milk so your partner can take over at least one night feed. This gives you a longer uninterrupted sleep. Your partner can also take on nappy changes and the morning routine to let you rest for an extra hour or two before the day begins.

What signs suggest tiredness has become something more serious?

If you cannot sleep even when your baby is sleeping, feel persistently hopeless, stop enjoying things you usually like, or feel you simply cannot cope, these may be signs of postnatal depression rather than ordinary tiredness. Contact your GP or health visitor promptly. You do not need to wait until your next routine appointment.

Who can I call if I am really struggling?

Your GP or health visitor is always a good first contact. The Cry-sis helpline (0800 448 0737) supports parents struggling with a crying or sleepless baby and is open every day from 9am to 10pm. Family Lives (0808 800 2222) takes calls Monday to Friday 9am to 9pm and Saturday to Sunday 10am to 3pm. Home-Start is a volunteer scheme that can arrange a trained parent helper to visit you at home regularly.
